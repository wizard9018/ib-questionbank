# AA1376

**Maximum mark: 17**

The plane $\Pi_1$ has a normal vector of $\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\3\end{pmatrix}$ and contains the point $(-3, -1, 2)$.

1.    Show that a Cartesian equation of plane $\Pi_1$ is  $x - 2y + 3z = 5$. [2 marks]

Consider the plane $\Pi_2$ with equation $-2x +y + 3z = 6$.

The planes $\Pi_1$ and $\Pi_2$ intersect at line $L_1$.

2.    Show that $L_1$ has the equation $3x+17=3y+16=9z$. [4 marks]

$L_2$ passes through point $A(2,3,-1)$ and is parallel to $L_1$.

3.    Verify that $A$ does not lie on either $\Pi_{1}$ or $\Pi_{2}$. [2 marks]

4.    Find a vector equation of line $L_2$. [3 marks]

5.    Find the shortest distance between $L_2$ and $\Pi_{1}$. [6 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1. To find a Cartesian form of a plane we can use the __equation of a plane (3.17)__

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \bm{r}\cdot \bm{n}=\bm{a}\cdot \bm{n}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    where $\bm{r}=\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}$ is the position vector of any point on the plane, $\bm{n}$ is the normal vector to the plane and $\bm{a}$ is the position vector of a known point on the plane.

    Hence, using what we were given in the question, we get

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}\cdot \begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\3\end{pmatrix}&=\begin{pmatrix}-3\\-1\\2\end{pmatrix}\cdot \begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\3\end{pmatrix}\\[20pt]

    x-2y+3z&=-3 \times 1+(-1)\times (-2)+2 \times 3\tag*{\textbf{M1A1}}\\[6pt]

    &x-2y+3z=5\hspace{0.5em}\text{... as required}\tag*{\textbf{AG}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

2. To find the line of intersection, we must solve the system of equations formed by the two planes.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \Pi_{1}&: &x - 2y + 3z &= 5 \hspace{2em} \textcolor{#b52209}{(1)}\\[6pt]

    \Pi_{2}&: &-2x +y + 3z &= 6 \hspace{2em} \textcolor{#b52209}{(2)}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Subtract $\textcolor{#b52209}{(2)}$ from $\textcolor{#b52209}{(1)}$ to eliminate $z$  which gives

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    (x - 2y + 3z) - (-2x + y + 3z) &= 5 - 6 \\[6pt]

    3x - 3y &= -1\hspace{2em} \textcolor{#b52209}{(3)}\tag*{\textbf{A1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Next, we can eliminate $x$ by combining the two equations in the way $2 \times \textcolor{#b52209}{(1)}+\textcolor{#b52209}{(2)}$,

    which gives

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    (2x-4y+6z)+(-2x+y+3z)&=10+6\\[6pt]

    -3y +9z &= 16 \hspace{2em} \textcolor{#b52209}{(4)}\tag*{\textbf{A1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Notice, both equations $\textcolor{#b52209}{\text{(3)}}$ and $\textcolor{#b52209}{\text{(4)}}$ we have $3y$. Hence, if we make $3y$ the subject of both equations, we can write an equation combining all $3$ variables.

    $\\[-2.5em]$

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \tag*{\textbf{M1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    3x+1 &= 3y \\[6pt]

    9z-16&=3y

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Combining the results we get

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    &\hspace{.5em}3x+1=3y=9z-16\tag*{\textbf{A1}}\\[6pt]

    &3x+17=3y+16=9z \hspace{0.5em}\text{... as required.} \tag*{\textbf{AG}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

3. Substitute the coordinates of $A(2,3,-1)$ into the equations of the planes.

    For $\Pi_{1}$,

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \text{L.H.S.} &= (2) - 2(3) + 3(-1) \\[6pt]

    &= 2 - 6 - 3 \\[6pt]

    \text{L.H.S.} &= -7\\[6pt]

    -7 &\neq 5 \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    For $\Pi_{2}$,

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \text{L.H.S.} &= -2(2) + (3) + 3(-1) \\[6pt]

    &= -4 + 3 - 3 \\[6pt]

    \text{L.H.S.}&= -4 \\[6pt]

    -4&\neq 6\tag*{\textbf{A1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Since the coordinates do not satisfy either equation, $A\text{ does not lie on either plane.}$

    $\\[-2.5em]$

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \tag*{\textbf{AG}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

4. A $\textbf{vector equation of a line (3.14) }$ is of the form $\bm{r}=\bm{a}+\lambda\bm{d}$,

    where $\bm{a}$ is the position vector of a known point on the line and $\bm{d}$ is the direction of the line.

    Using $A$ we have $\bm{a}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\\-1\end{pmatrix}$.

    As $L_2$ is parallel to $L_1$, then lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ have the same direction.

    Thus, we need the direction vector of $L_1$. 

        $\\[-2.3em]$

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    From part (b), the Cartesian form is

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    3x+17=3y+16=9z

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    To find the direction of the line we need to write the line in vector form. To do this we must introduce $\lambda$, which we can do in various ways.

    

    One way is to make the entire equation equal to $\lambda$. This gives

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    3x+17=3y+16=9z=\lambda

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    We can now define each component in terms of $\lambda$.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    x&=\frac{-17+\lambda}{3}&\hspace{1em}y&=\frac{-16+\lambda}{3}&\hspace{1em}z&=\frac{\lambda}{9}\\[6pt]

    &=\frac{-17}{3}+\textcolor{#b52209}{\frac{1}{3}}\lambda&&=\frac{-16}{3}+\textcolor{#b52209}{\frac{1}{3}}\lambda&&=\textcolor{#b52209}{\frac{1}{9}}\lambda

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    We can see, highlighted in $\textcolor{#b52209}{\text{red}}$, the direction vector is

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \bm{d}&=\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{1}{3}\\\tfrac{1}{3}\\\tfrac{1}{9}\end{pmatrix}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    As we need a direction only, to remove the fractions, we can multiply this vector by $9$, giving

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \bm{d}&=\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{1}{3}\\\tfrac{1}{3}\\\tfrac{1}{9}\end{pmatrix} \times 9\\[12pt]

    &=\begin{pmatrix}3\\3\\1\end{pmatrix}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Using the results we have found we get

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \textbf{r} &= \mathbf{a} + \lambda \mathbf{d} \\[6pt]

    \textbf{r}&= \begin{pmatrix}2\\3\\-1\end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix}3\\3\\1\end{pmatrix} \tag*{\textbf{A1A1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Note, your direction vector can be any multiple of $\begin{pmatrix}3\\3\\1\end{pmatrix}$.

5. From part (b) we know that $A$, which lies on $L_2$, does not lie in either plane.

    We also know that line $L_2$ is parallel to both planes as it is parallel to the line of intersection of the two planes.

    Therefore the shortest distance between $\Pi_1$ and $L_2$ is the distance between $A$ and $\Pi_1$.

    $\\[-2.3em]$

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \tag*{\textbf{R1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    

    ![AA1376e](images/AA1376e.JPEG)

    The line $\textcolor{#b52209}{L_3}$ goes through $A$, is normal to $\Pi_1$ and intersects $\Pi_1$ at $B$.

    The distance between the two points $A$ and $B$, is the shortest distance between $L_2$ and $\Pi_1$.

    To find this we'll use the vector equation of $\textcolor{#b52209}{L_3}$, $\textbf{r} = \mathbf{a} + \mu \mathbf{d}$, where $\bm{a}$ is the position vector of $A$ and, the direction vector $\bm{d}$, is the normal to $\Pi_1$.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \textcolor{#b52209}{L_3}: \mathbf{r} &= \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}+\mu\begin{pmatrix} 1\\ -2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} \tag*{\textbf{(A1)}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Note, the normal to $\Pi_1$ can be found using the coefficients of $x$, $y$ and $z$ for the Cartesian equation of the plane for $\Pi_1$.

    Now we need to find where $\textcolor{#b52209}{L_3}$ intersects $\Pi_1$.

    Let's rewrite the equation of the line in component form.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{r} &= \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}+\mu\begin{pmatrix} 1\\ -2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}\\[20pt]

    \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix} &=\begin{pmatrix} 2+\mu\\ 3-2\mu \\ -1+3\mu \end{pmatrix}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    To find the value of $\mu$ when $L_3$ intersects $\Pi_1$ we substitute these components into the Cartesian equation of $\Pi_1$.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    x-2y+3z&=5\\[6pt]

    (2+\mu)-2(3-2\mu)+3(-1+3\mu)&=5 \tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Solving this on a G.D.C. we get

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \mu&=0.8571...\\[6pt]

    &=\frac{6}{7} \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Using this value of $\mu$ with the vector equation of line $L_3$ we can find the position vector of $B$.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \mathbf{r}_B &= \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}+\frac{6}{7}\begin{pmatrix} 1\\ -2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} \tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}\\[20pt]

    &=\begin{pmatrix} \tfrac{20}{7} \\  \tfrac{9}{7}\\ \tfrac{11}{7} \end{pmatrix}\\[20pt]

    &\hspace{-.8em}\left(=\begin{pmatrix} 2.857... \\  1.285...\\ 1.571... \end{pmatrix}\right)

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Therefore the coordinates of $B$ are $(\tfrac{20}{7},\tfrac{9}{7}, \tfrac{11}{7})$.

    Now we can find the __distance between two points__ $\bm{(3.1)}$ using the coordinates of $A$ and $B$, which is the shortest distance between $L_2$ and $\Pi_1$.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    d_{AB}&=\sqrt{\left(2-\tfrac{20}{7}\right)^2+\left(3-\tfrac{9}{7}\right)^2+\left(-1-\tfrac{11}{7}\right)^2}\\[6pt]

    &=3.207...\\[6pt]

    &=3.21\text{ units} \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$
