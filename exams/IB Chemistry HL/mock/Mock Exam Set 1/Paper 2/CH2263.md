        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        In the electrolysis of water using a Hofmann voltameter, twice as much hydrogen is produced at the cathode as oxygen at the anode, reflecting the $2:1$ ratio in the balanced equation

        $2 \ce{\,H2O}\textrm{(l)} \longrightarrow 2 \ce{\,H2}\textrm{(g)} + \ce{O2}\textrm{(g)}$

        In the diagram, the cathode is therefore the right$-$hand$-$side with the greater volume of gas, where water is reduced to hydrogen. The anode is the left$-$hand$-$side, where water is oxidized to oxygen, and electrons flow through the external circuit from the anode toward the cathode.

    2.  electrolyte / provides ions to conduct electricity / allows current to flow through the solution $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The role of sodium sulfate is to provide mobile ions that allow current to pass through the solution and thus complete the electrical circuit. The $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{SO_4^{2-}}$ ions carry charge between the electrodes, but they are not oxidized or reduced under the applied voltage. This ensures that water itself undergoes electrolysis while the sodium sulfate simply enables the circuit to function.

    3. cathode: colorless to pink, $\ce{H+}$ ions are reduced to $\ce{H2}$ so the solution becomes more basic $\checkmark$\
    anode: no change, $\ce{OH-}$ ions are oxidized to $\ce{O2}$ so the solution is acidic $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Phenolphthalein is an acid$-$base indicator that is colourless in acidic solution but turns pink in alkaline solution, with its transition around pH $8\text{--}10.$ During the electrolysis, water is reduced at the cathode:

        ***cathode:*** $\ \ \ 2 \ce{\,H2O}\textrm{(l)} + 2 \ce{\,e-} \longrightarrow \ce{H2}\textrm{(g)} + \textcolor{blue}{2\ce{\,OH-}\textrm{(aq)}}$

        This process increases the local concentration of hydroxide ions, raising the pH above the indicator's transition range and producing a pink colour near the cathode. At the anode, water is oxidized:

        ***anode:*** $\ \ \ 2 \ce{\,H2O}\textrm{(l)} \longrightarrow \ce{O2}\textrm{(g)} + \textcolor{red}{4 \ce{\,H+}\textrm{(aq)}}+ 4 \ce{\,e-}$

        The generation of hydrogen ions lowers the pH, so phenolphthalein remains colourless near the anode. The contrasting changes in $\ce{[OH-]}$ and $\ce{[H+]}$ around the electrodes therefore explain the observed colour pattern.

    4. $n_{\ce{H2}} = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{100 \times 10^3 \textrm{\ Pa} \times 22.5 \times 10^{-6} \textrm{\  m}^3}{8.31 \textrm{\ J K}^{-1}\textrm{mol}^{-1} \times 298 \textrm{\ K}}  = 9.09 \times 10^{-4} \textrm{\ mol\ } \ce{H2} \: \checkmark$

        $n_\ce{e-}  = 9.09 \times 10^{-4} \textrm{\ mol\ } \ce{H2} \times \dfrac{2 \textrm{\ mol\ } \ce{e-}}{1 \textrm{\ mol\ } \ce{H2}} = 1.82 \times 10^{-4} \textrm{\ mol\ } \ce{e-} \: \checkmark$

        $N_{\ce{e-}} =  1.82 \times 10^{-4} \textrm{\ mol\ } \ce{e-} \times \dfrac{6.02 \times 10^{23} \ce{\ e-}}{1 \textrm{\ mol\ }\ce{e-}}   = \ $:answer[$1.09 \times 10^{21} \textrm{\ electrons}$] $ \: \checkmark$

        *Award full marks for final answer.*

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        To calculate the number of electrons that pass through the circuit, we begin by determining the amount of hydrogen gas collected using the ideal gas equation, $PV=nRT.$ Rearranging to isolate the moles of hydrogen and substituting the values gives:

        $n_{\ce{H2}} = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{100 \times 10^3 \textrm{\ Pa} \times 22.5 \times 10^{-6} \textrm{\  m}^3}{8.31 \textrm{\ J K}^{-1}\textrm{mol}^{-1} \times 298 \textrm{\ K}}  = 9.09 \times 10^{-4} \textrm{\ mol\ } \ce{H2}$

        From the half$-$equation at the cathode,

        $2 \ce{\,H2O}\textrm{(l)} + 2 \ce{\,e-} \longrightarrow \ce{H2}\textrm{(g)} + 2 \ce{\,OH-}\textrm{(aq),}$

         it is clear that two moles of electrons are required for every mole of $\ce{H2}$ produced. Therefore, the number of moles of electrons is:

        $n_\ce{e-}  = 9.09 \times 10^{-4} \textrm{\ mol\ } \ce{H2} \times \dfrac{2 \textrm{\ mol\ } \ce{e-}}{1 \textrm{\ mol\ } \ce{H2}} = 1.82 \times 10^{-4} \textrm{\ mol\ } \ce{e-}$

        Finally, multiplying the moles of electrons by Avogadro's constant gives the total number of electrons that passed through the circuit:

        $N_{\ce{e-}} =  1.82 \times 10^{-4} \textrm{\ mol\ } \ce{e-} \times \dfrac{6.02 \times 10^{23} \ce{\ e-}}{1 \textrm{\ mol\ }\ce{e-}}   =  1.09 \times 10^{21} \textrm{\ electrons}$

        Thus, $1.09 \times 10^{21}$ electrons must have flowed to produce the observed volume of hydrogen gas, which ties the macroscopic gas measurement to the microscopic scale of charge transfer.

2. 1. products $-$ reactants $\checkmark$\
    $\Delta H^{\minuso} = \left[2(0) + (0)\right] - [2(-286)] = \ $:answer[$+572 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1}$] $\: \checkmark$\
    $\Delta S^{\minuso} = \left[2(130.7) + 205.1\right] - \left[2(70)\right] = \ $:answer[$+326.5 \textrm{\ J K}^{-1} \textrm{\,mol}^{-1}$] $\: \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        For enthalpy, apply the general relationship

        $\Delta H^{\minuso} = \sum \Delta H_f^{\minuso}\textrm{(products)} - \sum \Delta H_f^{\minuso}\textrm{(reactants)}$

        Because elements in their standard states have $\Delta H^{\minuso}_f = 0$ by definition,  the products the overall change in enthalpy is

        $\Delta H^{\minuso} = \left[2(0) + (0)\right] - [2(-286)] = +572 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1}$

        For entropy, use

        $\Delta S^{\minuso} = \sum S^{\minuso}\textrm{(products)} - \sum S^{\minuso}\textrm{(reactants)}$

        Using the values in the data booklet and the question statement:

        $\Delta S^{\minuso} = [2(130.7) + 205.1] - [2(70)] = +326.5 \textrm{\ J K}^{-1} \textrm{\,mol}^{-1}$

        A bookkeeping note: the “per mole” in these results is per mole of the reaction as written$-$here, per the stoichiometric event that consumes 2 mol of water and forms 2 mol of hydrogen and 1 mol of oxygen. If you write the reaction with different coefficients (for example, halving every coefficient), both $\Delta H^{\minuso}$ and $\Delta S^{\minuso}$ scale accordingly.
    2. $\Delta G = 572 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1} - 298 \textrm{\ K} \times \dfrac{326.5 \textrm{\ J K}^{-1}\textrm{mol}^{-1}}{1000 \textrm{\ J kJ}^{-1}} = \ $ :answer[$475 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1}$] $\: \checkmark$\
        $\Delta G > 0,$ non$-$spontaneous $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        To decide spontaneity under standard conditions, use the Gibbs equation with consistent units:

        $\Delta G^{\minuso} = ΔH^{\minuso} - TΔS^{\minuso}$

        Here, $\Delta H^{\minuso} = +572 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1}$ and $\Delta S^{\minuso} = +326.5 \textrm{\ J K}^{-1} \textrm{\,mol}^{-1},$ so first convert entropy to $\textrm{kJ K}^{-1} \textrm{\,mol}^{-1}$ (divide by 1000)

        $\Delta G = 572 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1} - 298 \textrm{\ K} \times \dfrac{326.5 \textrm{\ J K}^{-1}\textrm{mol}^{-1}}{1000 \textrm{\ J kJ}^{-1}} = 475 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1}$

        Because $\Delta G^{\minuso}$ is positive, the decomposition of liquid water to hydrogen and oxygen is non$-$spontaneous at 298 K; the reaction requires an input of energy (*e.g.*, an external power source) to proceed.

    3. $n=4 \: \checkmark$\
    $E^{\minuso} = -\dfrac{\Delta G^{\minuso}}{nF} = - \dfrac{475 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1} \times 1000 \textrm{\ J kJ}^{-1}}{4 \times 9.65 \times 10^4 \textrm{\ C mol}^{-1}} = \ $:answer[$-1.23 \textrm{\ V}$] $\checkmark$\
    $ \geq 1.23 \textrm{\ V}$ required $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Relate Gibbs free energy to cell potential using

        $\Delta G^{\minuso} = - nFE^{\minuso}$

        From the previous part, $\Delta G^{\minuso}$ for $2 \ce{\,H2O}\textrm{(l)} \longrightarrow 2 \ce{\,H2}\textrm{(g)} + \ce{O2}\textrm{(g)}$ at 298 K is $+475 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1}.$ The overall reaction transfers $n=4$ moles of electrons per mole of reaction (the anode step produces $4 \ce{\,e-}),$ and Faraday's constant is $F= 9.65 \times 10^4 \textrm{\ C mol}^{-1}.$

        $E^{\minuso} = -\dfrac{\Delta G}{nF} = - \dfrac{475 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1} \times 1000 \textrm{\ J kJ}^{-1}}{4 \times 9.65 \times 10^4 \textrm{\ C mol}^{-1}} = -1.23 \textrm{\ V}$

        The negative sign confirms the decomposition of water is non$-$spontaneous under standard conditions; therefore, an external power supply must provide at least $+1.23 \textrm{\ V}$ across the cell to drive the electrolysis (greater in practice due to overpotentials and internal resistance).

            $\Delta H = \dfrac{90 \textrm{\ kJ}}{2 \textrm{\ mol\ } \ce{H2O}} = 45 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1} $

            Each water molecule forms four hydrogen bonds but shares each bond with a neighbouring molecule, so it effectively owns half of each, or two hydrogen bonds per molecule. The strength of one hydrogen bond can therefore be estimated as:

            $\Delta H_{\ce{H-bond}} = \dfrac{45 \textrm{\ kJ}}{1 \textrm{\ mol\ } \ce{H2O}} \times \dfrac{1 \textrm{\ mol\ }\ce{H2O}}{2 \textrm{\ H bonds}} = 22.5 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1} $

            This value represents the average energy required to break a single hydrogen bond in liquid water, consistent with experimental data (≈ 20 kJ mol⁻¹), and highlights the significant role of hydrogen bonding in water’s high enthalpy of vaporization.
