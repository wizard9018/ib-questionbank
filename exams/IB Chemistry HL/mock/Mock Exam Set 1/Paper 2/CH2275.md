1.  1. $M_r = \dfrac{157.84\times51 + 159.83\times100 + 161.83\times 49}{51+100+49} \: \checkmark$\
    $M_r = 159.81255 \: \checkmark$ \
    :answer[$M_r = 159.81$] $\: \checkmark$

        *Award full marks for final answer.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation:}}$\
        To determine the relative molecular mass of the bromine gas ($\ce{Br2}$), we take the weighted average of the masses of each of the molecules observed in the mass spectrometer.

        $M_r = \dfrac{\textrm{mass}_1 \times \textrm{abundance}_1 +\textrm{mass}_2\times \textrm{abundance}_2 + \cdots} {\textrm{abundance}_1+ \textrm{abundance}_2 + \cdots}$

        The two peaks on the left$-$hand$-$side correspond to the two isotopes of atomic Br, bromine$-$79 and bromine$-$81.  The three peaks on the right$-$hand$-$side correspond to the three isotopic froms of the molecule: one made with two bromine$-$79 atoms with a mass near 158, one made with two bromine$-$81 atoms with a mass near 162, and the last near 160 comprised of on atom of bromine$-$79 and bromine$-$81.  Reading their abundances from the mass spectrum provides the following data:

        |mass / charge ratio | abundance
        :-:|:-:
        157.84 | 51
        159.83 |  100
        161.83 |  49

        Substituting these values into the weighted average which is rounded to two decimal places as required.

        $M_r = \dfrac{157.84\times51 + 159.83\times100 + 161.83\times 49}{51+100+49}= 159.81255\longrightarrow 159.81$

    2. :answer[neutrons: 44 **AND** protons 35] $\checkmark$\
    :answer[electrons: 34] $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation:}}$\
        In mass spectrometry, species are first vaporized into the gas phase and then ionized, usually by electron bombardment, to form positively charged ions. These ions are accelerated, deflected in a magnetic field according to their mass$-$to$-$charge ratio ($m/z$), and finally detected. Because the charge is typically $1+$, the $m/z$ value corresponds directly to the mass number of the isotope in atomic mass units.

        The peak at $m/z = 79$ therefore represents the isotope $^{79}\ce{Br}^+$. All bromine atoms contain 35 protons, as given by the atomic number of bromine. The mass number of 79 is the sum of protons and neutrons:

        $\textrm{\# of neutrons} = 79 - 35 = 44$

        Finally the charge $1+$, is the number of protons minus the number of electrons.

        $\textrm{\# of electrons} = 35-1 = 34$

2.  1. $\ce{[Ar]}4s^2 3d^{10} 4p^5 \: \checkmark$

        Bromine has an atomic number of 35, so it possesses 35 electrons. According to the aufbau principle, electrons fill orbitals in order of increasing energy, as illustrated by the diagonal arrow diagram of sublevels.

        ![CH2275aufbau](images/CH2275aufbau.png)

        Following this pattern, the orbitals are filled in the sequence $1s,$ $2s,$ $2p,$ $3s,$ $3p,$ $4s,$ $3d,$, and then $4p.$  When all 35 electrons are placed, the full configuration is

        Br: $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 4s^2 3d^{10} 4p^5$

        In condensed form, this is written as

        Br: $\ce{[Ar]}4s^2 3d^{10} 4p^5$

        The aufbau diagram makes it clear why the $3d$ orbitals are filled after $4s$ but before $4p,$ and why bromine ends with five electrons in the $4p$ sublevel, consistent with its Group 17 placement.

    2. same / 7 valence electrons $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Fluorine, chlorine, and bromine are all halogens in Group 17, so their atoms each have seven valence electrons. This is represented in their electron configurations by an outer shell of the form $ns^2 np^5.$ For fluorine the outer shell is $2s^2 2p^5,$ for chlorine it is $3s^2 3p^5,$ and for bromine it is $4s^2 4p^5.$ The shared feature of seven valence electrons explains why all halogens readily gain one more electron to achieve a complete outer shell, and why they exhibit similar patterns of reactivity.

    3. electronegativity decreases down the group $\checkmark$
    constant effective nuclear charge **AND** increasing radius $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Electronegativity is defined as the ability of an atom in a covalent bond to attract the shared pair of electrons toward itself. In the halogens, electronegativity decreases from fluorine down to iodine. This trend arises from the balance between effective nuclear charge and atomic radius. The effective nuclear charge, calculated as the atomic number minus the number of inner shielding electrons, is approximately +7 for all halogens and therefore remains essentially constant down the group. However, as successive electron shells are added, the atomic radius increases markedly. The bonding pair of electrons is thus located further from the nucleus, and because electrostatic attraction decreases with distance, the pull of the nucleus on the shared pair is weaker in the heavier halogens. Consequently, fluorine, with its small radius and strong nuclear attraction, is the most electronegative, and electronegativity decreases steadily down the group.

3.  1. discrete emissions lines correspond to specific electronic transitions $\checkmark$\
        electrons must move between specific energy levels $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The hydrogen emission spectrum shows a series of distinct coloured lines, rather than a continuous spread of light. Each line corresponds to a photon of a specific frequency (and therefore energy) released when an electron in a hydrogen atom transitions from a higher energy level to a lower one. The presence of sharp, discrete lines demonstrates that electrons can only occupy certain fixed, quantized energy levels in the atom. If the electrons could take on any value of energy, the spectrum would be continuous rather than line$-$based.

    2. ionization $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        In the hydrogen emission spectrum, the limit of convergence is the point at which the spectral lines merge so closely that they appear continuous. This occurs because, as the principal quantum number increases, the energy difference between successive levels becomes very small. At the limit, the electron is being excited from the ground state all the way to the ionization level ($n = \infty$), meaning it has just enough energy to leave the atom entirely. Thus, the limit of convergence represents the transition of the electron to the point of ionization.

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        In the initiation step of a halogen free$-$radical reaction, the $\ce{Br-Br}$ bond is broken by homolytic fission. In this process, the bonding pair of electrons is shared equally between the two bromine atoms, producing two identical bromine radicals, each with one unpaired electron. This is shown with single$-$barbed (fish$-$hook) arrows, each indicating the movement of one electron from the bond to each bromine atom. By contrast, heterolytic fission would involve both electrons moving to a single atom, forming ions, which does not occur in radical initiation.

    2. $E_\ce{photon} = 193 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1} \times \dfrac{1000 \textrm{\ J}}{1 \textrm{\ kJ}} \times \dfrac{1 \textrm{\ mol}}{6.02 \times 10^{23} \textrm{\ photons}} = 3.21 \times 10^{-19} \textrm{\ J} \: \checkmark$

        $f = \dfrac{E}{h} = \dfrac{3.21 \times 10^{-19} \textrm{\ J}}{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \textrm{\ Js}} = 4.84 \times 10^{14} \textrm{\ s}^{-1} \: \checkmark$

        $\lambda = \dfrac{c}{f} = \dfrac{3.00 \times 10^8 \textrm{\ m s}^{-1}}{4.84\times 10^{14}\textrm{\ s}^{-1}} \times \dfrac{10^9 \textrm{\ nm}}{1 \textrm{\ m}} = \  $:answer[620 $\textrm{\ nm}$] $\: \checkmark$

        *Award full marks for final answer.*

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanations:}}$\
        To calculate the wavelength of light required to break a $\ce{Br-Br}$ bond, we begin by finding the energy of a single photon that corresponds to the bond enthalpy. The average bond enthalpy of $193 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1}$ represents the energy needed to break one mole of bonds, so dividing by Avogadro's constant and converting to joules gives the energy per bond:

        $E_\ce{photon} = 193 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1} \times \dfrac{1000 \textrm{\ J}}{1 \textrm{\ kJ}} \times \dfrac{1 \textrm{\ mol}}{6.02 \times 10^{23} \textrm{\ photons}} = 3.21 \times 10^{-19} \textrm{\ J}$

        This photon energy is related to frequency through Planck's equation $E = hf.$ Using Planck's constant, the frequency is:

        $f = \dfrac{E}{h} = \dfrac{3.21 \times 10^{-19} \textrm{\ J}}{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \textrm{\ Js}} = 4.84 \times 10^{14} \textrm{\ s}^{-1}$

        Finally, the wavelength of the light can be obtained from the relationship $c = \lambda f.$ Rearranging and substituting the speed of light gives:

        $\lambda = \dfrac{c}{f} = \dfrac{3.00 \times 10^8 \textrm{\ m s}^{-1}}{4.84\times 10^{14}\textrm{\ s}^{-1}} \times \dfrac{10^9 \textrm{\ nm}}{1 \textrm{\ m}} = 620 \textrm{\ nm}$

        Thus, the wavelength of light required is 620 nm, which lies in the red region of the visible spectrum.
