2.  1. Any three of the following:

        Same functional group $\checkmark$\
        Same general formula $\checkmark$\
        Differ by a $\ce{CH2}\: \checkmark$\
        Similar chemical reactivity $\checkmark$\
        Gradation in their physical properties $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        A homologous series is a family of compounds with the same functional group and therefore similar chemical properties. They can all be described by a general formula, and each member differs from the next by a $\ce{CH2}$ group. This leads to a smooth trend in physical properties such as boiling point or solubility.

    2. $\ce{C_{n}H_{2n+1}OH}$ **OR** $\ce{C_{n}H_{2n+2}O} \: \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Any alcohol will have the same general formula. This general formula could be worked out checking the first members:

        $\ce{CH3OH}$ |    $\ce{CH4O}$
        :--|:--|
        $\ce{CH3CH2OH}$|$\ce{C2H6O}$
        $\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH}$ | $\ce{C3H8O}$
        $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2OH}$ |    $\ce{C4H10O}$
        $\ldots$ | $\ldots$
        General formula:    |    $\ce{C_{n}H_{2n+2}O}$

        Alternatively, alcohols have a hydroxy group $(\ce{-OH})$ attached to an alkyl chain. The alkyl group is $\ce{C_{n}H_{2n+1}},$ so the general formula of alcohols is $\ce{C_{n}H_{2n+1}OH}.$

3.  $\ce{C4H9Cl}\textrm{(aq)} +\ce{OH-}\textrm{(aq)} \longrightarrow \ce{C4H9OH}\textrm{(aq)} + \ce{Cl-}\textrm{(aq)} \: \checkmark$\
    Nucleophile: $\ce{OH-} \: \checkmark$\
    Leaving group: $\ce{Cl-} \: \checkmark$\
    *Ignore state symbols.*

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    This reaction is an example of a nucleophilic substitution reaction. The hydroxide ion, $\ce{OH-},$ has a lone pair of electrons, which it uses to attack the carbon atom bonded to chlorine. This carbon is susceptible to attack because the $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond is polar: chlorine is more electronegative, pulling electron density towards itself, so the carbon becomes partially positive $(\delta^+).$

    As the hydroxide ion forms a new bond to carbon, the $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond breaks and the chloride ion leaves. The hydroxide ion is the nucleophile because it donates an electron pair to the carbon. The chloride ion is the leaving group, as it departs with the electron pair from the $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond.

    ![CH2273arrows](images/CH2273arrows.png)

    The overall effect is that chlorine in 1$-$chlorobutane is replaced by the hydroxyl group, producing butan$-$1$-$ol.

4.  $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond is polar $\checkmark$\
    Carbon is electron$-$deficient and accepts an electron pair $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    Chlorine is more electronegative than carbon, so the $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond is polar. This places a partial positive charge $(\delta^+)$ on the carbon bonded to chlorine and a partial negative charge $(\delta^-)$ on chlorine.

    An electrophile is a species (or site) that accepts an electron pair, and this $\delta^+$ carbon fits that description: it is electron$-$poor and therefore attracts electron$-$rich reagents (nucleophiles). When OH⁻ approaches, it donates a lone pair to that carbon (forms the new $\ce{C-O}$ bond), while the $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond breaks heterolytically, with both electrons going to chlorine to give $\ce{Cl-},$ a stable anion. Thus, the carbon behaves as an electrophilic center because bond polarity makes it electron$-$deficient and receptive to nucleophilic attack and bond formation.

5.  $\mbox{}\\[-30pts]$

    |![CH2273transstate](images/CH2273transstate.png)|$\checkmark \checkmark$
    :-:|:-:

    *M1 for overall charge, trigonal bipyramidal shape, brackets or $\ddag$*\
    *M2 for partial bonds to OH and Cl, 180$\degree$ apart.*

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    This substitution follows an $\ce{S_N2}$ (bimolecular nucleophilic substitution) pathway, which occurs in a single, concerted step: as the hydroxide ion approaches, the $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond begins to break while the $\ce{C-O}$ bond begins to form. In the transition state the central carbon is simultaneously interacting with five groups, so its geometry is best represented as trigonal bipyramidal: the incoming $\ce{OH-}$ and the departing Cl occupy the axial positions, and the other three substituents lie roughly in an equatorial plane.

    ![CH2273mech](images/CH2273mech.png)

    To show that this is a high$-$energy, fleeting transition state rather than an intermediate, the structure is written in square brackets or with a double dagger $(\ddag).$ The transition state has an overall negative charge due to the charge on the hydroxide ion.

    A defining feature of $\ce{S_N2}$ is back$-$side attack: the nucleophile approaches from the side opposite the leaving group to approach the partially positive carbon atom. Because of this, $\ce{HO-C-Cl}$ must be collinear in the transition state, and the $\ce{C}\cdot \cdot \cdot \ce{O}$ and $\ce{C}\cdot \cdot \cdot \ce{Cl}$ connections are drawn as dashed partial bonds to show that one bond is forming while the other is breaking.

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        A stereoisomer is a compound with the same molecular formula and the same connectivity, but a different three$-$dimensional arrangement of atoms. For 2$-$butanol, carbon 2 is a chiral center: it is bonded to  $\ce{-OH},$ $\ce{-H},$ $\ce{-CH3},$ and $\ce{-CH2CH3},$ four different groups. That means there are two stereoisomers, called enantiomers, that form a pair of non$-$superimposable mirror images. To earn full marks, draw two 3D wedge$-$dash structures at the chiral carbon: one arrangement for the first stereoisomer and its mirror image for the second. The pair must not be superimposable; if a rotation makes them identical, you have drawn the same stereoisomer twice.

    3. $-13.52\degree \: \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Enantiomers rotate plane$-$polarized light in equal but opposite directions. As a result, if one enantiomer rotates the light $+13.52\degree;$ the other enantiomer should have the same rotation with opposite direction,  $-13.52\degree.$

7. 2 signals $\checkmark$\
    Area ratio 9:1 $\checkmark$\
    Both singlets $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    A chemical environment is the local “surroundings” a hydrogen experiences, determined by what its own carbon is attached to, and where it sits in the molecule. Hydrogens in identical surroundings give the same NMR signal; hydrogens in different surroundings give different signals.

    In 2$-$methylpropan$-$2$-$ol, the central carbon is bonded to the $\ce{-OH}$ group and to three identical $\ce{CH_3}$ groups. Because those three methyl groups are arranged symmetrically, all nine of their hydrogens (in blue) experience the same environment, so they produce one signal together. The $\ce{O-H}$ hydrogen (in red) has a different environment (it is attached to oxygen, not carbon), so it gives a second signal. There are two signals in the $^1$H NMR spectrum.

    ![CH2273NMR](images/CH2273NMR.png)

    The areas under the signals match how many hydrogens are contributing to each. One signal comes from nine equivalent methyl hydrogens, and the other from one $\ce{O-H}$ hydrogen, so the integration ratio is $9:1.$

    For splitting, a signal is split only by hydrogens on the next carbon along the chain (to identify a neighbor, look for a hydrogen three bonds away). The nine methyl hydrogens sit on carbons that are next to a carbon with no hydrogens, so they have zero neighbors to interact with and appear as a singlet (the “$n+1$ rule” with $𝑛=0).$ The $\ce{O-H}$ hydrogen also shows up as a singlet as it has no neighbors.  (The $\ce{O-H}$ hydrogen exchanges rapidly with the solvent and is typically observed as a broad singlet in NMR.)

9. $74: \ce{C4H9OH^+} \textbf{\ OR\ } \ce{C4H_{10}O^+} \: \checkmark$\
    $31: \ce{CH2OH^+} \textbf{\ OR\ } \ce{CH3O^+} \: \checkmark$\
    *Penalize a missing + only once.*

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    The relative molecular mass of butan$-$1$-$ol is 74, so the peak at $m/z = 74$ corresponds to the parent (molecular) ion, $\ce{C4H9OH^+}$ or $\ce{C4H_{10}O^+}$. The peak at $m/z = 31$ represents a fragment. This can be deduced by noting that $74 - 31 = 43$, consistent with loss of a propyl fragment $(\ce{C3H7}),$ and it can also be recognized directly as a $\ce{CH3O+}$ or $\ce{CH2OH+}$ fragment.

10. 1$-$iodobutane $\checkmark$ \
    $\ce{I-}$ is a better leaving group **OR** $\ce{C-I}$ bond is weaker $\checkmark$ \
    *Award M2 for the opposite about Cl.*

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    In a primary halogenoalkane under aqueous conditions the hydrolysis is nucleophilic substitution. The $\ce{C-I}$ bond is weaker than the $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond, so it breaks more readily (lower activation energy), and iodide is a better leaving group than chloride (larger, more polarizable, more stable anion). Either justifies the faster rate for 1-iodobutane.
