2. $\ce{[OH-]} = \sqrt{K_b \times \ce{[(CH3)3N]}} = \sqrt{6.5 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.150} = 3.12 \times 10^{-3} \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3} \: \checkmark$\
    $\textrm{pOH} = -\log_{10} 3.12 \times 10^{-3} = 2.51 \: \checkmark$ \
    $\textrm{pH} = 14 - 2.51 = \ $ :answer[11.49] $\: \checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    Trimethylamine, $\ce{N(CH3)3},$ is a weak Brønsted base. To calculate the equilibrium, we can use a RICE analysis:

    R: | $\ce{(CH3)3N}\textrm{(aq)}$ |  + |  $\ce{H2O}\textrm{(l)}$ |  $\longrightarrow$ |  $\ce{(CH3)3NH+}\textrm{(aq)}$ | + | $\ce{OH-}\textrm{(aq)}$
    :-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
    $\textbf{I:}$ | 0.150 | | ||0|| 0
    $\textbf{C:}$ | $-x$ | | ||$+x$|| $+x$
    $\textbf{E:}$ | $0.150-x$ | | ||$x$|| $x$

    The expression for the base dissociation constant is

    $K_b = \dfrac{\ce{[N(CH3)3H+]}\ce{[OH⁻]}}{\ce{[N(CH3)3]}} = \dfrac{x^2}{0.150 - x}$

    With $K_b = 6.5 \times 10^{-5}$ and the standard weak$-$base approximation $(x \ll 0.150),$ we take $0.150 - x ≈ 0.150,$ giving

    $K_b = 6.5 \times 10^{-5} \approx  \dfrac{x^2}{0.150}$

    $x = \ce{[OH⁻]} \approx \sqrt{(6.5 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.150}  = 3.12 \times 10^{-3} \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$

    Hence,

    $\textrm{pOH} = -\log_{10}(3.12\times10^{-2}) = 2.51$

     and

    $\textrm{pH} = 14.00 - 2.51 = 11.49$

    The approximation is justified: the fractional change in the base concentration is

    $\dfrac{x}{0.150} ≈ \dfrac{0.00312}{0.150} ≈ 2.1\% \ \ (< 5\%)$

    so neglecting $x$ in the denominator is valid and the reported pH is consistent to the stated precision.

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    This is a weak base$-$strong acid titration, so the curve begins basic and finishes acidic. The initial pH is that of $0.150 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$ trimethylamine and is approximately 11.5. The equivalence volume follows from stoichiometry:

    $n_{\ce{(CH3)3N}} = CV = 0.150 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3} \times 0.0200 \textrm{\ dm}^3 = 0.00300 \textrm{\ mol}$

    $n_{\ce{(CH3)3N}}=n_\ce{HCl}$

    $V_\ce{HCl} = \dfrac{n}{C} = \dfrac{0.00300 \textrm{\ mol}}{0.100 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}} = 0.0300 \textrm{\ dm}^3 = 30.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3$

    As HCl is added, a buffer mixture of base and its conjugate acid forms and the pH follows the Henderson$-$Hasselbalch relation

    $\textrm{pH} = \textrm{p}K_a + \log_{10}\dfrac{[\ce{(CH3)3N}]}{[\ce{(CH3)3NH^+}]}$

    For bases, it is convenient to recognize that $\textrm{p}K_a = 14 - \textrm{p}K_b$, so

    $\textrm{pH} = 14-\textrm{p}K_b + \log_{10}\dfrac{[\ce{(CH3)3N}]}{[\ce{(CH3)3NH^+}]}$

    For trimethylamine, $K_b = 6.5 \times 10^{-5}$ so $\textrm{p}K_b = 4.19$.  At the half$-$equivalence point of $15 \textrm{\ cm}^3,$ $[\ce{(CH3)3NH+}]=[\ce{(CH3)3N}],$ so $\textrm{pH} = 14 - 4.19 = 9.81.$

    At the equivalence point $(30 \textrm{\ cm}^3),$ only the conjugate acid is present, so the solution is acidic $(\textrm{pH} < 7;$ for these concentrations it is around 5), and the inflection is less steep than for a strong$-$strong titration.

    Beyond equivalence, excess strong acid controls the pH and the curve declines toward the acidic region where is should approach a pH of 1.

    Complex formation with transition$-$metal ions requires a ligand that can act as a Lewis base, donating a lone pair to the metal to form a coordinate covalent (dative) bond.

5.  1. increases the proportion of molecules with energy above activation energy $\checkmark$\
        increases kinetic energy of molecules, more frequent collisions $\checkmark$\
        more effective collisions occur per unit time so rate increases $\checkmark$

        Collision theory states that the reaction rate depends on the number of effective collisions per unit time.  For a reactino to occur, collisions must have  sufficient energy to overcome the activation energy $(E_a)$ and with a suitable orientation.

        When temperature increases from $T_1$ to a higer temperature $T_2,$ the average kinetic energy of the molecules rises and the Maxwell$-$Boltzmann distribution broadens and shifts to higher energies.

        ![CH2276mb](images/CH2276mb.png)

        As a result, a much larger fraction of molecules has energy $\geq E_a,$ so the proportion of collisions that are successful increases sharply. In addition, the molecules move faster, so the collision frequency also increases. Together, more frequent collisions and a greater fraction of collision with energy above the activation energy increase the rate of formation of trimethylamine.

6. 1. trimethylamine nitrogen: $-3 \: \checkmark$\
        TMAO nitrogen: $-1 \: \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$ \
        Oxidation state is a bookkeeping device: assign the bonding electrons in each bond to the more electronegative atom. In trimethylamine, nitrogen is bonded to three carbons; because nitrogen is more electronegative than carbon, the electrons in each $\ce{C-N}$ bond are assigned to nitrogen.  Nitrogen has the two lone pair electrons as well as these six bonding electrons. While the nitrogen atom has five valence electrons, the nitrogen here has eight electrons.  Its oxidation state is $5-8=-3.$

        In trimethylamine N$-$oxide, nitrogen is still bonded to three carbons but now it is also bonded to oxygen, which is more electronegative than nitrogen; in that $\ce{N-O}$ bond the electrons are assigned to oxygen, so nitrogen has six electrons in this molecule and an oxidation state of $5-6=-1.$

        The change from $-3 \longrightarrow -1$ shows that nitrogen has been oxidized in the conversion of trimethylamine to its N$-$oxide.

    2.  oxidation: $\ce{(CH3)3N} + \ce{H2O} \longrightarrow \ce{(CH3)3NO} + 2 \ce{\,H+} + 2 \ce{\,e-} \: \checkmark$\
        reductions: $\ce{H2O2} + 2 \ce{\,H+} + 2 \ce{\,e-} \longrightarrow 2 \ce{\,H2O} \:\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$ \
        In acidic solution, balance redox half$-$equations by

        * balancing all atoms except H and O,
        * balancing O with $\ce{H2O},$
        * balancing H with $\ce{H^+}$, AND
        * balancing charge with electrons.

        Electrons appear on the products side for oxidation and on the reactants side for reduction.

        For the oxidation half reaction, we are converting trimethlyamine to TMAO:

        $\ce{(CH3)3N} \longrightarrow \ce{(CH3)3NO}$

        Add $\ce{H2O}$ to balance the oxygen:

        $\ce{(CH3)3N} + \ce{H2O} \longrightarrow \ce{(CH3)3NO}$

        Now balance H by adding 2 $\ce{H+}$ to the right,

        $\ce{(CH3)3N} + \ce{H2O} \longrightarrow \ce{(CH3)3NO} + 2 \ce{\,H+}$

        Finally, balance the charge with $2 \ce{\,e-}$ on the right:

        $\ce{(CH3)3N} + \ce{H2O} \longrightarrow \ce{(CH3)3NO} + 2 \ce{\,H+} + 2 \ce{\,e-}$

        Notice that the electrons lost are consistent with the change in oxidation state from $-3$ to $-1$ found in the previous question.

        For the reduction of hydrogen peroxided to water, we start with

        $\ce{H2O2} \longrightarrow \ce{H2O}$

        Balance O by making 2 $\ce{H2O}$ on the right:

        $\ce{H2O2} \longrightarrow 2 \ce{\,H2O}$

        Balance H by adding 2 H+ to the left,

        $\ce{H2O2} + 2 \ce{\,H+} \longrightarrow 2 \ce{\,H2O}$

        then balance charge with 2 $\ce{e-}$ to the left:

        $\ce{H2O2} + 2 \ce{\,H+} + 2 \ce{\,e-} \longrightarrow 2 \ce{\,H2O}$

        Adding the two half$-$reactions cancels 2 $\ce{H+},$ 2 $\ce{e-},$ and one $\ce{H2O},$ giving the overall reaction:

        $\ce{(CH3)3N} + \ce{H2O2} \longrightarrow \ce{(CH3)3NO} + \ce{H2O}$
