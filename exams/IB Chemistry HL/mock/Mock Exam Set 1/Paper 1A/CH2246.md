# CH2246

Two substances, J and Q, have the following properties:

Property|J|Q
:-:|:-:|:-:
Melting Point|High|High
Electrical Conductivity| Conducts when solid or molten|Conducts when molten or aqueous
Solubility in Water| Mostly Insoluble | Mostly Soluble
Mechanical Properties | Malleable and ductile |Brittle

Which best explains the difference in bonding and structure between J and Q?

-   A.$\hspace{1.em}$J has delocalized electrons that move freely through a lattice; Q contains charged particles that are fixed in place until the structure is disrupted.

-   B.$\hspace{1.em}$J has directional covalent bonds in a 3D network; Q has electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions.

-   C.$\hspace{1.em}$J has weak intermolecular forces between molecules; Q has strong electrostatic attractions between charged particles.

-   D.$\hspace{1.em}$J has mobile cations; Q has polar covalent bonding with hydrogen bonds.

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

**答案：A**

$\textrm{\underline{Explanation:}}$\
This question tests the connection between physical properties and underlying bonding models. Substance J conducts electricity as a solid and is malleable, indicating metallic bonding. In metals, delocalized electrons move freely through a lattice of positive metal cations. These mobile electrons allow for conductivity and enable layers of atoms to slide without breaking the structure.

Substance Q does not conduct as a solid but does when molten or dissolved, which is characteristic of an ionic compound. In the solid state, ions are fixed in a crystal lattice. When the structure is disrupted by melting or dissolving in water, these ions become mobile and conduct electricity. The brittle nature of Q also supports an ionic structure, as shifting layers can bring ions of the same charge into contact, causing repulsion and fracturing.

Both substances have high melting points due to strong electrostatic attractions between ions in Q and between cations and electrons in J. The key distinction lies in the type of mobile charge carriers: delocalized electrons in metals, and ions in ionic compounds.
