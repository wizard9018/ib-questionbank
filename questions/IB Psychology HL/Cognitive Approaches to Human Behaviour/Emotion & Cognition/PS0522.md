# PS0522

Explain how emotion may influence one cognitive process.
[9 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$: \

A maximum of **9 marks** are awarded if the response includes all of the following:

$\textcolor{#000000}{\textbf{1. Direct Answer}\ [\textbf{DA}]}$\

A direct answer to the question provided in the first few sentences *(e.g. “Emotion is a state of mind determined by mood, with cognitive factors increasingly seen as important in the experience of emotion. Lazarus' (1982) cognitive appraisal theory stresses the importance of cognition when as we experience an event, thoughts proceed physiological arousal and the sensation of emotion (which occur simultaneously). Therefore some cognitive processing must occur before an individual experiences an emotional response to an event. This can be linked to flashbulb memories.”)*

$\textcolor{#F04438}{\textbf{2. Key Terms}\ [\textbf{KT}]}$\

Accurate knowledge and understanding of emotion and the process

Important concepts and vocabulary terms to use and/or define include: 

- *Emotion and perception - Perception is biased by schemas. Perceptual defense- where emotionally threatening stimuli takes longer to perceive.*

- *Emotion and memory - Memory can be affected by schemas. flashbulb memory, repression and contextual emotional effects.*

- *Emotion and decision making - Emotions, especially negative ones, can lead to changes in decision-making.* 

The command term $`$explain' is addressed. 

This requires students to give a detailed account of emotion which includes possible reasons or causes for the behaviour in relation to one research study linked to the cognitive approach to understanding behaviour.   

$\textcolor{#008c3a}{\textbf{3. Study}\ [\textbf{S}]}$\

A clear focus on describing one study – including the aim, procedure, results, and conclusion of the study\

*Relevant studies include but are not limited to:*

- Brown and Kulik (1977) Flashbulb memories

- Hardy and Legge (1968) Perceptual defence with high and low emotional words

- Speisman et al. (1964) Cognitive appraisal and arousal

- Bower (1981) Encoding and retrieval based on emotional states

- Hirst et. al (2011) FBM and accuracy over time

- Holmes (1990) Repression over time

$\textcolor{#4472C4}{\textbf{4. Link}\ [\textbf{L}]}$\

An explicit link exploring emotion to one study and in reference to the question *(e.g. $`$Brown and Kulik (1977) found that when participants were asked to recall the circumstances they found themselves in when they first received the news about some significantly emotional events, participants have vivid memories of where they were, what they did and what they felt.')*

Marking limits:

- Emotion, its related parts and what cognitive process you will be discussing are not explained in the opening paragraph.

- Responses should clearly relate emotion to one cognitive process and must focus on the cognitive aspects of the research.

- Explaining more than one study will mean marks are only given to the first study described.

- Describing a study but the type of cognitive process is not explicitly addressed *(maximum 3 marks awarded)*

- Explaining emotion and a cognitive process but does not refer to an appropriate study *(maximum 4 marks awarded)*

- Clear links between emotion, the cognitive process and the study are needed to gain marks in the top mark band. *(If the link is only implicit a maximum of 6 marks will be awarded)*

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$: \

Emotion is a state of mind determined by mood, with cognitive factors increasingly seen as important in the experience of emotion. Lazarus' (1982) cognitive appraisal theory stresses the importance of cognition as when we experience an event, thoughts proceed physiological arousal and the sensation of emotion (which occur simultaneously). Therefore some cognitive processing must occur before an individual experiences an emotional response to an event. This can be linked to flashbulb memories.$\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{DA}]}$

A flashbulb memory is a very detailed and vivid $`$snapshot' of the moment and circumstances in which  emotionally arousing news was heard.$\textcolor{#F04438}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ The $`$flashbulb' terminology indicates the events will be registered like a photograph; it will be accurate in detail.$\textcolor{#F04438}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ The defining feature of a $`$flashbulb' memory is not the memory of the event, but a memory of its reception context -  where you were when you heard the news.$\textcolor{#F04438}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ It is assumed that they are very resistant to forgetting; that the details of the memory will continue to be intact and accurate because of the emotional arousal at the time the memory was encoded.$\textcolor{#008c3a}{[\textbf{S}]}$ For example, some individuals can report, in exceptional detail, when and how they heard the news of the September 11 US terrorist attacks, as well as exactly what they were doing at the time and their exact feelings and reactions in response to the news.$\textcolor{#008c3a}{[\textbf{S}]}$ There is a positive relationship between strong emotion at the time of encoding and exceptional details of these memories.$\textcolor{#008c3a}{[\textbf{S}]}$ Brown and Kulik (1977) aimed to study whether shocking events are recalled more vividly and accurately compared to other events.$\textcolor{#008c3a}{[\textbf{S}]}$ They gathered 80 US participants who were asked questions about ten events. Nine of the events were mostly assassinations or attempted assassinations of well-known American personalities ( JF Kennedy, Martin Luther King). The tenth was a self-elected event of personal relevance and feeling of surprise. The examples include the death of a friend or a family member or a serious accident. Participants were asked to remember the circumstances they found themselves in when they heard the news about the ten events. They were also asked to indicate how often they had rehearsed (over the years) information about each event.$\textcolor{#008c3a}{[\textbf{S}]}$ 

The results show that participants had vivid memories of where they were, what they did and what they felt when they first heard about the shocking public events. The participants also said they had similar memories of their own shocking personal events. The results indicated that $`$flashbulb' memory is more likely for unexpected and personally relevant events. This led the researchers to suggest $`$the photographic nature of $`$flashbulb' memories'. Brown and Kulik suggested that flashbulb memory is caused by physiological arousal and physiological emotional arousal, for example, activity of the amygdala.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{L}]}$
