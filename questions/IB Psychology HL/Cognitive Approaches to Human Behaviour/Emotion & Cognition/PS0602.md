# PS0602

With reference to one or more research studies, discuss the influence of emotion on cognition.

[22 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:\

Flashbulb Memory (FBM) Theory suggests that emotion does affect the cognitive process of memory. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ Memory is the process by which we manipulate, store, and retrieve information and experiences. According to FBM theory, emotional events are remembered in more vivid detail than ordinary memories. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$  It is important to understand the influence of emotion on memory because of potential implications related to the extent to which we can trust our emotionally charged memories. The influence of emotion on memory can be discussed by examining FBM theory with reference to studies conducted by Conway et al. (1994) and Talarico $\&$ Rubin (2003). $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

According to FBM theory, flashbulb memories are remarkably resistant to fading over time, making them more accurate, vivid, and enduring compared to regular memories. The theory also states that people can often recall specific details about flashbulb memories, such as: where they were when they heard about the event, what activity they were engaged in, how they were informed, their personal reactions, and the responses and reactions of those around them. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ It is worth noting that a great deal of debate surrounds the validity of flashbulb memory theory. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

One study that provides support for FBM theory is Conway et al. (1994). The aim of the study was to investigate the formation of flashbulb memories after hearing the news of UK Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher's resignation. The procedure involved 369 participants from inside  and outside the UK. Each of them was given a flashbulb memory questionnaire (FBQ) 10-14 days after Thatcher's resignation. The questionnaire asked them to describe their memory of when they learned of the resignation, without prompting. Each participant had their memory re-tested 11 months later. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

The results showed that the UK participants had exceptionally vivid and accurate memories 11 months later, with 86% having flashbulb memories. However, the majority of non-UK participants showed high levels of forgetting, consistent with ordinary memory decay. The researchers concluded that the UK participants formed long-lasting and vivid flashbulb memories because of the emotional significance of the event. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ Therefore, this study provides support for FBM theory and suggests that strong emotions may have a positive effect on memory. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ 

However, the extent to which this study provides support for FBM theory and the influence of emotion on memory depends on how robust the research was; therefore, it is essential that we evaluate the study and its conclusions. As this study made use of a questionnaire after the event to collect the data, no independent variable was manipulated. As such, the researchers have not conclusively established that it is the emotional nature of the memories that has enhanced participant recall. Furthermore, the researchers did not question participants until 10-14 days after the resignation. By this time, the original memory may have already been corrupted. This means that when they compared the 11-month-old memory with the original memory, they may have been comparing two flawed memories, which reduces internal validity and confidence in the findings significantly. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

Further research investigating FBM theory and the influence of emotion on cognition was conducted by Talarico $\&$ Rubin (2003). $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ Their aim was to investigate the vividness and accuracy of flashbulb memories compared to ordinary memories. 52 university students filled out questionnaires about their memories of 9/11 and an ordinary event from the days leading up to 9/11. These questionnaires were completed one day after the 9/11 terrorist attacks. The researchers then gave them a follow up questionnaire at different intervals of 7 days, 42 days, and almost 1 year later. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

The results showed that the rate of memory decline and recall accuracy was similar between the ordinary memory and the 9/11 memory, with both being highly inaccurate. Despite this, participants were more confident in their 9/11 memory than their ordinary memory and reported that these memories were more vivid. The researchers concluded that while emotion may not make memories more accurate, it may make people believe that those memories are more accurate, meaning that people are more confident in these memories. Overall, this study did not provide support for Flashbulb Memory Theory, but it does provide some support for the role of emotion in our personal memory experience. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ While the study does not suggest that emotion in any way improves the accuracy of our memories, the study does suggest that emotion may cause us to believe that it does. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ 

While Talarico $\&$ Rubin's (2003) study also suffers from an inability to establish cause and effect, it does have a number of controls in place that enhance internal validity when compared to the previous study. Firstly, the initial recordings of participants' memories of the event took place the day after the event. This means that the memories were still likely to be fresh in their minds. Therefore, a comparison between the initial memories and the future recollections is more meaningful. Furthermore, unlike the previous study, the researchers had a control condition which involved comparing the flashbulb memory to memories of an ordinary event, which increased the internal validity of the study. Talarico & Rubin also noted that many other research studies have reached the same conclusions as them, thus increasing the reliability of the findings and conclusions. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

In conclusion, while Conway et al's (1994) study provides support for FBM theory and the positive influence of emotion on memory, there are significant concerns with the validity of the research and its conclusions. Research conducted by Talarico $\&$ Rubin (2003) suggests that emotion may impact our perception of our own memories by making us more confident in them, but emotion does not necessarily improve the accuracy of memories. Talarico $\&$ Rubin's (2003) more robust research study concluded that flashbulb memories suffer from the same issues with decay over time as ordinary memories. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ It would be worth investigating whether these unreliable, emotionally charged memories are measurably more vivid than regular memories. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$
