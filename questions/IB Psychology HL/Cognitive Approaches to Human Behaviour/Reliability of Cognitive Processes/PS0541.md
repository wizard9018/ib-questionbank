# PS0541

Evaluate one or more studies investigating reconstructive memory.
[22 marks]

---

$\textcolor{#000000}{\textbf{Criterion A: Focus on the question}\ }$

These responses can also discuss advancements and challenges in completing research on the topic, though this should ideally be linked to the studies presented.

**1 mark** if the question is either simply restated, misinterpreted (though still related to reconstructive memory, *e.g. This essay will evaluate reconstructive memory*), or if the overall response does not address the prompt (*e.g. the candidate writes in their introduction that they will evaluate research, but only discusses reconstructive memory*).

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:\
Memory is not photographic; it is reconstructive - meaning that memories are constructed by piecing together information from various sources. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Cognitive research can shine a light on patterns that exist in how we reconstruct those memories. Two studies that explore reconstructive memory are Brewer and Treyens (1981) and Loftus and Palmer (1974). The former explores how our schema or cognitive frameworks, for environments, can influence the accuracy of the objects we recall from that environment. The latter investigates how post-event information, even in the phrasing of questions, can shape our recall and cause us to reconstruct memories that distort our recollection of details. Each study can be evaluated in order to determine the validity of the research and its findings. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$

Brewer and Treyens' study aimed to understand how schema may play a role in the encoding and recollection of memories related to an office scene. Participants were seated in an office without knowing that the experiment had taken place to reduce participant variability's effect on working memory or effort. Some of the objects were $\text{\textquotedblleft}$schema consistent" with an object, such as desks, a coffee pot, and paper. Some of the objects were schema inconsistent and would be unexpected to see in an office, such as tree bark. After spending time in the office, participants were invited to a new room and asked to recall the objects within that office either by writing what they remembered, drawing what they remembered, or by responding yes or no to verbal prompts from a researcher. Their findings showed that participants often recalled items consistent with their schema of an office regardless of the recall condition. Participants who drew or wrote objects tended to forget objects that were schema-inconsistent. When listening to a list of objects, they were more likely to remember seeing schema-inconsistent objects but also tended to falsely recall objects that were typical of offices but were not in the room. These patterns of distortion suggest that schema does shape our encoding and recollection of objects. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

There are a number of strengths and limitations that impact the validity of this study. Brewer and Treyens utilised a questionnaire to determine whether or not participants may have been alert to the goals of the study while in the staged office area (they generally were not). This suggests that demand characteristics would likely not have influenced participant behaviour. Had they realised that the study had already started, they may have tried to memorise items in the office. While minor deception was used in this study, the collection of accurate data would not have been possible if participants were aware of the true aim of the study. No psychological harm was caused as a result of the study and participants were fully debriefed at the conclusion of the research. The use of minor deception was therefore ethical and helped to reduce the likelihood of demand characteristics. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$

In contrast, Loftus and Palmer's research focused on how question phrasing could predictably alter participants' memories of an event.  In this experiment, participants watched footage of a car accident and were then asked about the speed of the cars using different verbs to describe the collision (*e.g., $\text{\textquotedblleft}$hit," $\text{\textquotedblleft}$smashed"*). The results of this first study demonstrated that the verb used influenced participants' perception of the accident's severity, with participants who were asked the question phrasing involving the verb $\text{\textquotedblleft}$smashed'' reporting higher speeds than those asked with the verb $\text{\textquotedblleft}$hit." When asked a week later whether they saw broken glass in the footage (there was none), those who were asked with the term $\text{\textquotedblleft}$smashed" were more likely to report seeing it than both the $\text{\textquotedblleft}$hit” and control conditions. It is likely that participants integrated the intensity of the verb into their memories of the event, and because the more intense verbs are associated with more destructive accidents, they tended to recall broken glass because it is a feature of severe accidents. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

Loftus and Palmer's experiment was rigorously controlled, with manipulation of an IV to see the effect on the DV, with random allocation of participants to groups. Some examples of controlled variables include: the use of identical questions (other than the critical question), and identical videos across each condition. So, it was possible to isolate the effect of the IV and establish a cause-effect relationship between the independent variable (the critical words) and the dependent variable (estimation of speed). The experiment, however, was conducted in a laboratory setting, as such, there may be a problem of ecological validity. On many occasions, this type of memory reporting comes after shocking or otherwise emotionally arousing events, such as being asked to provide eyewitness testimony after a traumatic accident. The fact that the experiment used students as participants has also been criticized because students are not representative of the wider population. Importantly, students may be less experienced drivers and therefore less confident in their ability to estimate speeds. As such, their estimates may be based on guesswork, rather than the influence of the verb. While the findings of this study were significant, there are some contradictory findings, with other studies suggesting that leading questions or other post-event information does not distort memory accuracy for most participants. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$

In conclusion, both studies contribute to our understanding of reconstructive memory by highlighting two important aspects: the role of schemas and the influence of suggestive information. Brewer and Treyen's study demonstrates that our memories are not just recordings of sensory information but are constructed from both what we perceive, as well as being influenced by our schemas. Loftus and Palmer's research suggests that memory reconstruction may be impacted by leading questions and post-event information. While both studies contain strengths, ecological and external validity, along with contradictory findings should be considered. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$
