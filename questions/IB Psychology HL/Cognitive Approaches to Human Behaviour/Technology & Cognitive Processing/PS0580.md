# PS0580

Evaluate one or more studies that explore how the use of technology can affect cognition.

[22 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:\

Thinking is a cognitive process that involves using information and knowledge to consider concepts and ideas. Whereas decision making makes use of thinking processes to make choices. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ With the rapid advancement of various forms of technology, technology has become so pervasive in our lives that psychologists have begun exploring its effects on our decision-making abilities, notably system 1 thinking, which is a fast, intuitive, and automatic thinking process $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$. Bavelier et al (2011) explored how playing computer games improves the accuracy of rapid decision-making among its users, dispelling widely held beliefs that playing games entails no redeeming effects. However, with the rise of social media use, Chou and Edge (2012) have demonstrated that through the utilisation of the availability heuristic, social media platforms cause a rise in our tendency to evaluate our self-worth according to how we compare with the success and positive traits of others, known as social comparison theory $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. Often, this mechanism can cause downward comparison where people feel bad about themselves in comparison, or to put down others and feel better about themselves. It is clear that technology has both positive and negative effects on our thinking and, yet its integration into our lives has been so rapid and recent that its true effects are unprecedented. It is worth evaluating each of these studies to better understand how useful they are in shedding light on the effect of technology on cognitive processes.$\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$

Chou and Edge (2012) aimed to assess the effect of Facebook use on thinking and the availability heuristic, by focusing on how this shortcut leads individuals to make social comparisons $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. 425 undergraduate students took part in a survey that had a series of 10-point Likert scale questions centred around comparisons the participants made between themselves and people they see on their newsfeeds. Results demonstrated that the more people spent on Facebook each week, the more likely they were to evaluate others as being happier than they were. The conclusion can be made that the more time spent online, the more that users see the heavily curated lives of their friends and acquaintances, the less likely they are to be critical about what they see and more likely to attribute snapshots of time to someone's feed to their level of happiness. This flawed thinking can be explained in terms of the availability heuristic as people use the information that is most $\text{\textquotedblleft}$available" to them when making evaluative decisions about others' happiness compared to their own $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. Therefore, this suggests that the use of social media, which is a form of digital technology can result in flawed thinking.

In the above study, Chou & Edge used a Likert scale for participants to respond to the survey. While this allows data to be easily collated and quantified, it is difficult to capture the complexity of someone's lived experience on a numbered scale and lack of granularity means it is difficult to make distinctions between the response numbers $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. Different individuals will interpret the scale differently, which causes some construct invalidity in the concepts that are attached to the scale. While there is a clear relationship between the time that someone spends on Facebook and the way they negatively evaluate their happiness in relation to others, the relationship is correlational, and so no cause-and-effect relationship can be established $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. It is difficult to rule out other variables that are having an effect; a more controlled study would provide more reliable data $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. 

Bavelier et al. (2011) aimed to explore the relationship between playing action video games and decision-making skills. In a group of men and women whose average age is 26 and who had not played video games in one year, half were asked to play a video game for two hours a day where they had to make decisions about a character's life, and half were not asked to play a game. After a set period of time, all participants were asked to play a video game where they had to intuit the movement of dots on a screen. While all participants were able to complete the task successfully, the group who had played the action game were able to do so faster, while making fewer errors. The researchers concluded that the group who had played the game were able to deal with large amounts of visual information more quickly, had improved spatial reasoning, and were able to make faster decisions than the group who had not played the game. Highlighting a potential positive effect of technology on the cognitive process of decision-making.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$.

Bavelier's (2011) study was standardised, meaning the design is replicable and can be carried out by other researchers, which strengthens the reliability of the results. Also, the design is a true experiment, meaning a cause-and-effect relationship can be established $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. However, the participants were split randomly into the experimental and the control conditions, meaning participant variability may have been the cause of the differences in results between both conditions $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. Perhaps a baseline test could have been introduced to assess speed of decision-making before the study began, as this would have provided information about the improvement of skills of the experimental group after playing the character game. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

While these two studies suggest that the use of technology can have both positive and negative effects on our cognitive processes, both studies are limited in scope in that they only focused on Facebook and action video games. They were also only focused on thinking and decision making, which means they fail to take into account other cognitive processes. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$  It may be that the impact of digital technology on cognitive processes is influenced by the type of technology, how it is used and what you are measuring. It is, therefore, difficult to make informed conclusions about the impact of technology on cognitive processes from just these two studies. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ The experimental nature of Bavelier's study benefits from tight controls and being able to establish a cause-and-effect relationship, both of which are lacking in the study conducted by Chou & Edge. However, both studies lack an examination of the long term effects of technology and are limited in scope. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$
