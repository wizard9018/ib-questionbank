# PS0518

Explain the schema theory.
[9 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$: \

A maximum of **9 marks** are awarded if the response includes all of the following:

$\textcolor{#000000}{\textbf{1. Direct Answer}\ [\textbf{DA}]}$\

*A direct answer to the question provided in the first few sentences (e.g. “Schema theory explores the idea that our schemas affect and/or influence our perception of an event or idea…”)*

$\textcolor{#F04438}{\textbf{2. Key Terms}\ [\textbf{KT}]}$\

Accurate knowledge and understanding of schema theory.\

Important concepts and vocabulary terms to use and/or define include: *A schema is a knowledge structure that allows organisms to interpret and understand the world around them. Schemata are a method of organising information that allows the brain to work more efficiently.*

- *Piaget's theory of cognitive development put the concept at the forefront of cognitive science. Contemporary conceptions of schema evolved in the 1970s and 1980s.*

- *The widespread use of computers in the last decades of the 20th century also affected theories of how people store and use information in the brain.*

- *There are four main types of schemas. These are centred around objects, the self, roles, and events.*

- *Schemas can be changed and reconstructed throughout a person's life. The two processes for doing so are assimilation and accommodation.*

- *Schemas have been pivotal in influencing theories of learning as well as in teacher instruction methods.*

The command term $`$explain' is addressed. This requires to give a detailed account of the concept of schema and schema theory, including possible reasons or causes for the theory in relation to one research linked to the cognitive approach to understanding behaviour.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$: \

A schema is a cognitive structure that provides a framework for organizing information about the world, events, people and actions. Schema allows us to form expectations. These are integrated chunks of knowledge stored in long-term memory. They are mental representations of categories (from our knowledge, beliefs and expectations) and about particular aspects of the world such as people, objects, events, and situations.$\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{DA}]}$ \

There are three different types of schemas: scripts - provide information about the sequence of events that occurs in particular contexts (e.g going to a restaurant, visiting the dentist, attending class). Self-schemers organized information we have about ourselves (information stored in our memory about our strengths and weaknesses and how we feel about them). Social schemers (stereotypes) - represent information about groups of people (e.g Americans, Egyptians, women, accountants, etc.).$\textcolor{#F04438}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ 

Schemas perform a variety of functions: they organize information in memory, in that, once a schema for school is activated, then this will also activate other schema representations associated with school stereotypes (classroom, teacher,, rules) and scripts for dentist visits (memories for pain associated with homework tasks and punishments etc.).$\textcolor{#F04438}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ These schemas can be activated to increase information processing efficiency because it is much more efficient in terms of mental resources to have a prepared script of what to do in a given situation, which allows for the ability to engage simultaneously in other cognitively demanding activities.$\textcolor{#F04438}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ They generate a set of expectations about objects, events and people and regulate behaviour in accordance with this. They tend to be relatively stable and resistant to change which ensures continuity in the ways we process information and therefore the ways we act.$\textcolor{#F04438}{[\textbf{KT}]}$

In their 1972 study, Bransford and Johnson investigated schema theory by presenting participants with a passage about a routine activity—doing laundry.$\textcolor{#008c3a}{[\textbf{S}]}$ The crucial aspect was the manipulation of context: one group received the title "Laundry Day," while the other received "Moving Day."$\textcolor{#008c3a}{[\textbf{S}]}$ The results showed that participants with the appropriate schema, "Laundry Day," demonstrated better recall and comprehension than the other group. This experiment highlighted the impact of pre-existing mental frameworks, or schemata, on information processing.$\textcolor{#008c3a}{[\textbf{S}]}$ It emphasized that our existing knowledge structures significantly influence how we encode, interpret, and remember new information. Bransford and Johnson's study has been influential in shaping our understanding of how schema contribute to cognitive processes, emphasizing the importance of context and prior knowledge in memory and comprehension.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{L}]}$
