# PS0922

Evaluate one or more models of thinking and decision-making.

[22 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$: 

Models of thinking and decision-making aim to explain how individuals process information and make judgments. The Dual Process Model distinguishes between two systems of thinking: System 1, which is fast, intuitive, and automatic, and System 2, which is slower, more deliberate, and analytical. Furthermore, the model suggests that system 1 uses heuristics (mental shortcuts), whereas system 2 does not. Finally, unlike system 1, system 2 tends to consider alternatives before reaching a final conclusion, making it less prone to error and biased thinking.  $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$  In order to evaluate the Dual Processing Model, it is important to examine its strengths, limitations, and empirical support from studies such as Alter and Oppenheimer (2007) and Englich and Mussweiler (2001). 

Englich and Mussweiler (2001) explored the influence of anchoring (a type of heuristic) on judicial decisions, examining whether a prosecutor's sentence recommendation would influence the judgment of trial judges. In their experiment, judges were presented with a case of alleged rape and asked to recommend a sentence. One group was given a high anchor (a very severe sentence of 34 months), while the other group was given a low anchor (a much milder sentence of 2 months). The study found that judges were significantly influenced by the anchor provided, often recommending sentences closer to the anchor. The results confirmed that judges exposed to the high anchor (34 months) recommended an average sentence of 28.70 months, while those given the low anchor (2 months) recommended an average sentence of 18.78 months. This supports the model by showing how, through the use of heuristics, System 1 thinking can overpower System 2 thinking, leading to biased decision-making. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

It is important to critically evaluate Englich and Mussweiler's supporting study to determine how much support it can provide for the dual processing model. A key strength of the study is its high reliability due to its controlled experimental design. One key feature of this design was the independent samples design, where participants were randomly assigned to either the high-anchor or low-anchor condition, ensuring that the results were not influenced by individual differences. The researchers controlled for the case materials used in both conditions, ensuring that the content of the case was identical for both groups, except for the anchor provided (either 34 months or 2 months). The same questionnaire was given to both groups, ensuring that the participants were asked the same questions in the same format, which makes the study replicable. In addition to this, the findings have strong real-world applications. The study illustrates how anchoring bias can affect decision-making, even in professional contexts like courtrooms. A limitation of the study is its generalisability. The sample was small, consisting of only 19 young trial judges with limited experience (an average of 9.34 months). This raises concerns about whether the findings can be applied to older, more experienced judges who may be less susceptible to cognitive biases. The study's sample was also predominantly male (15 out of 19), which may limit the generalisability to female judges or judges from different cultural backgrounds. $\textcolor{#8c6188}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

Another key study that supports the Dual Process Model is Alter and Oppenheimer (2007), who investigated whether presenting questions in a difficult-to-read font would lead to more correct answers on the Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT). Prior research suggested that disfluent fonts promote System 2 thinking, which is more analytical and deliberate. Based on this, the researchers hypothesised that participants exposed to a hard-to-read font would be more likely to engage System 2 and perform better. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

Forty Princeton undergraduates were randomly assigned to one of two conditions: CRT questions in either an easy-to-read or difficult-to-read font. CRT questions are designed to trigger incorrect answers when individuals rely on quick, intuitive judgments (System 1), rather than reflective thinking (System 2). Results showed that participants provided more correct answers in the difficult-to-read font condition, suggesting that the hard-to-read font activated their rational System 2 thinking, making them less likely to make use of heuristics. However, the participants in the easy-to-read condition likely used heuristics and their intuitive System 1, leading to quicker but incorrect responses. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

This study highlights the different modes of thinking outlined by the Dual Process Model, thus suggesting that the model benefits from having some explanatory power. However, critical evaluation is essential. $\textcolor{#8c6188}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

One limitation is the use of an independent groups design, which introduces potential participant variability. Some participants may have had higher cognitive ability or more experience with CRT questions, reducing internal validity. While all were Princeton students, which implies generally high intelligence, this does not eliminate the risk of prior exposure to similar tasks. Participants were randomly assigned, and the researchers manipulated the IV (font type) while controlling other variables, such as using standardised questions. This qualifies as a true experiment, allowing for cause-and-effect conclusions between font type and performance. However, while the study links font difficulty to more correct answers, it does not directly prove that System 2 thinking was used, as cognitive processes are not directly observable. This highlights a key limitation of the Dual Process Model: many supporting studies infer, rather than demonstrate, support for the theory. $\textcolor{#8c6188}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

It is evident that the Dual Process Model provides a valuable framework for understanding decision-making by clearly distinguishing between System 1 (fast, automatic, intuitive) and System 2 (slow, deliberate, analytical) thinking. This distinction helps to clarify how different cognitive processes influence our judgments and decisions. The model is supported by numerous empirical studies, such as those by Alter and Oppenheimer (2007) and Englich and Mussweiler, which demonstrate how System 1 thinking can lead to biases and errors in thinking. However, the model has notable weaknesses. It can be overly simplistic, as human cognition is more complex and sometimes decisions involve a blend of both systems, rather than acting independently. Additionally, many of the studies supporting the model are conducted in controlled laboratory settings, which may not fully reflect real-world decision-making and raise concerns about the ecological validity of the findings. Moreover, the boundaries between System 1 and System 2 are not always clear,  and some decisions may incorporate both intuitive and analytical elements, complicating the classification of cognitive processes into just two systems. $\textcolor{#8c6188}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

Overall, while the Dual Process Model provides useful insights into how we make decisions, this approach ignores how complex thinking really is and how different mental processes can work together. Therefore, a more holistic perspective is needed to better capture the full range of factors that influence decision-making in real-world contexts.

$\textcolor{#C34A2C}{\underline{\textrm{Possible ideas \& key terms:}}}$

 - Thinking: The process of using information and mental representations to make sense of the world, solve problems, or make decisions.

 - Decision-making: The process of choosing between alternatives, often involving reasoning and judgment.

 - Dual Process Model: A model suggesting two types of thinking: System 1: Fast, intuitive, automatic, and based on heuristics. System 2: Slow, analytical, deliberate, and logical.

 - Heuristics: Mental shortcuts used in System 1 that often lead to cognitive biases.

 - Anchoring Bias: The tendency to rely heavily on the first piece of information offered (the $\text{\textquotedblleft}$anchor”) when making decisions.

$\textcolor{#8c6188}{\underline{\textrm{Possible critical thinking points:}}}$

 - Generalisability: Many supporting studies, such as Alter and Oppenheimer (2007) and Englich & Mussweiler (2001), were conducted with specific populations (*e.g., Princeton University students, inexperienced judges*). This limits generalisability to broader populations, particularly across cultures, ages, and levels of expertise.

 - Reliability: The model is supported by highly replicable, controlled lab experiments. 

 - Applicability: The model has strong real-world relevance, especially in understanding biases in fields like law, finance, and healthcare.

 - Validity: Internal validity is strong due to the controlled nature of studies (*e.g., random assignment, manipulation of independent variables*). However, ecological validity is often low because tasks do not reflect natural decision-making contexts.

 - Reductionist: The model may also be considered reductionist, as it oversimplifies thinking into two discrete systems and doesn't account for how they may interact or blend.

$\textcolor{#056608}{\underline{\textrm{Possible key studies:}}}$

 - Englich & Mussweiler (2001) - Anchoring bias in judicial sentencing. Judges were influenced by arbitrary sentencing recommendations, showing how System 1 overrides System 2.

 - Tversky & Kahneman (1974) - Anchoring in numeric estimation tasks. Participants gave biased estimates based on starting values (*e.g., ascending vs descending multiplication*).

 - Tversky & Kahneman (1986) - Framing effect: how positive vs. negative presentation influences decisions.

 - Alter and Oppenheimer (2007) - Difficult-to-read font can activate System 2 thinking, resulting in greater accuracy in thinking and decision-making.
