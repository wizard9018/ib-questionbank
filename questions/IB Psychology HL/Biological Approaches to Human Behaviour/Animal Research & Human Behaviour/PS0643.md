# PS0643

Discuss one or more ethical considerations in animal research.

[22 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\textcolor{#000000}{\textbf{Criterion A: Focus on the question}\ }$

**2 marks** if the issue or problem raised in the question iss explained. The question uses the command term discuss, paired with the ethical considerations question stem; therefore, the response should present a balanced discussion regarding the use of one or more ethical considerations (*e.g., the right to withdraw, anonymity/confidentiality, minimisation of harm*) when producing knowledge regarding the topic.

**1 mark** if the question is not accurately explained, has limited explanation,  is simply restated, or is misinterpreted, though still related to ethical considerations (*e.g., This essay will evaluate ethical considerations in the study of topics in the biological approach; or the candidate writes in their introduction that they will discuss ethical considerations but either does not define them at any point or discusses different ethical considerations than were described in the introduction*). No marks will be awarded for this criterion if the answer is not relevant to the question.

[2 marks]

$\textcolor{#F04438}{\textbf{Criterion B: Knowledge \& understanding}\ }$

**5-6 marks** if relevant and appropriate knowledge and terminology related to ethical considerations in the context of animal research are explained in detail without errors and are explicitly linked to the studies in Criterion C (*e.g., reduction, refinement, replacement, transgenic mice, autopsy, etc.*).

**3-4 marks** if the relevant terminology is explained and has minimal errors but is lacking in some detail or shows only knowledge about research methods, such as a brief explanation of the topic, with little focus on evaluation of study (*e.g., response primarily demonstrates sustained knowledge of animal research in neglect of the ethical considerations*).

**1-2 marks** if the relevant terminology has errors and/or is limited or superficial in nature (*e.g., incorrectly describing confidentiality*) and if the answer does not demonstrate knowledge and understanding of animal research. No marks will be awarded for this criterion if no relevant terminology is explained.

**5-6 marks** if one or more relevant studies are explained and used to support the answer or the arguments stated, and a specific link is made between the findings of the study(ies) and the question. Relevant details, including the aim, procedure, results, and conclusion, from one or more studies, should be included. Studies that could be referred to include, but are not limited to:
- An investigation on the use of transgenic mice to study MAOA: Cases et al. (1995)
- A study on the role of acetylcholine and spatial navigation: Rogers & Kesner (2003)
- A study on cortisol and long-term health outcomes of stress: Sapolsky (1990, 2005)
- A study investigating the role of glucocorticoids on memory: Meaney et al. (1988)

**3-4 marks** if a relevant study is explained and used to develop arguments but with some errors (*e.g., the aim, procedure, results, conclusions of the study are not clearly presented, or there are some errors with regard to the relevance of the findings or the details of the methodology*).

**1-2 marks** if limited relevant research is used with insufficient detail, errors, or superficial links to the question (*e.g., the study was not linked to the discussion of ethical considerations*). No marks will be awarded for this criterion if the answer does not include any relevant studies.

**5-6 marks** if the answer shows relevant, consistent, and well-developed critical thinkingpoints. Critical thinking points could include a combination of the following: strengths and limitations of the research methods used in supporting research studies and their conclusions, strengths and limitations of the relevant theories, potential applications of the findings, or an acknowledgment of complexities and alternative arguments. At least 2-3 discussion or critical thinking points are explained regarding ethical considerations.

**3-4 marks**  if relevant critical thinking points are presented (as above), but they are not always fully developed. They may not be directly linked to the specific study or theory, or they may be limited in number. For example, a response might state that the study is low in internal validity without explaining what this means in reference to the specific study. At least 1-2 discussion or critical thinking points are explained.

**1-2 marks** if evaluation points are superficial or contain inaccuracies (*e.g., evaluation points only stated but not explained properly*). No marks will be awarded for this criterion if the answer does not contain relevant critical thinking points, discussion, or evaluation.

$\textcolor{#999999}{\textbf{Criterion E: Clarity \& organization}\ }$

**1 mark** if organisation and clarity are not sustained throughout the answer (*e.g. unfinished response, lack of a clear introduction, lack of body paragraphs, and/or lack of a conclusion; a structure that is not logical or easy to follow; or incomplete description of key terms and studies*). $\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$: \
Animal research is frequently proposed as an ethical alternative to human research. However, there are still significant ethical concerns in studies involving living creatures. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ Supporters of animal research say that there is a utilitarian benefit: so long as research leads to knowledge or solutions that could help people, we should minimize harm as much as possible but accept that it may occur. Those who reject this argument say this is fundamentally an anthropocentric—or human-centric—approach. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Animals are living things that can feel pain and experience social bonds. Without the ability to gain animal consent, proponents of this argument say we should not use them in research to benefit humans. As a form of compromise for these arguments, psychological research involving animals is governed by principles referred to as the three Rs: replacement, reduction, and refinement. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$

One reason animals are desirable for research is because they have shorter lifespans and can demonstrate the effects of environmental factors sooner than humans. Unlike with humans, we can also exert total control over animals' environments. One example of a study where this was used to understand the impact of stress and adverse childhood experiences is Meaney et al. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ Meaney and his team weaned a litter of rats from the same mother and randomly allocated them to 2 conditions. In 1, researchers groomed them with a brush. In the other, they were not groomed. When the rats reached 2 years of age, they were put into a pool of milky water, making it difficult to see a platform just below the surface of the water. The groomed mice took a more direct route to the platform, whereas the ungroomed mice looped back on their own path and had a less systematic approach to finding the platform. Researchers hypothesized that this was due to their higher levels of glucocorticoids, or stress hormones. Without grooming, they failed to express genes that helped regulate stress. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ Further, their overall greater exposure to cortisol led to overstimulation and, eventually, hippocampal cell death. This explains the poor maze completion of the mice in this condition, as the hippocampus in mice also dictates spatial navigation. When the mice were euthanized and autopsied, Meaney and his team were able to confirm the reduced volume of the hippocampus. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

Cases et al. genetically engineered knockout mice to study the potential role of the MAOA gene in aggressive behaviour. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ Knockout mice have a gene of interest deleted, and researchers observe their behaviour to infer the role of that gene. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$These studies can yield important findings in genetics, but the lifespan and quality of life of the animals can be severely reduced. In this study, the underproduction of MAOA led the mice to have higher-than-normal average levels of serotonin and norepinephrine. After 11 days, the mice began to display violent shaking, aggressive behaviour when handled, and biting. Using a $\text{\textquotedblleft}$
resident-intruder$\text{\textquotedblright}$
protocol, researchers introduced a control mouse into the cage with one of the MAOA knockout mice. The knockout mice would display heightened aggressive behaviour, even when introduced to control mice of the opposite sex. After being euthanised, the mice were autopsied so neurotransmitter levels could be measured. Serotonin levels were six to nine times higher in the knockout mice than in the control mice. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ The study helped researchers rule out the role of social learning or environmental factors in being a primary cause of aggressive behaviour in low MAOA mice and humans. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

Both studies were conducted relatively early in the field, and so options for replacement were developed after understanding their initial findings. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Replacement asks researchers to find alternate models or approaches to spare animals from experimentation or harm. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$This could involve using computer models to simulate the impact or interaction of multiple genetic mutations, neural network changes, or hormonal changes. However, such models cannot be developed without any direct experimentation. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Modern advancements also allow for experimentation on cell cultures. To have the most ethical study possible today, both Cases and Meaney would be expected to minimise the number of mice involved to yield as much data as possible. This is the principle of reduction. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Sharing their complete datasets after their research could also reduce the number of mice involved in similar studies, as other teams could run their own analyses using the original data. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$  Finally, refinement asks the researchers to consider how they can improve their procedures to reduce pain and provide better living conditions and care for the animals involved. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Variables unrelated to the study should be maximized. For example, in both Meaney and Cases, the mice should have been provided clean and spacious cages and a high-quality diet as control variables across all conditions. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Further, the process for euthanization should be as painless as possible.

Ultimately, cost-benefit analyses should be performed before and after animal studies to assess whether similar veins of research should be carried out. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$  Both Meaney and Cases contributed substantial information that guided our collective understanding of the relationship between genes, environment, and behaviour. Despite this, it is impossible to know the results of a study ahead of time. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ We did not understand the relevance to epigenetics that Meaney's study had until several years after the study was carried out. Further, the cost-benefit analysis of animal research is complicated by the relative social complexity of humans. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ In studies of behaviour that are moderated by social relationships (like aggression), it is challenging to determine the extent to which findings from animal research also apply to humans. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Striving to improve our ability to replace, refine, and reduce animal use in research is likely the best way to mitigate concerns while also carrying out research that cannot be done on humans. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$
