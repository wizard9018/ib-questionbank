# PS0784

Rogers & Kesner (2003) studied the role of Acetylcholine in memory consolidation. They found that rats injected with Scopolamine, which blocks Acetylcholine, took longer and made more errors in learning a maze. However, Scopolamine did not affect memory retrieval. These results suggest that Acetylcholine is important for encoding new memories but not for retrieving them.

Discuss two strengths and two limitations of Roger & Kesner's (2003) study. 

[8 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:

**Strengths may include but are not limited to the following:**

**High reliability**\

The study used a controlled experimental design, including a placebo group, allowing for replication and consistency in results. (:span$\color{red}\checkmark$) This means that the study's procedure can be repeated in other labs. (:span$\color{red}\checkmark$)

**Internal validity (cause-and-effect relationship)**\

The use of a controlled environment and random assignment of rats to the treatment and control groups help ensure that the manipulation of acetylcholine levels caused the observed effects on memory and not other confounding variables. (:span$\color{red}\checkmark$) This enhances the internal validity of the study, making the results more convincing in establishing a direct cause-and-effect relationship between acetylcholine and memory. (:span$\color{red}\checkmark$)

**Applicability (Alzheimer's Disease Research)**\

The findings are relevant to real-world issues, particularly in understanding memory impairments like those seen in Alzheimer's disease, where acetylcholine levels are known to be low. (:span$\color{red}\checkmark$) This means the study could have practical applications in future treatments or interventions for cognitive impairments, making it more applicable to human health. (:span$\color{red}\checkmark$)

**High Control**\

The study controlled for extraneous variables, such as the rats' prior experiences and environments, allowing for a clearer understanding of the role of acetylcholine. (:span$\color{red}\checkmark$) This control increases the generalizability of the findings to other experiments testing the effects of acetylcholine on memory. (:span$\color{red}\checkmark$)

**Limitations may include but are not limited to the following:**

**Ecological validity (artificial task)**\

The memory task, where rats had to swim to find a platform, is artificial and does not closely resemble real-life memory tasks humans encounter. (:span$\color{red}\checkmark$) This reduces the ecological validity of the study, meaning the findings may not translate well to everyday memory processes in humans. (:span$\color{red}\checkmark$)

**Ethical concerns (use of animals)**\

The study involved using rats, which were subjected to separation from their mothers, handled by researchers, and euthanasia at the end of the experiment. (:span$\color{red}\checkmark$) These procedures raise ethical concerns about the well-being of the animals, which is a critical consideration in animal research. (:span$\color{red}\checkmark$) 

**Lack of generalisability to humans**\

Although rats are often used as animal models for human behaviour, there are significant differences between the physiology of rats and humans. (:span$\color{red}\checkmark$) This limits the generalisability of the findings, as it is uncertain whether the role of acetylcholine in rats will apply in the same way to humans, especially in the context of complex human memory. (:span$\color{red}\checkmark$)

**Reductionist approach**\

The study only looked at acetylcholine and its impact on spatial memory, ignoring other factors that could influence memory, such as emotion, other neurotransmitters, or environmental influences. (:span$\color{red}\checkmark$) This reductionist approach oversimplifies memory processes and does not provide a comprehensive understanding of how memory works in a complex, real-world context. (:span$\color{red}\checkmark$)
