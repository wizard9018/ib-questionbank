# PS0933

Discuss the role of animal research in investigating hormones and/or pheromones and behaviour.

[22 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$: 

Animal research plays a crucial role in the study of biological processes, including the influence of hormones on behaviour. A key justification for using animals in research is that many species share significant physiological similarities with humans, enabling researchers to draw meaningful insights into human behaviour from animal studies. Furthermore, animal research allows for controlled experiments and invasive procedures that may otherwise be unethical or impractical to conduct with human participants. This essay will discuss the role of animal research in understanding how hormones influence behaviour, focusing on a study conducted by Meaney et al. (1988) and Friedman's discovery of the hormone leptin with reference to research by Zhang et al. (1994). $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$

The aim of Meaney et al. (1988) was to investigate how stress hormones, called glucocorticoids, affect memory in rats. The researchers focused on how early maternal care, such as grooming, could influence how rats respond to stress later in life. The procedure included two groups of newborn rats, which included the treatment group and the control group. In the treatment group, the baby rats were separated from their mothers for 15 minutes each day over a period of three weeks. During this time, the researchers gently brushed them to imitate the natural grooming a mother rat would provide. This simulated care was meant to mimic the comforting touch a mother gives. In contrast, the control group was also separated from their mothers for 15 minutes each day, but did not receive any brushing or grooming from the researchers. As the rats aged, they were tested in a water maze to assess their memory. The task involved finding a hidden platform in a pool of milky water. The results showed that rats from the control group, who had not received grooming, had higher levels of stress hormones and performed worse in the memory test. They took longer, less direct routes to find the platform. These rats also showed damage in the hippocampus, a part of the brain important for memory. On the other hand, the treatment group had lower cortisol levels, healthier brain structure, and performed better in the maze. This suggests that early nurturing experiences can impact how stress-related genes are expressed, which in turn influences memory and behaviour. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

One strength of using animals in this study was that it allowed for a highly controlled laboratory setting, where the procedure (such as the timing and method of grooming) was standardised. This level of control would not be possible with human participants because of ethical and practical concerns. Another strength is that rats share some physiological similarities with humans, especially in hormonal systems, meaning the study can offer useful insights into how early experiences might influence stress regulation and memory in humans. However, the artificial lab environment and the researcher-administered grooming do not reflect real-life conditions, where natural mother-infant interactions are very different. This makes it harder to apply the results to real-world human situations. In addition to this, rats do not have the same cognitive and emotional abilities as humans. Therefore, they may not respond the same way as humans in these situations.  $\textcolor{#8c6188}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

Friedman's discovery of leptin, a hormone that helps control hunger and body weight, was made possible through animal research involving genetically modified mice. The aim of the study was to understand the biological causes of obesity and how the body signals when it has enough energy stored. The study focused on a special type of mouse called the ob/ob mouse, which is born with a mutation in the ob gene. This mutation means the mice cannot produce leptin, the hormone that normally tells the brain to stop eating. Because of this, ob/ob mice constantly feel hungry, overeat, and become severely obese, even when fed the same food as normal mice. To investigate further, Friedman used a method where two mice are surgically joined so they share a blood supply. When an ob/ob mouse was joined to a healthy mouse, the ob/ob mouse began to eat less and lose weight. This suggested that something in the normal mouse's blood, later identified as leptin, was missing in the obese mouse. To confirm this, researchers injected leptin into the ob/ob mice, and found that their appetite decreased, their activity increased, and they lost weight. The study showed that leptin is a key hormone involved in appetite regulation and in controlling eating behaviour. This was a major breakthrough in understanding the link between hormones and behaviour, and it provided a biological explanation for obesity. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

The study has strong applicability, as it led to further research into how hormones influence eating behaviour. It also helped in gaining a deeper understanding of the biological factors behind obesity and laid the foundations for follow-up research with humans. However, the ecological validity of the study is limited. The conditions under which the mice were tested (such as surgical procedures and hormone injections) do not represent how eating behaviour works in everyday life, where psychological and social factors also play a role. This highlights a potential limitation of using animals in this area of research, as it restricts the ability to explore underlying influences on food choices through follow-up qualitative questions. $\textcolor{#8c6188}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

Both studies show how animal research can help uncover the role of hormones in behaviour, but there are important ethical and biological concerns. Animal studies often involve procedures that would be considered unacceptable in human research, such as surgical operations, hormone injections, or euthanasia. While such methods are sometimes necessary in animal research, they raise ethical concerns about animal welfare and whether the potential benefits to humans justify the harm to animals. There are also important differences between animals and humans. While hormones like cortisol and leptin function similarly across species, human behaviour is more complex and influenced by culture, emotion, and sociocultural factors. $\textcolor{#8c6188}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

Overall, animal research continues to be a valuable tool in exploring the role of hormones in behaviour. Studies such as Meaney et al. (1988) and Friedman's research on leptin show how hormones like cortisol and leptin affect memory, stress regulation, and eating behaviour. However, both studies are limited by the fact that there are important differences between animals and humans in terms of physiology and cognition. Other limitations include low ecological validity, ethical concerns, and issues with generalisability. Despite this, these studies have made significant contributions and discoveries that may not have been possible with humans.

 - Hormones: Chemical messengers that influence behaviour (e.g., cortisol, leptin)

 - Glucocorticoids: Stress hormones (e.g., cortisol) involved in memory and neurodevelopment

 - Ob/ob mice: Genetically modified mice lacking leptin; used to study obesity

 - Leptin: A hormone produced by fat cells that signals the brain to reduce appetite and regulate energy balance

$\textcolor{#8c6188}{\underline{\textrm{Possible critical thinking points:}}}$

 - Psychological similarities: Many species share significant physiological similarities with humans, enabling researchers to draw meaningful insights into human behaviour from animal studies. 

 - Generalisability: Differences in brain structure, cognition, and social behaviour can make it challenging to apply results from animal studies to humans.

 - Lack of in-depth understanding: Using animals removes the potential to ask follow-up qualitative questions to gain a deeper understanding.

 - Reliability: Meaney's study is highly reliable due to its controlled lab setting and standardised, replicable procedures.

 - Application: Friedman's study on Leptin has  strong applicability, as it led to further research into how hormones influence eating behaviour.

 - Ecological validity: The tightly controlled conditions that are possible in animal studies may mean that the findings no longer replicate real-world settings.

 - Ethical concerns, including stress and physical harm: animal studies often involve procedures that would be considered unacceptable in human research, such as surgical operations, hormone injections, or euthanasia.

$\textcolor{#056608}{\underline{\textrm{Possible key studies:}}}$

 - Meaney et al. (1988): Examined how early handling affected stress hormone regulation and memory in rats, showing that early experiences influence cognitive function through hormones.

 - Zhang et al. (1994): Identified the obese gene in mice and its human counterpart, revealing leptin's role in appetite regulation and obesity.
