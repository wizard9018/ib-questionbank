$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:\
Pheromones are heavily disputed in the scientific community for many reasons. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ Pheromones, in non-human species, are chemicals secreted or emitted outside of an organism’s body that other members of that species detect. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Pheromones can serve a wide range of communicative purposes in animals: to share information about health, mating availability, or fear. But the existence and function of pheromones in humans is widely contested, in part because there is a lack of convincing research to support its existence in humans. However, there may be some limited biological evidence for pheromones in humans. Two studies that highlight these areas of discussion are Zhou et al and Wedekind et al, both of which sought to identify or test the role of potential human pheromones in behaviors related to sexual selection. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$

Zhou et al explored two specific chemical substances: estratetraenol (EST) and androstadienone (AND). $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ Both of these substances potentially communicate information about the sex status of members of another species, and if they could reliably produce a specific behavior or perception, they could be considered human pheromones. EST is produced by women, and AND is produced by men. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Using a diverse sample involving both heterosexual and homosexual men and women, Zhou et al wanted to understand whether or not being exposed to these substances would influence the perception of gender of androgynous stick figures. In a double-blind study, each participant was administered a dose of either AND, EST (which was mixed with cloves to hide any potential unpleasant or detectable smell of the chemical), or a control also mixed with cloves, asked to view the stick figure’s motion on the screen, and then asked to report whether the figure was more masculine (male) or feminine (female). Researchers found that heterosexual women and homosexual men were more likely to perceive the sex of the stick figure as male when they had been exposed to AND, and heterosexual men were more likely to perceive the stick figure as female when exposed to EST. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ The results of this study could suggest that these substances communicate important information to members of our species who experience sexual and/or romantic attraction to members of our biological sex. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$

Unlike Zhou, Wedekind does not attempt to isolate a specific pheromone, but suggests that there is some information in our body odor that somewhat reliably communicates about a potential partner’s “genetic fit.” $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ Researchers hypothesized that because there is an evolutionary advantage to mating with people with varied immune system genetics, referred to as major histocompatibility complexes (MHCs), that women would find the scents of men with relatively different MHCs more attractive. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Also a lab experiment, researchers asked men with varying MHC compositions to wear a plain shirt for multiple days while avoiding the use of artificial scents or deodorants. These t-shirts were then laid in cardboard boxes, and women not on any form of contraception were asked to smell a set of seven of these t-shirts. Approximately half of these shirts belonged to men with very different MHCs than them, and half were similar. One was an unworn control. The women were asked to rate the attractiveness, pleasantness, and sexiness of each shirt’s smell. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ The study found that women tended to rate shirts worn by men with different MHCs as more pleasant than the shirts worn by men with more similar MHCs. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$  Because producing offspring with a partner with different MHCs produces children with higher birth weight and other health indicators that favor survival, it could be the case that this advantageous preference is something that has been naturally selected for over time in humans. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ While Wedekind does not isolate a specific chemical, it could support the idea that there is something olfactory that is being secreted and detected by humans which carries meaning to other members of our species. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$

Overall, a combination of challenges with research and also realistic use of pheromones make it difficult to assert that they play a significant role in human behavior. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ Experimental studies like Zhou et al and Wedekind use rigorous controls to eliminate confounding variables and allow us to conclude that the pheromone is what is causing the change in each study’s dependent variable. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$  This strength actually also serves as a limitation: for pheromones to exist in humans, we must see that they are reliably used by all members of a species to communicate information in everyday environments. In Wedekind, the t-shirts accumulated scents over multiple days without the use of perfumed detergents or soaps. This is not typical. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$  In Zhou et al, the concentrations of AND and EST were magnitudes higher than what would ever be secreted by one person on one day. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

Humans manipulate their own scents, and nearly all cultures have hygienic practices that use perfumes and other artificial scents to enhance our desirability to prospective partners. In other words, we would not be smelling and making mating choices based on pheromones, but on perfumes. In addition, the organ that mammals use to sense pheromones – the vomeronasal organ – is vestigial in most humans, with no active sensory neurons associated with this region of the nose. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ This means we would not be able to identify how humans can sense pheromones without identifying an alternative biological mechanism that is unique to humans, which is unlikely. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$  Finally, the populations of these studies tend to be Western – which, keeping in mind that some human populations lack the gene that produces body odor, threatens the assumptions of studies such as Wedekind that pheromones may be both released and understood by all members of a species.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

Overall, there seem to be many difficulties in presuming the use of pheromones to influence mating behavior in humans, ranging from poor generalizability to real world scenarios to biological practicalities of sensing pheromones from other members of our species.$\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$
