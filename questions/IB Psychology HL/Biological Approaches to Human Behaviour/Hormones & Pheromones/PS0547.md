# PS0547

Discuss the role of hormones on behaviour or cognition.
[22 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:\
Hormones are chemicals which are released and regulated by the endocrine system. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$  They travel through our bloodstream to target cells, and when it makes a connection with a receptor site on those cells they either increase or decrease its function. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$  Some hormones, like testosterone and adrenaline, can have impacts on brain function. In contrast to neurotransmitters, however, hormones can take longer to produce changes in behaviour and cognition. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Testosterone facilitates aggressive behaviour and also correlates with risk seeking behaviour because of its effect of impairing prefrontal cortex activity. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Adrenaline is linked to the fight or flight response and can activate the amygdala. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Overall, these two hormones present examples of how hormones can impact both behaviour and cognitive processes. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$

Ronay and von Hippel wanted to understand the relationship between testosterone and risk-seeking behaviours. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ Ronay and von Hippel recruited 96 young adult, male skateboarders at a park. The researchers asked the participants to do one easy trick and one difficult trick, which they could reliably complete about half of the time. They were asked to repeat each trick 10 times. After a short break, they were asked to do the same again but this time in front of either the same male researcher that had recorded observations of their tricks in the first round, or an attractive female researcher. For the same tricks, the researchers recorded the number of times they were successful at landing their difficult trick, how many times they bailed out before attempting, and how many times they had a crash landing. High rates of bailing out from the trick early correlates with low risk-taking, and high rates of crash landing correlates with high risk-taking. Salivary testosterone was measured to understand the role testosterone may play in risk-taking. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

The researchers found that participants were more willing to take risks and follow through with their difficult trick when they were in front of the attractive female researcher. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ Also, testosterone in each participants' saliva was measured, and it was greater in the group which was in front of the female researcher. The researchers suggested that testosterone impacts the function of the prefrontal cortex, a region of the brain associated with higher order decision-making. It could be that this has developed as an evolutionary mechanism: upon seeing a suitable partner, testosterone is released and as a cascading series of responses throughout the brain and body, the part of the brain responsible for telling us to not take risks is impaired. This can reduce fear and second-guessing and may have been selected for overtime as risk-taking can allow a male to demonstrate their physical fitness and competitiveness. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

This was a well controlled study that took place in naturalistic settings, improving the external validity of the study. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ There are some limitations to this study that interfere with our ability to understand the role of testosterone, however. Most critically, it is difficult to standardize the selection of difficult tricks. It is unclear if all of the tricks were of a similar level of difficulty for each participant. If participants selected tricks that were either too difficult to complete or too easy, the biological response to the task may be altered. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$  Additional participant characteristics, such as testosterone level differences among participants, may also interfere with the findings. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Random allocation was used, and there was a pretest posttest design allowing for participants to be measured both with an unattractive and attractive researcher present. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ This control, however, leads to potential concerns with order effects, like fatigue. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

An example of a hormone's impact on cognition is adrenaline's impact on emotional learning, specifically related to flashbulb memory creation. McGaugh and Cahill explored the role of adrenaline and the flight-or-fight response in the creation of flashbulb memories. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ In part one of their study, they randomly allocated participants to two conditions: one saw 12 slides and heard a visceral story about a car accident in which a boy had his limbs severed. The participants heard many details about his trip to the hospital and the work of surgeons who reattached his limbs. In the other condition, participants heard a comparatively dull story about a family who came to visit someone in a hospital and witnessed a safety drill while there. The first condition was designed to create a greater emotional experience, whereas the latter was not. Researchers hypothesized that memory would be greater in the first condition. Sure enough, participants both rated the vivid, shocking story as more emotional, and recalled more details from the story and slides than the mundane story condition. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

In a follow up study, researchers administered propranolol, a beta blocker that blocks the target cells for the hormone adrenaline. When the study was repeated as a 2x2 factorial design (one level being the propranolol, the other being the intensity of the story), the condition which heard the high-emotion story and was administered the beta blocker recalled information at the same rate and accuracy as participants in the mundane story conditions. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

This suggests that adrenaline, which is a stress hormone that activates the amygdala, plays an important role in creating memories from emotionally arousing events, such as flashbulb memories. Because the amygdala is associated with emotional learning, blocking activation of this brain region reduces learning to the same rate as ‘safe,' ordinary events. The use of a true experiment allows us to understand this cause-and-effect relationship between adrenaline, amygdala activation, and flashbulb memories. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ The study is highly replicable as well, and so it's possible to establish the reliability of these findings. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Yet, a tradeoff of this study is that the high control over variables reduces our ability to presume that this is what we would observe in real life, outside of the confines of a lab. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

Overall, both studies demonstrate the impacts of hormones on both behaviour and cognition. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$
