$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:\

If humans were not able to adapt to the world around them, survival would be impossible. As such, we have developed evolutionary behaviours that ensure the likelihood of survival; this is called natural selection $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$. In particular, sexual selection is the notion that the human species looks for traits in their partners that are likely to engender reproductive success and survival of future generations. Intrasexual selection is when individuals of the same sex compete with each other for access to mates $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$, whereas intersexual selection is when individuals choose a mate based on desirable traits, such as strong shoulders as a sign of strength in men, or wide hips that convey suitability for child rearing $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$. While there is empirical evidence to support the existence of sexual selection, this is just one factor that explains mate selection, it would be reductive not to acknowledge the importance of both sociocultural and cognitive factors $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$. Evolutionary explanations of behaviour, in terms of mate choice, and competing with others to win potential mates, can be discussed with reference to research conducted by Wedekind (1995) and Ronay and von Hippel (2010). $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$

Wedekind (1995) wanted to determine whether someone's major histocompatibility complex (MHC) gene would affect their mate choice $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$.  MHC genes play a major role in the immune system in that they release molecules that help us to recognise pathogens and psychologists argue that we are attracted by smell to those with different MHC genes to ensure the strength of the immune system in our offspring. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ In this quasi-experiment, 49 male students and 44 female students were asked to take part. The men were asked to wear a t-shirt for two days, taking care not to wear deodorant or eat spicy food. The women were then asked to rank the smell of the t-shirts in a cardboard box with a smelling hole: three of the seven boxes contained T-shirts from men with MHC similar to the woman's own; three contained T-shirts from MHC dissimilar men; and one contained an unworn T-shirt as a control. Results demonstrated that women scored male body odours as more pleasant when they differed from their own MHC than when they were more similar. It can, therefore, be concluded that the MHC genes of a potential mate affect our mate choice, supporting the evolutionary notion that mates are chosen to engender reproductive success [S]$\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$.

Wedekind's (1995) study was well-controlled, with researchers enacting control measures to ensure that other smells were not affecting the judgement of the female participants $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. The researchers took care not to bias the women's decision by putting each t-shirt in a box, so they were unaware of the characteristics of the wearer, protecting the integrity of the results $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. As the researchers could not manipulate the genetic make-up of the participants, this was not a true experiment and no strong cause-and-effect relationship can be established. Despite confirmation of the original hypothesis, it is quite limiting to consider that the decision about pursuing a mate is solely dependent on smell; there are other factors that play a role in the decision-making process $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. In a real-life setting, the MHC scent may be masked by other smells, and so perhaps the same results would not be obtainable in a natural environment $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$.

According to intrasexual selection, individuals of the same sex will compete with each other to win the attention of potential mates. Ronay and von Hippel (2010) aimed to explore whether males would engage in increasingly risky behaviour in front of an attractive female$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. 96 Australian male skateboarders were asked to do a trick they could complete 50$\%$ of the time in front of a middle-aged male researcher, and then in front of an attractive 18-year old researcher who was blind to the aim of the study. Saliva samples were also collected to measure the testosterone levels of the skateboarders. Results demonstrated that greater risks were taken with the female researcher, in that the skateboarders pursued the trick more times, even after bad execution. The skaters also demonstrated higher levels of testosterone after performing in front of the female researcher, showing that there is a biological drive for risk-taking in the presence of a potential mate. Conclusions of this experiment indicate the level of competitiveness among the skateboarders as a need to demonstrate attractive traits to the female researchers, such as strength, skill and dominance.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ This highlights the evolutionary concept of intrasexual selection, where individuals of the same sex will compete with each other to win the attention of potential mates, thus increasing the likelihood of reproductive success. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$. 

Ronay and von Hippel's (2010) study is well-controlled and had a test and experimental condition to show how the skateboarders would perform under $\text{\textquotedblleft}$normal" conditions, and how this changed when an attractive female was present; the researchers were able to isolate this variable $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. The conditions of the experiment were fairly natural and at an outdoor skatepark, although ecological validity may have been affected by having researchers present. This study also made use of method triangulation in testing for testosterone levels as well as assessing risky behaviour, further strengthening the validity of the results $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. While it is clear that the female researcher had an effect on the behaviour of the skateboarders, it is difficult to say whether this behaviour is linked to reproduction, as a goal of attracting the female's attention $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. 

In conclusion, evolutionary psychology determines that humans have genetically adapted to their environment in ways that ensure we have the best characteristics which are then passed down the generations $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$. Evidence available in the field supports the notion that we have biological mechanisms such as a drive for genetically dissimilar sexual partners, or a surge of hormones in a situation where we are confronted with a potential partner, which kickstarts behaviours that reveal our desirable traits to the opposite sex $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. The evidence, then, supports the idea of sexual selection as an evolutionary behaviour. A great deal of research in this area is done in a laboratory setting, which leads to high control and protection against extraneous variables. However, as these studies are based on evolutionary arguments, their premises are not falsifiable, and we cannot make strong claims in terms of cause and effect. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. Furthermore, human beings are complex and make decisions based on a variety of factors; it is somewhat reductive to reduce behaviour to biological mechanisms - humans make conscious decisions when selecting a partner and are not governed only by biological drives $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$.
