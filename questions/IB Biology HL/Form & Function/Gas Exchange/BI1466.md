$\underline{\textrm{Sample answer}}$: \

The oxygen dissociation curve of a **tadpole**, positioned to the **left** and **steeper compared to** that of an **adult frog** ($\checkmark$), indicates a **higher oxygen affinity** in **tadpole haemoglobin** ($\checkmark$), essential for **efficient oxygen extraction** in the **aquatic environment** which has a **lower oxygen concentration than air** ($\checkmark$). This adaptation allows **tadpoles** to **bind oxygen more readily** and **release it less readily**, which contrasts with **frog haemoglobin**, which is adapted more for **releasing oxygen** at **tissues**, an adaptation to the **higher oxygen concentration** in **air** ($\checkmark$). When the **pH changes** from **7.1 to 6.8**, typically brought about by increased **carbon dioxide** or **lactic acid concentration** from **increased respiration** rate due to increased activity, the **tadpole's** oxygen dissociation **curve** remains **unchanged**. This indicates **tadpole haemoglobin's reduced sensitivity to pH changes** ($\checkmark$), possibly an adaptation to the relatively **stable levels** of **carbon dioxide** and **oxygen** in **water** ($\checkmark$). Conversely, the **frog's** oxygen dissociation curve **shifts** significantly to the **right** under the same conditions, showing a **strong Bohr effect** ($\checkmark$). This enables **frogs** to **release oxygen more readily** to their **tissues** during **increased activity** or stress. In conclusion, the tadpole's haemoglobin is optimised for acquiring oxygen in water, while the frog's haemoglobin prioritises oxygen delivery in tissues, reflecting the distinct challenges of their contrasting environments.
