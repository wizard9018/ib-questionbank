Engineered receptors with transmembrane anchors, like the one shown in diagram B, are commonly used in research and therapeutic applications.

$\hspace{1em}$ The **intracellular receptor likely has hydrophobic** ($\checkmark$) amino acids and the **transmembrane receptor likely has hydrophilic amino acids** ($\checkmark$).

2. any **two** of the following:

    $\ce{ -> }$ the receptor would be restricted to the membrane **OR** unable to diffuse freely in the cytoplasm [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ might prevent it from interacting with cytoplasmic signalling molecules **OR** cofactors required for its activity [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ the receptor-ligand complex could not get to the nucleus [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ gene regulation by the receptor would be inhibited    [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ receptor could gain new functionality by interacting with membrane-bound ligands **OR** signalling pathways    [1 mark]
    [2 marks]

    $\underline{\textrm{Sample answer}}$: \
    If the intracellular receptor were anchored into the plasma membrane, it would **not be able to enter the nucleus** ($\checkmark$) to **affect transcription** ($\checkmark$).

3. any **two** of the following:

    $\ce{ -> }$ intracellular receptors signal more slowly **OR** transmembrane receptors act more quickly [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ intracellular receptors require ligand diffusion which is slow    [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ membrane receptor signalling acts through second messengers which is faster than diffusion    [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ intracellular receptors result in a change in gene transcription, which is a slow response    [1 mark]
    [2 marks]

    $\underline{\textrm{Sample answer}}$: \
    **Transmembrane receptor signalling is faster** ($\checkmark$) because they use second messengers, which is a high-speed method of signal amplification, and they do not have to wait for the **ligand to diffuse through the membrane to bind as intracellular receptors do** ($\checkmark$).
