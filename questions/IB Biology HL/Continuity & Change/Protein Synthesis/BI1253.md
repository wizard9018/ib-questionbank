# BI1253

The following graph shows the number of genes suspected to exist in humans based on estimates collected between 1964-2007. The earliest estimate was calculated by comparing the average amino acid length of proteins and the molecular weight of chromosomes in the human body.

Why were initial gene estimates much higher than those collected more recently?

![BI1253](images/BI1253.png)

- A.$\hspace{1em}$ The structure of DNA had not yet been discovered.

- B.$\hspace{1em}$ The presence of introns and other non-coding regions of DNA had not yet been discovered.

- C.$\hspace{1em}$ The structure of proteins had not yet been discovered.

- D.$\hspace{1em}$ The human genome project had not yet begun.

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$: \
Introns were not discovered until 1977, more than 10 years after the initial gene estimate calculated in 1964. Friedrich Vogel made this estimate assuming that all DNA is involved in polypeptide production, which we now know to be false. Thus, **Choice B** is the correct response.

**Choice A** is not correct since Watson and Crick described the structure of DNA in the 1950s. Similarly, **Choice C** is not correct because the earliest protein structures were also discovered in the 1950s. Furthermore, Vogel could not have made his 1964 gene estimate without knowing these two factors, as described in the question. Although it is true that the human genome project occurred much later, it had more to do with sequencing the genome than identifying the total number of genes, and gene estimates had already dropped considerably by this point anyway, so **Choice D** is incorrect.
