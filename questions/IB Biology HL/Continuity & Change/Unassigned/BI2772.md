# BI2772

**Maximum mark: 10**

John Endler experimented on guppies, investigating sexual and natural selection. In one experiment, he raised guppies for six months in tanks with no predators, then transferred them to three different pools. In one pool, no predators were added, in another, a strong predator was added, and in a third, a weak predator was added. Six months later, he collected data on the number and colour of spots on the guppies.

![2772](images/2772.png)

1. Determine which colour showed the greatest decrease in mean number of spots when predators were added. [1 mark]

2. Using the data and your own knowledge, discuss the hypothesis that having spots makes individuals more fit. [3 marks]

3. Suggest with reasons what selection pressures may have led to variation in spot colour in guppies. [2 marks]

A fourth group of guppies was transferred from a pool back into the wild, and the mean number of spots was determined intermittently over 7 months.

![2772b](images/2772b.png)

4. Describe how the mean number of spots changed after the guppies were released into the wild. [2 marks]

5. Suggest reasons for the trend seen in the mean number of spots between 5 and 7 months. [2 marks]

$\hspace{1em}$ The mean number of **blue** spots decreased the most when predators were added ($\checkmark$).

2. any **three** of the following:

 $\ce{ -> }$ in guppies females tend to mate with males that have more spots [1 mark]

 $\ce{ -> }$ males with more spots are more reproductively successful [1 mark]

 $\ce{ -> }$ mean number of red spots did not show a large decrease under predation [1 mark]

 $\ce{ -> }$ having red spots in the presence of predators is adaptive because females may prefer red spots [1 mark]

 $\ce{ -> }$ spots remained for all colours when predators were added suggesting sexual selection may favour having spots even in the presence of predators [1 mark]

 $\ce{ -> }$ predators may not always be present in the wild, so selection pressure from predation may vary [1 mark]

 $\ce{ -> }$ having blue spots decreases fitness in the presence of predators, **OR** having red spots slightly decreases fitness in the presence of predators [1 mark]

 [3 marks]

 $\underline{\textrm{Sample answer}}$: \
 **Having spots** makes males more **reproductively fit** ($\checkmark$) because **females tend to mate** with males with **more spots** ($\checkmark$). On the other hand, **having coloured spots** makes males **more visible to predators**, which would make them **less fit** ($\checkmark$).

3. any **two** of the following:

 ***Sexual selection:***\
 $\ce{ -> }$ different females may prefer different spot colours [1 mark]

 ***Natural selection:***\
 $\ce{ -> }$ different colours may be more **OR** less visible to different predators [1 mark]

 $\ce{ -> }$ different environments **OR** backgrounds **OR** rock colours may influence which colours provide effective camouflage [1 mark]

 [2 marks]

 $\underline{\textrm{Sample answer}}$: \
 If **females prefer different** spot **colours**, then **sexual selection** may sometimes select for a new colour of spot ($\checkmark$). Some spot colours may be **less visible** to different **predators**, so **natural selection** may select for different colours depending on what predator is present ($\checkmark$).

4. any **two** of the following:

 $\ce{ -> }$ mean number of spots decreased until month 5 [1 mark]

 $\ce{ -> }$ between months 5 and 7, the mean number of spots stayed the same [1 mark]

 $\ce{ -> }$ during the first month mean number of spots did not change [1 mark]

 [2 marks]

 $\underline{\textrm{Sample answer}}$: \
 The mean number of **spots decreased** for the **first 5 months** ($\checkmark$) then **stayed the same** between **months 5 and 7** ($\checkmark$).

5. any **two** of the following:

 $\ce{ -> }$ individuals with one spot may have higher fitness than those with no spots or many spots [1 mark]

 $\ce{ -> }$ having one spot does not increase guppy visibility to predators much [1 mark]

 $\ce{ -> }$ although one spot may increase visibility of guppies to predators, the benefit of finding a mate outweighs the risk [1 mark]

 $\ce{ -> }$ individuals with alleles for more spots may have been removed by predation, reducing the frequency of those alleles in the gene pool [1 mark]

 $\ce{ -> }$ natural selection has acted on the population over time, favouring guppies with intermediate spotting [1 mark]

 [2 marks]

 $\underline{\textrm{Sample answer}}$: \
 Having **one spot** makes individual guppies **more fit** than having no spots or many spots ($\checkmark$). Over time, individuals with **more spots** may have been **eaten**, so the **gene pool** now mostly includes **alleles** for **fewer spots** ($\checkmark$). This means the **population trends** toward **intermediate spotting** because it's the **best trade-off** between **avoiding predators** and **attracting mates** ($\checkmark$).
