# BI2881

**Maximum mark: 16**

1. Explain how community vaccination programs contribute to herd immunity but may not result in the eradication of the disease    [4 marks]

2. Outline the features that are common to all viruses.        [5 marks]

3. Describe how mutations in proto-oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes can lead to the formation of a secondary tumour.                        [7 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1. any **four** of the following *(must include at least **one** explanation of herd immunity and **one** statement for why it may not eradicate the disease to gain full marks)*:

    $\ce{ -> }$ herd immunity occurs when enough individuals in a population are immune either via vaccination **OR** previous exposure reducing disease transmission    [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ this reduces chance of unvaccinated people **OR** vulnerable individuals **OR** people within the population who do not have immunity, from getting the disease        [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ herd immunity reduces the basic reproductive number **OR** $\ce{R_0}$ below 1, causing infection rates to decline over time                [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ vaccination disrupts transmission chains, especially for diseases with human-only transmission                [1 mark]

    ***It may not lead to the eradication of the disease if…***

    $\ce{ -> }$ not enough of the population are vaccinated, so the disease is still able to spread between susceptible people        [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ pathogen mutates frequently **OR** antigenic drift, reducing vaccine effectiveness        [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ the immunity provided by the vaccine is relatively short-lived, so even vaccinated people become vulnerable to the disease over time    [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ asymptomatic carriers can transmit  disease to susceptible people, even if carriers have been vaccinated        [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ pathogen has animal reservoirs **OR** zoonotic disease, so it can re-enter the human population        [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ vaccine coverage is uneven geographically, allowing disease to persist in under-vaccinated areas            [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ socioeconomic **OR** political barriers prevent effective vaccine delivery **OR** uptake in some regions                    [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ public vaccine hesitancy **OR** misinformation reduces uptake, keeping immunity levels below the threshold        [1 mark]

    [4 marks]

    $\underline{\textrm{Sample answer}}$: \
    Herd immunity occurs when a  **large enough proportion**  of the population is **immune** to a disease, either via **prior infection** or **vaccination**, which reduces the overall transmission of the pathogen ($\checkmark$). This **indirectly protects** individuals who are **not immune** ($\checkmark$). However, if the level of immunity within the population is **not high enough**, the disease can still **circulate** between **susceptible individuals** ($\checkmark$). Additionally, the **immunity** gained from vaccines may **decline over time**, so without booster doses, even **previously vaccinated** individuals can become **susceptible again** ($\checkmark$).

2. any **five**  of the following:

    $\ce{ -> }$ non-cellular **OR** acellular structure            [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ smaller than bacteria **OR** ultramicroscopic **OR** have a diameter range of 20 - 400 nm    [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ contain DNA **OR** RNA **OR** genetic material        [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ have no cytoplasm **OR** organelles **OR** plasma membranes **OR** ribosomes    [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ no metabolism **OR** do not carry out metabolic processes independently     [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ surrounded by capsid **OR** protein coat        [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ obligate intracellular parasites **OR** reproduction depends on infecting host cells    [1 mark]

    [5 marks]

    $\underline{\textrm{Sample answer}}$: \
    Features common to all viruses include their **acellular structure** ($\checkmark$) and the presence of **either DNA** or **RNA** as genetic material ($\checkmark$). They **lack organelles** and **do not carry out metabolism** ($\checkmark$). Viruses are also **extremely small**, much **smaller than bacteria** ($\checkmark$), and are enclosed in a **protein coat** called a **capsid** ($\checkmark$).

3. any **seven** of the following *(must include at least **one** statement  from each of the three sections to gain full marks)*:

    ***Proto-oncogenes*** \
    $\ce{ -> }$ proto-oncogenes are genes that code for proteins that stimulate normal cell division    [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ Mutations can convert proto-oncogenes into oncogenes, leading to overexpression **OR** increased translation of the proto-oncogenes' active protein **OR** a permanently activated protein that drives continuous cell division                [1 mark]

    ***Tumour suppressor genes***\
    $\ce{ -> }$ tumour suppressor genes encode proteins that inhibit cell division **OR** repair damaged DNA **OR** promote controlled cell death **OR** apoptosis        [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ mutations in tumour suppressor genes result in no **OR** decrease in protein being translated **OR** the translation of an inactive **OR** non-functional protein        [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ resulting in uncontrolled OR unregulated cell division OR tumour formation    [1 mark]

    ***Formation of a secondary tumour***\
    $\ce{ -> }$ a primary tumour forms at the original mutation site    [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ some cells in the primary tumour acquire additional mutations, enabling invasion of local tissues **OR** detachment        [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ cancerous cells from the primary tumour metastasise **OR** move through the bloodstream **OR** lymphatic system to other sites in the body    [1 mark]

    $\ce{ -> }$ these cells can establish **OR** grow into a secondary tumour at a new site        [1 mark]

    [7 marks]

    $\underline{\textrm{Sample answer}}$: \
    **Proto-oncogenes** are normal genes that code for proteins that **promote normal cell division** ($\checkmark$). When proto-oncogenes undergo **mutation**, they can become **oncogenes**, resulting in **increased translation** of the protein and therefore **uncontrolled cell division** ($\checkmark$). In contrast, **tumour suppressor genes** code for proteins that **inhibit cell cycle progression** ($\checkmark$), which can **prevent the translation** of a protein, removing important cell cycle checkpoints ($\checkmark$). Mutations in either tumour suppressor or proto-oncogenes can result in **uncontrolled cell division** ($\checkmark$), forming a **primary tumour** at the **original mutation site** ($\checkmark$). Gradually, some cells within the primary tumour may accumulate **additional mutations**, enabling them to **metastasise** ($\checkmark$) and form a **secondary tumour** in another part of the body ($\checkmark$).
