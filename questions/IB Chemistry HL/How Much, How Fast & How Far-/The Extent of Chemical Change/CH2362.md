## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1. $K_c = \dfrac{[\ce{SO_3}]^2}{[\ce{SO_2}]^2[\ce{O_2}]} \: \checkmark$

    <!-- almost always use \dfrac instead of \frac.  Alway use \: \checkmark when following a math expression -->
    <!-- there are never $$ in a row.-->

    <!-- Further Explanation is used in paper 2 questions while Explanation is used in Paper 1A multiple choice question.-->

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    The equilibrium constant expression places products in the numerator and reactants in the denominator, each raised to the power of its stoichiometric coefficient. For the general equation

    $a \ce{\,A} + b \ce{\,B} \rightleftharpoons c \ce{\,C} + d \ce{\,D}$

    the expression is

    $K_c = \dfrac{[\ce{C}]^c[\ce{D}]^d}{[\ce{A}]^a[\ce{B}]^b}$

    Applying this to the reaction

    $2\ce{\,SO_2}\textrm{(g)} + \ce{O_2}\textrm{(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\ce{\,SO_3}\textrm{(g)} \qquad K_c = \dfrac{[\text{SO}_3]^2}{[\text{SO}_2]^2[\text{O}_2]}$

    **Note:** only species in the gaseous or aqueous phase appear in the expression. Since all species here are gases, all three appear. Square brackets denote equilibrium concentrations in $\text{mol dm}^{-3}$.

2.  1. concentrations of all species remain constant over time $\checkmark$\
        *Accept the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction.*

        <!-- the answers are typically brief, not capitalized, no punctuation -->

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Equilibrium is a dynamic state. Both the forward and reverse reactions continue, but at equal rates. Because the rate of formation of each species exactly matches its rate of consumption, the macroscopic concentrations no longer change. On the graph, this is visible as the horizontal (flat) portions of all three curves. Equilibrium does not mean the reactions have stopped; it means the opposing processes are balanced.

    2.  $K_c = \dfrac{(0.400)^2}{(0.500)^2 \times 0.800} \: \checkmark$\
        $K_c = 0.800 \: \checkmark$

        <!-- The lines in the answer are separated by a \, and then a new line.-->

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Reading the equilibrium concentrations from the graph:

        ![CH2159biims](images/CH2159biims.png)

        $[\text{SO}_3] = 0.400 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}, \quad [\text{SO}_2] = 0.500 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}, \quad [\text{O}_2] = 0.800 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$

        Substituting into the $K_c$ expression:

        $K_c = \dfrac{[\text{SO}_3]^2}{[\text{SO}_2]^2[\text{O}_2]} = \dfrac{(0.400)^2}{(0.500)^2 \times 0.800} = \dfrac{0.160}{0.200} = 0.800$

        Since $K_c < 1$, the equilibrium lies toward the reactants. This is consistent with the graph, where the reactant concentrations at equilibrium are higher than the product concentration.

3. $\mbox{}\\[-25pts]$

    | Change | Effect | Reason |
    |--------|--------|--------|
    | Increasing the volume $\newline$ at constant temperature | Equilibrium shifts to the left (toward reactants) $\checkmark$ | Increasing the volume decreases the total pressure. The system shifts to the side with more moles of gas (3 mol on the left vs 2 mol on the right) to partially restore the pressure. $\checkmark$ |
    | Decreasing the temperature $\newline$ at constant volume | Equilibrium shifts to the right (toward products) $\checkmark$ | A decrease in temperature favours the exothermic direction. The forward reaction is exothermic $(\Delta H < 0),$ so it is favoured. $\checkmark$ |

    <!-- remove the em-dashes -->

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    These are applications of Le $\textrm{Ch\^{a}telier's}$ principle. When an equilibrium system is subjected to a change in conditions, the position of equilibrium shifts to partially counteract that change.

    *Volume increase:* Counting moles of gas on each side: the left$-$hand side has $2 + 1 = 3$ mol of gas, and the right$-$hand side has 2 mol of gas. When the volume increases, the total pressure drops. The system responds by shifting toward the side that produces more moles of gas (the left), which partially restores the pressure. Note that the value of $K_c$ is unchanged, it depends only on temperature, but the equilibrium position changes because the individual concentrations adjust.

    *Temperature decrease:* Temperature is the one factor that changes the value of $K_c$ itself. For an exothermic forward reaction $(\Delta H < 0),$ lowering the temperature shifts equilibrium to the right, increasing $K_c.$ You can think of heat as a product of the exothermic reaction: removing heat (lowering $T)$ drives the equilibrium toward producing more heat, *i.e.* toward products.

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        When $\ce{O_2}$ is added at $t_0$, its concentration jumps instantaneously (shown as a vertical increase on the graph). The system is no longer at equilibrium (the reaction quotient $Q_c$ is momentarily less than $K_c$ because the denominator has increased). To restore equilibrium, the forward reaction is temporarily favoured: $\text{SO}_2$ and $\text{O}_2$ are consumed, and $\text{SO}_3$ is produced. All three concentrations adjust in a smooth curve toward new equilibrium values.

        The changes must respect the stoichiometry of the balanced equation. For every $1 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ decrease in $[\ce{O_2}]$, $[\ce{SO_2}]$ decreases by $2 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ and $[\ce{SO_3}]$ increases by $2 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$. On the graph, the $[\ce{SO_2}]$ and $[\ce{SO_3}]$ curves change by equal amounts, and $[\ce{O_2}]$ changes by half as much.

        The new equilibrium concentrations are different from the original ones, but the value of $K_c$ is the same.

    2. no effect on $K_c \:\checkmark$\
        $K_c$ depends only on temperature $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The equilibrium constant $K_c$ is a function of temperature alone. Adding a reactant changes the individual equilibrium concentrations (the position of equilibrium shifts), but the ratio defined by the $K_c$ expression returns to the same numerical value once the new equilibrium is established. Only a change in temperature alters $K_c.$ For this exothermic reaction, increasing $T$ would decrease $K_c$, while decreasing $T$ would increase $K_c.$
