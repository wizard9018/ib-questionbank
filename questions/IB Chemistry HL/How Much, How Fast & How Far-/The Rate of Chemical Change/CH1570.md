        $\underline{\textrm{Explanation:}}$

        

        The equilibrium constant magnitude decreases as the temperature increases. This occurs for exothermic reactions, as temperature increases do not favour them. When the temperature increases in an exothermic reaction's chemical equilibrium, the reverse process is favoured.

        

        Exothermic reactions show products with lower enthalpies than the corresponding reactants. As a result, products are more chemically stable than reactants.

    2.  An increase in temperature shifts the equilibrium to the left/reactant side $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Explanation:}}$

        

        As temperature is increased, $K_c$ is reduced. The reverse reaction is favoured and the formation of reactant is preferred. As a result, phosphogene is consumed if the temperature is increased and its concentration decreases.

2.  $[\ce{Cl2}]_0 = 0.200\text{\ mol dm}^{-3}$ and $[\ce{Cl2}]_{eq} = 0.165\text{\ mol dm}^{-3}\ \checkmark$

    $[\ce{COCl2}]_{eq} = 0.100+0.035 = 0.135 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}\ \checkmark$

    $K_c = 4.96\ \checkmark$

    $\underline{\textrm{Explanation:}}$

    

    The information can be used to deduce the initial concentrations at 600 K.

    $C = \dfrac{n}{V}$

    $[\ce{CO}]_0 = \dfrac {0.400 \textrm{\ mol}}{2.00 \textrm{\ dm}^3} = 0.200 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$

    $[\ce{Cl2}]_0 =  \dfrac {0.400 \textrm{\ mol}}{2.00 \textrm{\ dm}^3} = 0.200 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$

    $[\ce{COCl2}]_0 =  \dfrac {0.200 \textrm{\ mol}}{2.00 \textrm{\ dm}^3} = 0.100 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$

    The problem statement tells us that chlorine is consumed during the reaction, and 0.330 mol is left once equilibrium is reached.

    $[\ce{Cl_2}]_{eq} = \dfrac{0.330 \textrm{\ mol}}{2.00 \textrm{\ dm}^3} = 0.165 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$

    To determine the changes in concentration as the system reaches equilibrium, we can use a RICE table (Reaction, Initial, Change, Equilibrium) to organize the information. The stoichiometric relationship between the reactants and products is $1:1:1$, as given by the balanced chemical equation. For every mole of $\ce{Cl_2}$ that reacts, 1 mole of CO is consumed, and 1 mole of $\ce{COCl_2}$ is produced. Let's represent the amount of $\ce{Cl_2}$ that reacts as $x$.

    Now we can fill out the RICE table:

    |$\hspace{4em}$||||||||

    |:-:|:-:|:--|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|

    R|$\ce{CO(g)}$ |+| $\ce{Cl2(g)}$ |$\ce{<=>}$ |  $\ce{COCl2(g)}$||

    I|0.200 || 0.200 || 0.200|

    C|$-x$||$-x$||$+x$|

    E|$0.200-x$ ||$0.200-x$||$0.100+x$

    Using the equilibrium concentration of $\ce{Cl2}$ provided:

    $[\ce{Cl2}]_{eq} = 0.200-x = 0.165 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$

    Solving for $x:$

    $x = 0.200 - 0.165 = 0.035 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$

    Substituting $x$ into the expressions in the table gives the other equilibrium concentrations

    $[\ce{CO}]_{eq} =0.200-0.035=0.165 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$

    $[\ce{COCl2}]_{eq} =0.100+0.035=0.135 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$

    The equilibrium constant can be deduced at this temperature:

    $K_c = \dfrac{[\ce{COCl_2}]}{[\ce{CO}] [\ce{Cl_2}]} = \dfrac {0.135}{(0.165)(0.165)} = 4.96$

    1.  (order with respect to) $\ce{CO} = 1$ and $\ce{Cl2} = 1\ \checkmark$

        

        $k = 1.20 \times 10^{-27} \textrm{\ dm}^3\textrm{mol}^{-1}\textrm{s}^{-1}\checkmark$

     

        $\textrm{rate} = 1.20 \times  10^{-27}\textrm{\ dm}^3 \textrm{mol}^{-1}\textrm{s}^{-1} [\ce{CO}][\ce{Cl_2}]\ \checkmark$

        *Note: Consider ECF for incorrect order or incorrect rate constant determination and award M3.*

     

        $\underline{\textrm{Explanation:}}$

        

        The orders can be determined from the experimental data.

        | Experiment |[CO]$_\mathbf{0}$  $\\$ $\mathbf{/ mol \ dm^{-3}}$ |[Cl$\mathbf{_2}$]$_\mathbf{0}$ $\\$ $\mathbf{/ mol \ dm^{-3}}$ | Initial rate $\\$ $\mathbf{/ mol \ dm^{-3} \ s^{-1}}$  |

        | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: |

        |1|0.100|0.100|$1.20 \times 10^{-29}$|

        |2|0.100|0.400|$4.80 \times 10^{-29}$|

        |3|0.300|0.400|$1.44 \times 10^{-28}$|

        **Method 1:**

        The rate law for any reaction can be written as the rate constant times the concentrations of the reactants raised to a power, the order of the reaction.  For this reaction,

        $\textrm{rate}=k\big [\ce{CO} \big]^a [\ce{Cl_2} \big]^b$

        If we compare the rates of experiments 1 and 2 by considering their ratio:

        $\dfrac {\textrm{rate}_{1}}{\textrm{rate}_2}$ = $\dfrac{k \big [\ce{CO} \big ]^a_{1}\big [\ce{Cl_2} \big ]_{1}^b}{k \big [\ce{CO} \big ]_{2}^a \big [\ce{Cl2} \big ]_{2}^b}$ 

        $\dfrac{1.20 \times 10^{-29} \textrm { mol dm}^{-3}\textrm{s}^{-1}}{4.80 \times 10^{-29}\textrm{ mol dm}^{-3}\textrm{s}^{-1}}  = \dfrac {k \big [0.100 \textrm { mol dm}^{-3} \big ]^a[0.100 \textrm{ mol dm}^{-3} \big ]^b}{k\big [0.100 \textrm{ mol dm}^{-3}\big ]^a \big [0.400 \textrm {\ mol dm}^{-3} \big ]^b} $

        Canceling out the terms that appear in the numerator and denominator, and simplifying the ratio of rates,

        $\dfrac{1}{4}  = \dfrac { \big [0.100 \textrm{ mol dm}^{-3} \big ]^b}{ \big [0.400 \textrm {\ mol dm}^{-3} \big ]^b} =   \bigg ( \dfrac{1}{4} \bigg )^b$

    

        $b=1$

        So the order with respect to $\ce{Cl2}$ is 1.

        Comparing experiments 2 and 3 will give the order with respect to CO.

        $\dfrac {\textrm{rate}_{2}}{\textrm{rate}_3}$ = $\dfrac{k \big [\ce{CO} \big ]^a_{2}\big [\ce{Cl_2} \big ]_{2}^b}{k \big [\ce{CO} \big ]_{3}^a \big [\ce{Cl2} \big ]_{3}^b}$ 

        $\dfrac{4.80 \times 10^{-29} \textrm { mol dm}^{-3}\textrm{s}^{-1}}{1.44 \times 10^{-28}\textrm{ mol dm}^{-3}\textrm{s}^{-1}}  = \dfrac {k \big [0.100 \textrm { mol dm}^{-3} \big ]^a[0.400 \textrm{ mol dm}^{-3} \big ]^b}{k\big [0.300 \textrm{ mol dm}^{-3}\big ]^a \big [0.400 \textrm {\ mol dm}^{-3} \big ]^b} $

        Canceling out the terms that appear in the numerator and denominator, and simplifying the ratio of rates,

        $\dfrac{1}{3}  = \dfrac { [0.100 \textrm{ mol dm}^{-3}]^a}{ \big [0.300 \textrm {mol dm}^{-3} \big ]^a} =   \bigg ( \dfrac{1}{3} \bigg )^a$

    

        $a=1$

        The rate expression is $\textrm{rate} = k [\ce{CO}][\ce{Cl_2}].$

        **Method 2:**

        In experiments 1 and 2, the concentration of CO is constant, but the concentration of $\ce{Cl_2}$ quadruples.  The rate also increases by a factor of 4. The reaction is 1st order in $\ce{Cl_2}.$ In experiments 2 and 3, the concentration of $\ce{Cl2}$ is constant, but the concentration of CO triples.  The rate also increases by a factor of 3, the reaction is 1st order in CO.  The rate expression is $\textrm{rate} = k [\ce{CO}][\ce{Cl_2}].$

        **Determining the rate constant:** 

        The rate constant can be deduced from the experimental data. 

        $k = \dfrac{\textrm{rate}}{[\ce{CO}][\ce{Cl_2}]}$

        Any trial would allow us to determine $k.$ Using Experiment 1,

        $k= \dfrac{1.20 \times  10^{-29}\textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}\textrm{s}^{-1}}{(0.100 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3})(0.100 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3})}$

        $k=1.20 \times 10^{-27} \textrm{\ dm}^3\textrm{mol}^{-1}\textrm{s}^{-1}$

        Finally, the complete rate expression is  

        $\textrm{rate} = 1.20 \times 10^{-27}\textrm{\ dm}^3\textrm{mol}^{-1}\textrm{s}^{-1} [\textrm{CO}][\ce{Cl_2}]$

    2.  Rate expression has CO and $\ce{Cl_2}$ to the first power; RDS must involve a collision between one CO and one $\ce{Cl_2}\ \checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Explanation:}}$

        

        Mechanisms of chemical reactions show the sequence of elementary steps involved so that the chemical reaction can occur. The slowest elementary step (that with the highest activation energy) is the one known as the rate$\text{--}$determining step (RDS) as it is the one that determines the rate. As a result, the rate expression that is experimentally found provides information about this RDS. The molecularity of the reaction can be deduced from the orders of the reactants.

        In this case, the reaction is first order with respect to CO and $\ce{Cl2}$, meaning that the slowest elementary step of the mechanism (RDS) involves the collision of one CO with one $\ce{Cl2}.$ It is a bimolecular elementary step.
