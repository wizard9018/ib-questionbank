        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:\

        The rate for the first 100 seconds can be calculated as the change in the concentration of $\ce{N2O5}$ in that period.

        $\text{rate} = -\dfrac{\Delta[\ce{N2O5}]}{\Delta t}$

        $\text{initial rate} \approx \dfrac{[\ce{N2O5}]_{t=0} - [\ce{N2O5}]_{t=100}}{100\,\text{s} - 0\,\text{s}}$

        ***Experiment 2:***

        $\text{rate} = \dfrac{0.0500\,\text{mol dm}^{-3} - 0.0422\,\text{mol dm}^{-3}}{100\,\text{s}} = 7.80 \times 10^{-5}\,\text{mol dm}^{-3}\,\text{s}^{-1}$

        ***Experiment 3:***

        $\text{rate} = \dfrac{0.0750\,\text{mol dm}^{-3} - 0.0634\,\text{mol dm}^{-3}}{100\,\text{s}} = 1.16 \times 10^{-5}\,\text{mol dm}^{-3}\,\text{s}^{-1}$

    2.  $\ce{N2O5}$ is order 1 $\checkmark$\

        $\text{rate} = k[\ce{N2O5}]\,\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:\

        The rate law can be deduced from the given data.

        ***Method 1:***\

        Compare Experiments 1 and 2:

        -   $\ce{[N2O5]}$ doubles $\Rightarrow$ rate doubles $\Rightarrow$ $\ce{N2O5}$ is order 1

  

        There is a direct proportionality between the reactant concentration and the rate, so the reaction is first order with respect to $\ce{N2O5}.$

        The rate law is $\text{rate} = k[\ce{N2O5}]$

        ***Method 2:***\

        The general rate law for the reaction is 

        $\text{rate} = k[\ce{N2O5}]^x$

        For experiments 1 and 2 (or any other pair),  set up the following ratio of the initial rates.:

        $\dfrac{\text{rate}_1}{\text{rate}_2} = \dfrac{k([\ce{N2O5}]_{\text{1}})^x}{k([\ce{N2O5}]_{\text{2}})^x} = \left(\dfrac{\ce{[N2O5]}_{\text{1}}}{\ce{[N2O5]}_{\text{2}}}\right)^x$

        Plugging in the values fromt the table above,

        $\dfrac{3.90 \times 10^{-5}\,\text{mol dm}^{-3}\,\text{s}^{-1}}{7.80 \times 10^{-5}\,\text{mol dm}^{-3}\,\text{s}^{-1}} = \left(\dfrac{0.0250\,\text{mol dm}^{-3}}{0.0500\,\text{mol dm}^{-3}}\right)^x$

        $\dfrac{1}{2} = \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^x\ ; \quad x = 1$

        The rate law for the reaction is $\text{rate} = k[\ce{N2O5]^1}$

    3.  $k = 1.56 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{s}^{-1}\,\checkmark \checkmark$

2.  

    1.  Mechanism A $\checkmark$\

        RDS (slow step) depends on $\ce{N_2O_5}$ only$\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:\

        The rate$-$determining step (RDS) is the slowest step in the reaction mechanism. This step determines the overall rate of the chemical reaction. In this case, the rate depends on $\ce{N2O5}$ with order 1, meaning that the RDS must only depend on $\ce{N_2O_5}$, and the elementary step should only have $\ce{N2O5}$ on the reactant side as a unique reactant. Mechanism A has that characteristic.

        In addition, the mechanism should result in the expected overall reaction. Mechanism A accomplished this condition as shown below.

        **Mechanism A**

        $\begin{aligned}

        \ce{N2O5 &-> NO2 + NO3} & & slow\ step \\

        \ce{NO2 + NO3 &-> NO2 + NO + O2} & & fast\ step \\

        \ce{NO + N2O5 &-> 3 NO2} & & fast\ step \\\\

        \ce{2 N2O5(g) &-> 4 NO2(g) + O2(g)} & & \text{OVERALL REACTION}

        \end{aligned}$

        Looking at the other mechanisms, the mechanistic steps sum to the correct overall reaction but the rate laws, based on the rate determining steps, do not match experiment.

        **Mechanism B**

        $\begin{aligned}

        \ce{N2O5 &-> NO2 + NO3} & & fast\ step \\

        \ce{NO2 + NO3 &-> NO2 + NO + O2} & & fast\ step \\

        \ce{NO + N2O5 &-> 3 NO2} & & slow\ step

        \end{aligned}$

        The rate law for the rate determining step is $r = k\ce{[N2O5][NO]}.$ Since NO is an intermediate it needs to be replaced. NO is made in the second step at a rate that is dependent on the amounts of $\ce{NO2}$ and $\ce{NO3}.$ In turn, those are produced in the first step at a rate that is proportional to the concentration of $\ce{N2O5}.$ Overall then, Mechanism B would have a rate law, $r = k[\ce{N2O5}]^2$

        For the final mechanism,

        **Mechanism C**

        $\begin{aligned}

        \ce{N2O5 &-> NO2 + NO3} & & fast\ step \\

        \ce{NO3 + N2O5 &-> 3 NO2 + O2} & & slow\ step

        \end{aligned}$

        The rate law would be $r = k\ce{[N2O5][NO3]}.$ Since $\ce{NO3}$ is an intermediate, we need to replace it. As $\ce{NO3}$ is made in the first step at a rate that is proportional to the concentration of $\ce{N2O5},$ again the rate law would be $r = k[\ce{N2O5}]^2$

    2.  $\ce{NO2 or NO3 or NO}\: \checkmark$

        *Note: Do not accept $\ce{NO}$ or $\ce{NO2}$ if Mechanism C was proposed in section (i).*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:\

        Intermediates take part in the mechanism but do not appear in the overall chemical reaction.

3.  $1:4$ ratio between $\ce{NO2}$ and $\ce{O2}$ production$\checkmark$\

    $\ce{NO2}$ rate of production is four times greater than that of $\ce{O2}\,\checkmark$

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:\

    Given the chemical reaction $\ce{2 N2O5(g) -> 4 NO2(g) + O2(g)},$ it can be seen that the rate of production of $\ce{NO2}$ is  four times bigger than that of oxygen as $\ce{NO2}$ presents a coefficient 4 in the balanced chemical reaction whereas $\ce{O2}$ presents a coefficient 1.

    $\text{rate} = \dfrac{1}{4} \times \dfrac{\Delta[\ce{NO2}]}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{\Delta \ce{O2}}{\Delta t}$

4.  $\ce{NO2}$ is dangerous$\checkmark$\

    Avoid contact by using a fume hood in the lab$\checkmark$

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:\

    Nitrogen dioxide is dangerous as it dissolves in water to form nitrous and nitric acids.

    $\ce{2 NO2(g) + H2O(l) -> HNO2(aq) + HNO3(aq)}$

    It is mandatory to prevent contact with this gas to avoid health concerns. These substances can affect human health, irritating the respiratory tract and increasing the risk of some illnesses.  It can also irritate the eyes if present in the environment in the lab.

    The fume hood must be used for this chemical reaction to avoid contact.
