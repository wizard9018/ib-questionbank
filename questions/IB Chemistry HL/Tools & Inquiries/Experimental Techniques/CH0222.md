# CH0222

**Maximum mark: 5**

1.  Define calorimetry. [1 mark]

2.  A student sets up a coffee$-$cup calorimeter to measure the enthalpy change of the reaction

    $\ce{Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) \longrightarrow ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s)}$

    Describe two assumptions that are typically made in this experiment.  [2 marks]

3.  State two ways in which errors in this calorimetry experiment can be minimized.  [2 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  technique to measure the change in temperature of a system to determine heat change during a  reaction  $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    In calorimetry, the temperature change of a known mass of water (or solution) is measured, and the heat absorbed or released by the system is calculated. It allows the enthalpy change of a reaction to be determined experimentally.

2.  any two of the following:

    -   no heat lost to surroundings  $\checkmark$
    -   mass of solution is same as that  of water $\checkmark$
    -   heat capacity of solution is same as that of water $\checkmark$

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation:}}$\
    These assumptions simplify calculations by treating the calorimeter as perfectly insulated and the solution as behaving exactly like pure water. In reality, some heat is absorbed by the calorimeter itself or lost to the air, and the solution’s properties (mass, density, specific heat capacity) may differ slightly from those of water.

3.  any two of the following:

    -   increase insulation / use lid to prevent heat loss $\checkmark$
    -   extrapolate cooling curve to start of reaction $\checkmark$
    -   measure density of solution $\checkmark$
    -   measure heat capacity of solution $\checkmark$
    -   measure heat absorbed by calorimeter using standard $\checkmark$
    -   stir continuously to ensure uniform temperature $\checkmark$

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation:}}$\
    Even well$-$insulated calorimeters absorb some heat. To correct for this, the calorimeter's heat capacity can be determined using a standard reaction or known temperature change. Subtracting this quantity from the total measured energy ensures that only the energy transferred to the solution is used in the enthalpy calculation. This correction, along with improved insulation and data extrapolation, greatly increases the reliability and accuracy of calorimetric measurements.
