    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

    Burettes are graduated glassware used to deliver precise volumes of liquid. They are typically used to deliver the titrant, the solution of known concentration that is used to determine the concentration of an unknown solution.

    Erlenmeyer flasks are used to hold and swirl the solutions during titration.

2.  Any two of the following:

    Using an indicator *(award mark for naming a specific indicator)* $\checkmark$

    A pH meter/ conductivity meter $\checkmark$

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

    In an acid-base titration, the equivalence point refers to when the acid or base is completely neutralized. There are a few ways to tell when the equivalence point has been reached:

    - Using a pH indicator: A pH indicator is a substance that changes color at a certain pH value. This substance is usually a weak acid. Before the titration starts, the pH indicator is added to the solution of the analyte. The pH of the solution will change as the titrant is added. The indicator will change color when the pH of the solution matches the pH value of the indicator. This is the equivalence point.

    - Using a pH meter: A pH meter is a tool that measures the pH of a solution. During titration, the pH meter can keep track of the solution's pH. When the solution's pH reaches the equivalence point, the pH meter will show a sharp significant change in pH.

    - Using a conductivity meter: A conductivity meter is a tool that measures how well a solution conducts electricity. The amount of ions in a solution affects how well it moves electricity. The number of ions in the solution will rise as the titrant is added. When the number of ions in the solution reaches the equivalence point, the conductivity meter will show a sharp rise in conductivity.

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

    An error that enters an experimental process due to the apparatus or procedure used is systematic. Systematic errors cause a decrease in accuracy, leading to an increased deviation of the observed value from the literature or true value. Systematic errors consistently manifest in one specific direction, and their reduction can be achieved through experimental procedure modifications and properly calibrated devices. In opposition, random errors surface due to the inherent limitations of the used measuring apparatus, including insufficient precision and uncontrollable variables inevitable in any experimental situation, such as a delay in human response. Typically, the values are dispersed around the true value and tend to negate one another when multiple measurements are conducted. Therefore, while it may not be possible to eradicate random errors completely, reducing them by repeating measurements is feasible.
