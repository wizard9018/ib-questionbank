# CH2440

A student prepares a standard solution by dissolving a known mass of anhydrous sodium carbonate in a $250\textrm{ cm}^{3}$ volumetric flask. Which step, if omitted, would introduce the largest systematic error in the concentration?

-   A.$\hspace{1.em}$Rinsing all traces of the dissolved solid into the volumetric flask

-   B.$\hspace{1.em}$Drying the volumetric flask before adding the solution

-   C.$\hspace{1.em}$Inverting the flask several times after filling to the mark

-   D.$\hspace{1.em}$Repeating the mass measurement three times on the balance

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

**答案：A**

$\textrm{\underline{Explanation:}}$\
When preparing a standard solution, the concentration depends on transferring all of the weighed solid into the volumetric flask. If the beaker and weighing vessel are not rinsed thoroughly, some solute remains behind and the concentration of the solution is systematically lower than intended. This cannot be corrected by repeating the experiment because the same fraction of solid is lost each time.

Drying the volumetric flask is a common misconception. The flask does not need to be dry because the final volume is determined by the graduation mark, not by the amount of water already in the flask. Adding distilled water to a wet flask does not change the amount of solute.

Inverting the flask affects the homogeneity of the solution but not the overall concentration. Every sample drawn from the flask would average to the correct concentration once the solution is eventually mixed.

Repeating the mass measurement confuses precision with accuracy. Repeating reduces random error in the mass, but the dominant systematic error in a standard solution preparation arises from incomplete transfer of the solute, not from the balance reading.
