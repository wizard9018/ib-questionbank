# CH0214

Theoretical and experimental values for enthalpy change of combustion may differ. Which of the below is **not** a reason why?

- A.$\hspace{1em}$Heat was lost to the surroundings

- B.$\hspace{1em}$Incomplete combustion

- C.$\hspace{1em}$Heat was absorbed from the surroundings

- D.$\hspace{1em}$Not accounting for heat absorbed by the calorimeter

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

**答案：C**

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:\
When we compare the experimental enthalpy change of combustion with the theoretical value, it is important to remember that real experiments rarely capture all of the energy released. Some of the heat escapes to the surroundings rather than being transferred to the water being measured. In addition, combustion is not always complete: instead of forming only carbon dioxide and water, some of the fuel may burn only partially, producing carbon monoxide or leaving traces of soot. This incomplete combustion releases less energy than predicted from the theoretical reaction. Another common source of discrepancy is the calorimeter itself, which absorbs a portion of the released heat. If this is not accounted for in the calculations, the measured enthalpy change will be less negative than expected.

What is not a valid explanation is the idea that heat might be absorbed from the surroundings. Combustion is a highly exothermic process, and the challenge is to prevent energy from escaping, not to imagine that the system is somehow warmed by external heat input. The experimental value is therefore lower than the theoretical value because energy is lost or the combustion is incomplete, not because the surroundings provide additional heat.
