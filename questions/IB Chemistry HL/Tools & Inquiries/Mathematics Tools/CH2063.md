## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  visible light promotes an electron between split $d\text{--}$orbital levels $\checkmark$\
    splitting is altered by number of ligands / identity of ligands / shape of compound $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    Transition metal complexes are colored due to the splitting of $d\text{--}$orbitals when ligands interact with the central metal ion. The $d\text{--}$orbitals of the metal ion experience different degrees of repulsion from the ligands, causing them to split into two energy levels. The energy gap between these levels determines the wavelength of light absorbed, as electrons transition from a lower$\text{--}$energy $d\text{--}$orbital to a higher$\text{--}$energy $d\text{--}$orbital. The color observed is the complementary color of the absorbed light.

    The nature of the ligands affects the size of the energy gap. Stronger field ligands (such as $\ce{H2O})$ create a larger energy gap, meaning higher$\text{--}$energy (shorter wavelength) light is absorbed. Weaker field ligands (such as $\ce{Cl-})$ cause a smaller energy gap, meaning lowe$\text{--}$energy (longer wavelength) light is absorbed, producing a different observed color. $\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}$ appears pink because it absorbs light in the green/blue region, indicating a larger $d\text{--}$orbital splitting due to water's stronger ligand field. Conversely, $\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}$ appears blue because it absorbs orange/yellow light, reflecting a smaller $d\text{--}$orbital splitting caused by the weaker chloride ligands.

    The oxidation state of the metal ion and its coordination geometry also influence the splitting pattern and, consequently, the color of the complex.

2.
    1.  $250\text{ cm}^3$ volumetric flask\
        **AND**\
        analytical balance $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        To make a solution of concentration $1.000\text{ mol dm}^3,$ the solid cobalt (II) nitrate must be massed out on a balance capable of providing four significant figures. The molar mass of $\ce{Co(NO3)2}$ is $182.94\text{ g mol}^{-1}.$ To make $250\text{ cm}^3,$ $45.74\text{ g}$ of the $\ce{Co(NO3)2}$ must be weighed on an analytical balance. To keep the precision, the powder must be added to a $250\text{ cm}^3$ volumetric flask.  The powder must be dissolved completely in some water, and then the solution made up to precisely $250\text{ cm}^3.$

        It is not correct to use a beaker or conical flask to make this solution as the uncertainty in the volume will be much too high. It is also incorrect to add $250\text{ cm}^3$ of water to the powder using a graduated cylinder as the volume of the solution must be $250\text{ cm}^3,$ not the volume of water added.

    2.  $V_2 = 40.0\text{ cm}^3\ \checkmark$\
        combine $40.0\text{ cm}^3$ of the stock solution, make up to $50.0\text{ cm}^3$ with water $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The volume of the stock solution can be determined using the dilution formula $C_1 V_1 = C_2 V_2.$ The concentration and volume of the desired solution are given, and the concentration of the stock solution is also known.

        $\begin{aligned}
        C_1 &= 0.800\text{ mol dm}^3 \\
        V_2 &= 50.0\text{ cm}^3 \\
        C_2 &= 1.000\text{ mol dm}^3
        \end{aligned}$

        Rearranging for the unknown volume, the volume of the stock solution can be determined.

        $V_2 = \dfrac{C_1 V_1}{C_2} = \dfrac{(0.800\text{ mol dm}^3)(50.0\text{ cm}^3)}{1.000\text{ mol dm}^3} = 40.0\text{ cm}^3$

        To make the final volume $50.0\text{ cm}^3,$ water should be added until the solution reaches $50.0\text{ cm}^3.$ For dilute aqueous solutions, we often assume that the volume of water will exactly add to the volume of stock solution, so $10.0\text{ cm}^3$ of water could be added.

        It is not correct to dilute the entire stock solution to the lower concentration. If $50.0\text{ cm}^3$ of the solution are required, that is the amount that should be made. In this problem, a series of solutions are being produced to construct the calibration curve. Unnecessary error would be introduced by diluting the solution over and over. Each of the solutions should be made by adding water to the stock solution.

    3.  $\textrm{\% uncertainty} = 7\%\ \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The percent uncertainty is defined as

        $\dfrac{\text{absolute uncertainty}}{\text{measured value}}\times 100\%$

        For the absorbance of the $0.200\text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ solution,

        $\dfrac{0.005}{0.075}\times 100\% = 6.67\% \longrightarrow 7\%$

        The value is typically reported to one signficant figure when it is larger than 2\%.

    4.  *strength:* data is appropriately modeled by a linear fit $\checkmark$\
        *limitation:*  $y\text{--}$intercept is non$\text{--}$zero, the absorbance is not directly proportional to the concentration $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The Beer$\text{--}$Lambert Law states that absorbance $A$ is directly proportional to concentration $C,$ meaning a plot of absorbance versus concentration should yield a straight line passing through the origin. The given data suggest a generally linear relationship between absorbance and concentration, which is consistent with this relationship. However, for Beer's Law to be fully supported, the line must pass through zero when the concentration is zero.

    5.  not valid; the linear relationship may not hold for more concentrated solutions $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The data provided suggests a linear relationship between concentration and absorbance only within the measured range. Since no experimental data points exist beyond $1.000\text{ mol dm}^{-3},$ assuming that the trend continues without verification is risky. Higher concentrations could result in systematic errors due to changes in solution properties or limitations of the spectrophotometer.

        It is not valid to extrapolate the absorbance for a $1.2\text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ solution with confidence.

3.  $K = \dfrac{\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}\big][\ce{Cl-}]^4}{\big[\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}\big]}\ \checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    The equilibrium constant expressions is the ratio of the concentrations of the products raised to their stoichiometric coefficiencts divided by the concentrations of the reactants raised to their stoichiometric coefficiencts.  By convention, all liquids and solids are omitted.

    For the equation:

    $\ce{[CoCl4]^2-(aq) + 6 H2O(l) <=> [Co(H2O)6]^2+(aq) + 4 Cl-(aq)}$

    The equilibrium constant expression is

    $K = \dfrac{\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}\big][\ce{Cl-}]^4}{\big[\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}\big]}$

    The water is not included because it is in the liquid phase.

4.
    1.  $[\ce{Cl-}] = 6.0\text{ mol dm}^{-3}\ \checkmark$\
        $\big[\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}\big] = 0.50\text{ mol dm}^{-3}\ \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        As the volume is doubling when the two $10\text{ cm}^3$ solutions are mixed, the concentrations will both be halved.

        $[\ce{Cl-}] = \dfrac{(12.0\text{ mol dm}^{-3})(10\text{ cm}^3)}{20\text{ cm}^3} = 6.0\text{ mol dm}^{-3}$

        $\big[\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}\big] = \dfrac{(1.000\text{ mol dm}^{-3})(10\text{ cm}^3)}{20\text{ cm}^3} = 0.50\text{ mol dm}^{-3}$

    2.  $y = 0.082 = 0.115x + 0.045 \: \checkmark$\
        $\big[\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}\big] =  0.32\text{ mol dm}^{-3}\ \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Using the equation of the line of best fit, $y = 0.115x + 0.045,$ the value for the measured absorption $(y)$ can be used to deduce the concentration $(x).$

        $x = \dfrac{y - 0.045}{0.115};$

        $\big[\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}\big] = \dfrac{0.082 - 0.045}{0.115} = 0.32\text{ mol dm}^{-3}$

    3.  $\big[\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}\big] = 0.18\text{ mol dm}^{-3}\ \checkmark$\
        $K = 1380\ \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        A table will help determine the equilibrium concentrations.

        R: | $\ce{[CoCl_4]^{2-}}$ | + | $6 \ce{\ H2O}\textrm{(l)}$ | $\rightleftharpoons$ |  $\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^{2+}}$ |+| $4 \ce{\ Cl-}$
        :-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
        $\textbf{I:}$ | $0$ |  | $-$ | |  $0.50$ || $6.0$
        $\textbf{C:}$ | $+x$ |  |  $-$ | |  $-x$ || $-4x$
        $\textbf{E:}$ | $x$ |  |  $-$ | |  $0.50-x$ || $6.0-4x$

        The equilibrium concentration of $\big[\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}\big] = 0.32\text{ mol dm}^{-3},$ so $x = 0.18\text{ mol dm}^{-3}.$ The equilibrium concentrations of the other reactant and product can be determined.

        $[\ce{Cl-}] = 6.0-4(0.18) = 5.28 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$

        $\big[\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}\big] = x = 0.18 \textrm{mol dm}^{-3}$

        The values can be inserted into the expression for $K.$

        $K = \dfrac{\big[\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}\big][\ce{Cl-}]^4}{\big[\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}\big]} = \dfrac{(0.32)(5.28)^4}{0.18} = 1380$

5.
    1.  $\Delta H < 0\ \checkmark$\
        reaction shifts backward, forward reaction is exothermic $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        As the temperature rises, the reaction mixture appears more blue, indicating that the equilibrium shifts toward the reactant $\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}.$ According to Le Châtelier's Principle, an increase in temperature shifts the reaction in the endothermic direction because the system absorbs heat to counteract the temperature change.

        Since the forward reaction produces the pink complex $\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+},$ but higher temperatures favor the formation of the blue complex $\ce{[CoCl4]^2-},$ this means that the reverse reaction is endothermic and the forward reaction is exothermic. Therefore, $\Delta H$ for the forward reaction is negative.

    2.  $x\text{--}$axis: $\dfrac{1}{T}$ **AND** $y\text{--}$axis: $\ln K\ \checkmark$\
        gradient: $- \dfrac{\Delta H^{\minuso}}{R}$ **AND** $y\text{--}$intercept $\dfrac{\Delta S^{\minuso}}{R}\ \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The equation

        $\ln K = - \dfrac{\Delta H^{\minuso}}{RT} + \dfrac{\Delta S^{\minuso}}{R}$

        is in the form of a straight$\text{--}$line equation,  $y = mx + b,$ where:

        $\begin{aligned}
        y &= \ln K & \text{dependent variable} \\
        x &= \dfrac{1}{T} & \text{independent variable} \\
        m &= -\dfrac{\Delta H^{\minuso}}{R} & \text{gradient} \\
        b &= \dfrac{\Delta S^{\minuso}}{R} & y\text{--intercept}\\
        \end{aligned}$

        To analyze the relationship between equilibrium constant and temperature, the student should graph $\ln K$ on the $y\text{--}$axis and $\dfrac{1}{T}$ on the $x\text{--}$axis. The gradient of the resulting straight line gives $-\dfrac{\Delta H^{\minuso}}{R},$ allowing the enthalpy change to be determined. The $y\text{--}$intercept gives $\dfrac{\Delta S^{\minuso}}{R},$ which can be used to find the entropy change.
