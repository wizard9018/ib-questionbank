# CH0630

A student is measuring the density of a solution. 

The volume was determined by difference using a 

burette. He recorded the data in the following table.

$\mbox{}\\[-15pts]$

Trial|Mass of Beaker $\pm$ 0.01 g)|Mass of Beaker with Solution ($\pm$ 0.01 g)|Volume ($\pm$ 0.10 cm$^\mathbf{3}$) 

|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|

1|30.02|45.97|7.05

2|30.00|45.96|7.20

3|30.01|45.95|7.10

1.  Calculate the density of the solution for each 

    trial and determine the average 

    density. [3 marks]

2.  1.  Calculate the percentage uncertainty in 

        the mass of the solution and the volume 

        for Trial 1. [2 marks]

    2.  Determine the absolute instrumental 

        uncertainty in the density. [1 mark]

    3.  Determine the random uncertainty in the 

        density using the spread of the 

        data. [1 mark]

    4.  State the average density of the solution 

        with its uncertainty. [1 mark]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  $d_1 = \dfrac{15.95}{7.05} 

    = 2.26\text{\ g cm}^{-3}$

    $d_2 = \dfrac{15.96}{7.20} 

    = 2.22\text{\ g cm}^{-3}$

    $d_3 = \dfrac{15.94}{7.10} 

    = 2.25\text{\ g cm}^{-3}

    \ \checkmark$

    $\bar{d} = \dfrac{2.26 + 2.22 + 2.25}{3} 

    = 2.24\text{\ g cm}^{-3}

    \ \checkmark \checkmark$

    *Award M1 for the correct density calculations for the trials.*\

    *Award M2 for the correct average.*\

    *Award M3 for signficant figures.*

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation:}}$\

    The density must be calculated for each trial 

    individually before averaging. A common error is 

    to compute the average mass and average volume 

    separately and then divide:

    $\dfrac{\bar{m}}{\bar{V}} = \dfrac{15.95}{7.12} 

    = 2.24\text{\ g cm}^{-3}$

    In this data set the two methods give similar 

    results, but they are not mathematically 

    equivalent. The ratio of averages equals the 

    average of ratios only when the denominator is 

    constant across all trials. In general, 

    $\dfrac{\bar{m}}{\bar{V}} \neq 

    \overline{\left(\dfrac{m}{V}\right)},$ 

    and the 

    correct procedure is always to calculate the 

    derived quantity for each trial first, then 

    average.

    The volume is measured to 3 significant figures 

    (burette reading by difference), so density 

    values are reported to 3 significant figures 

    throughout.

2.  1.  Mass of solution: $15.95 \pm 0.02\text{\ g}$ 

        (uncertainties added for 

        subtraction) $\checkmark$

        Percentage uncertainty in mass: 

        $\dfrac{0.02}{15.95} \times 100\% 

        = 0.13\%$

        Percentage uncertainty in volume: 

        $\dfrac{0.10}{7.05} \times 100\% = 1.42\%$

        Total percentage uncertainty: 

        $0.13\% + 1.42\% = 1.6\%\ \checkmark$

    2.  Instrumental uncertainty: 

        $2.26 \times 0.016 

        = \pm\,0.04\text{\ g cm}^{-3}\ \checkmark$

    3.  Random uncertainty (from spread of data):

        $\dfrac{d_\text{max} - d_\text{min}}{2} 

        = \dfrac{2.26 - 2.22}{2} 

        = \pm\,0.02\text{\ g cm}^{-3}\ \checkmark$

    4.  The instrumental uncertainty ($\pm\,0.04$) is 

        larger than the random uncertainty 

        ($\pm\,0.02$), so the instrumental uncertainty 

        is used:

        $d = 2.24 \pm 0.04\text{\ g cm}^{-3}

        \ \checkmark$

        *Uncertainty rounded to 1 s.f., density 

        rounded to match.*

    

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation:}}$\

    When repeated trials produce a derived quantity, 

    there are two sources of uncertainty to consider:

    **Instrumental uncertainty** arises from the 

    precision of the measuring instruments. For a 

    quotient, percentage uncertainties are added. The 

    burette ($\pm\,0.10$ cm$^3$ on a ${\sim}7$ cm$^3$ 

    reading $= 1.4\%$) dominates over the mass 

    measurement ($0.13\%$). This illustrates the 

    importance of selecting appropriate apparatus.

    **Random uncertainty** reflects the scatter in 

    the data across trials. For IB, this is estimated 

    as half the range: 

    $\dfrac{d_\text{max} - d_\text{min}}{2}.$

    The larger of the two uncertainties is reported 

    with the final answer, as it represents the 

    dominant limitation of the experiment. In this 

    case, the instrumental uncertainty is roughly 

    twice the random uncertainty ($\pm\,0.04$ vs 

    $\pm\,0.02$), suggesting that equipment 

    precision remains the main limitation.
