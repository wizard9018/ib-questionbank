# CH1274

**Maximum mark: 14**

When copper powder is added to concentrated nitric acid $(\ce{HNO3}),$ nitrogen dioxide gas $(\ce{NO2})$ is produced. The equation for this reaction is shown.

$\ce{Cu(s) + x HNO3(aq) -> Cu(NO3)2(aq) + y NO2(g) + z H2O(l)}$

1.  State the values of the coefficients $x,\, y,$ and $z.$ [2 marks]

2.  The rate of $\ce{NO2}$ formation can be measured using the following apparatus.

    ![CH1274b](images/CH1274b.png)

    1. State the purpose of the water bath in this experiment. [1 mark]

    2.  A small amount of $\ce{NO2}$ remains dissolved in the nitric acid.

        State why this effect produces a **systematic** error in the measured gas volume. [1 mark]

    3.  $\ce{NO2}$ reacts to produce gaseous dinitrogen tetroxide $(\ce{N2O4}):$

        $\ce{2NO2}\textrm{(g)} \rightleftharpoons \ce{N2O4}\textrm{(g)} \qquad \Delta H = -57.2 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1}$

        The gas syringe is outside the water bath, so the gas cools as it leaves the flask. Explain how this affects the measured volume of gas. [3 marks]

    4.  Suggest an alternative method of measuring the quantity of $\ce{NO2}$ produced that would not be affected by the error in (iii). [1 mark]

3.  A $1.0\,\text{g}$ sample of copper powder was added to $500 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ of aqueous nitric acid. The following graph shows the volume of $\ce{NO2}$ produced over time.

    ![CH1274c](images/CH1274c.png)

    1.  Calculate the rate of $\ce{NO2}$ production at $1.5\,\text{min},$ in $\text{cm}^3\,\text{min}^{-1}.$ [2 marks]

    2.  The uncertainty in the gas volume is $\pm 10\,\text{cm}^3.$

        Estimate the percentage uncertainty in the gas volume after $3.25\,\text{min}.$ [2 marks]

    3.  State how the graph would change if a $1.0\,\text{g}$ copper plate were used instead of copper powder, giving a reason for your answer. [2 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  $x = 4, y = 2, z = 2\:\checkmark \checkmark$

    *Award [1] if two values stated correctly*

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:\
    This is a redox reaction, and the coefficients follow from the electron transfer. Copper is oxidised from $0$ to $+2,$ losing two electrons. Nitrogen is reduced from $+5$ in $\ce{HNO3}$ to $+4$ in $\ce{NO2},$ gaining one electron per atom. Two nitrogen atoms must therefore be reduced for every copper atom oxidised, so $y = 2.$

    Not all of the nitric acid is reduced. The two nitrate ions in $\ce{Cu(NO3)2}$ hold nitrogen in the $+5$ state, unchanged from the acid, and act as spectator ions. Four molecules of $\ce{HNO3}$ are consumed in total: two are reduced to $\ce{NO2},$ and two supply the nitrate ions that pair with the $\ce{Cu^2+}.$ Hence $x = 4.$

    The four hydrogen atoms from those four molecules are accounted for as water, so $z = 2.$ Oxygen confirms the result: 12 atoms on the left, and $6 + 4 + 2 = 12$ on the right.

2.
    1.  to keep the temperature of the reaction mixture constant $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The reaction between copper and concentrated nitric acid is strongly exothermic. Without a water bath the mixture would warm as the reaction proceeded, the rate would rise during the run, and the measured gradient would not correspond to a single temperature.

    2.  measured gas volumes are all lower than the true volume $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:\
        A systematic error displaces every measurement in the same direction. Repeating the experiment and averaging does not remove it, because each repeat is shifted the same way and the mean simply settles on a value that is consistently too low or too high.

        Nitrogen dioxide dissolves in the aqueous nitric acid, so some of the gas produced never reaches the syringe. Gas is only lost from the measurement and never gained, so every measured volume is too low.

    3. forward reaction is exothermic, cooling shifts position of equilibrium right / favours $\ce{N2O4}$ $\checkmark$\
    two molecules of $\ce{NO2}$ are converted into one molecule of $\ce{N2O4},$ number of gas molecules decreases $\checkmark$\
    measured volume is lower than true volume / amount of $\ce{NO2}$ is underestimated $\checkmark$

        *Do not award M1 unless the shift is linked to the sign of $\Delta H.$*

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Le $\textrm{Ch\^{a}telier's}$ principle states that if a change is made to a system at equilibrium, the position of equilibrium shifts to counteract that change. The negative value of $\Delta H$ shows that the forward reaction releases heat, so cooling the gas favours the forward reaction and the proportion of $\ce{N2O4}$ in the mixture rises.

        Two molecules of $\ce{NO2}$ are converted into one molecule of $\ce{N2O4},$ so dimerisation halves the number of gas molecules it consumes. At constant temperature and pressure the volume of a gas is proportional to the amount of gas present, so the mixture in the syringe occupies less volume than the nitrogen dioxide released in the flask.

        The syringe therefore reads low and the amount of $\ce{NO2}$ produced is underestimated. Since the syringe is always cooler than the water bath, every reading is displaced in the same direction, so this is a systematic error rather than a random one.

        Cooling also contracts the gas directly, because volume is proportional to absolute temperature at constant pressure. This adds to the shortfall, but the two effects are not the same: contraction changes the volume occupied by a fixed amount of gas, whereas the equilibrium shift changes the amount of gas itself.

    4.  measure the loss in mass of reaction mixture
 $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        If the $\ce{NO2}$ is allowed to escape from the flask as it forms, the mass of the flask and its contents decreases. The mass lost is the mass of $\ce{NO2}$ produced, and dividing by the molar mass gives the amount in moles; following the mass over time gives the rate. Dimerisation does not affect the result, because converting two molecules of $\ce{NO2}$ into one molecule of $\ce{N2O4}$ conserves mass.

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:\
        The rate of change in a graph is equal to the gradient of the graph. If the rate changes over time, the graph will be curved. To calculate the rate of change at a particular time, a tangent must be drawn to that point on the curve. The slope of this tangent is then calculated by dividing the change in the $y$ value by the corresponding time interval.

    2.  $V = 475\,\text{cm}^3\: \checkmark$\
        $\text{\% uncertainty } = 2\%\,\checkmark$

        *Award M1 for values in range 470$-$485 cm$^\textit{3}$*

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The volume at $3.25 \textrm{\ min}$ was not recorded, so it must be interpolated. Taking the midpoint of the readings at $3.0 \textrm{\ min}$ and $3.5 \textrm{\ min}$ gives

        $V = \dfrac{450 \textrm{\ cm}^3+ 500\textrm{\ cm}^3}{2} = 475 \textrm{\ cm}^3.$

        Reading from a best$-$fit curve gives a marginally higher value, $V \approx 480 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ since the curve is concave down across this interval.

        Expressing the absolute uncertainty as a percentage of the reading makes the precision of different measurements comparable.

        $\textrm{\% uncertainty} = \dfrac{10 \textrm{\ cm}^3}{475 \textrm{\ cm}^3} \times 100\% = 2\%$

    3.  gradient / slope of the graph would decrease **OR** graph would be shallower $\checkmark$\
        surface area of plate would be smaller **AND** rate would decrease $\checkmark$

        *Accept a curve sketched on the graph with a smaller gradient, lying below the printed curve, for M1.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:\
        Reaction occurs only where acid particles meet copper atoms at the surface of the metal. Powder exposes far more of its atoms than a single plate of the same mass, so collisions occur less frequently with the plate and the rate falls. The rate of reaction is the gradient of the graph, so the plate gives a shallower curve.

        The mass of copper is the same in both experiments, so the amount of copper, and therefore the maximum amount of $\ce{NO2}$ that can be produced, is unchanged. Both reactions would eventually reach the same total volume of gas; the plate simply takes longer. Neither curve reaches that limit within the five minutes plotted.
