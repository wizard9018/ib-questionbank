**Maximum mark: 6**

Consider the data below on the successive ionization energies for $\ce{Al}.$

| $\enskip$Electrons Removed$\enskip$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| :---: | :---: | :---: |:---: |:---: |

$\mbox{}\\[-25pts]$

1. 1. Explain why the successive ionization energies for an element increase. [1 mark]

   2. Explain how the successive ionization energies for aluminium give evidence for its three valence electrons. [2 marks]

2. Using Sections 1 and 2 of the IB Chemistry data booklet, calculate the wavelength of light required to remove an electron from an aluminium atom. [3 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  1.  remove electrons from increasingly positive ion $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    Successive ionization energies increase because each electron is removed from an increasingly positive ion. As the positive charge of the ion increases, the attraction between the nucleus and the remaining electrons becomes stronger, so more energy is required to remove each subsequent electron.

    |$1 \hspace{1em}$|$\ce{M(g)} \longrightarrow \ce{M+(g)} + \ce{e-}$|
    :-|:--
    |2|$\ce{M+(g)} \longrightarrow \ce{M^{2+}(g)} + \ce{e-}$
    |3|$\ce{M^{2+}(g)} \longrightarrow \ce{M^{3+}(g)} + \ce{e-}$
    |4|$\ce{M^{3+}(g)} \longrightarrow \ce{M^{4+}(g)} + \ce{e-}$
    |$\ldots$|$\ldots$

    2.  large increase between 3rd and 4th ionization $\checkmark$\
        4th electron is removed from a lower / closer energy level  $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        There is a large increase between the third and fourth ionization energies of aluminium.
        The first three electrons are removed from the outer $n = 3$ shell, but the fourth electron must be removed from the inner $n = 2$ shell, which is closer to the nucleus and less shielded. This large jump in ionization energy shows that aluminium has three valence electrons in its outermost shell, consistent with its electron configuration: $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^1.$

2.  $\textrm{IE} = \dfrac{577  \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1} \times 1000 \textrm{\ J kJ}^{-1}}{6.02\times 10^{23} \textrm{\ mol}^{-1}} = 9.58\times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}\: \checkmark$\
    $\mbox{}\\[-10pts]$
    $f = \dfrac{E}{h} = \dfrac{9.58\times 10^{-19} \textrm{\ J}}{6.63\times10^{-34} \textrm{\ J\,s}} = 1.45\times 10^{15}\,\text{s}^{-1}\: \checkmark$\
    $\mbox{}\\[-10pts]$
    $\lambda = \dfrac{c}{f} = \dfrac{3.00\times 10^{8} \textrm{\ m s}^{-1}}{1.45\times10^{15} \textrm{\ s}^{-1}} = 2.08\times 10^{-7}\text{m} \textbf{\ OR\ } 208 \textrm{\ nm} \: \checkmark$\
    $\mbox{}\\[-10pts]$
    *Award [3] for correct final answer.*

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    To calculate the wavelength of light required to remove an electron from an aluminium atom, we first convert the molar ionization energy given in the data booklet into the energy per atom. The first ionization energy of aluminium is $577 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1}.$ Converting from kJ to J and dividing by Avogadro's constant, we can find the energy of one photon:

    $E = 577 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1} \times \dfrac{1000 \textrm{\ J}}{1 \textrm{\ kJ}} \times \dfrac{1 \textrm{\ mol}}{6.02 \times 10^{23} \textrm{\ photon}} = 9.58 \times 10^{-19} \textrm{\ J}$

    Next, we relate this energy to the frequency of light through $E = hf,$ where $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \textrm{\ J\,s}$.

    $f = \dfrac{E}{h} = \dfrac{9.58\times 10^{-19} \textrm{\ J}}{6.63\times10^{-34} \textrm{\ J\,s}} = 1.45\times 10^{15}\,\text{s}^{-1}$

    Finally, the wavelength can be found using $c = f\lambda,$ where $c = 3.00 \times 10^8 \textrm{\ m s}^{-1}:$

    $\lambda = \dfrac{c}{f} = \dfrac{3.00\times 10^{8} \textrm{\ m s}^{-1}}{1.45\times10^{15} \textrm{\ s}^{-1}} = 2.08\times 10^{-7}\text{m} \times \dfrac{1 \times 10^9 \textrm{\ nm}}{1 \textrm{\ m}} =  208 \textrm{\ nm}$
