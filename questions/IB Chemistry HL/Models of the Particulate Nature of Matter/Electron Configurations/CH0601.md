     *Award M1 for a general increase in ionization energy.\
     Award M2 for a drop between Be and B.\

     $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation:}}$\
     Across Period 2, the first ionization energy shows a general increase from lithium to neon. This occurs because, as the number of protons increases, the nuclear charge becomes greater while the added electrons occupy the same principal energy level. The increasing positive charge draws the outermost electrons closer to the nucleus, resulting in a  stronger electrostatic attraction between the nucleus and the valence electrons. Consequently, more energy is required to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms, leading to the overall upward trend.

    There are, however, two deviations from this pattern. The first occurs between beryllium and boron. In beryllium, the outer electron is in a $2s$ orbital, whereas in boron it enters the higher$-$energy $2p$ subshell. The $2p$ electron in boron is slightly further from the nucleus and partially shielded by the filled $2s$ subshell, so it experiences less effective nuclear attraction. As a result, it is more easily removed and boron's first ionization energy is lower than that of beryllium. The second deviation occurs between nitrogen and oxygen. In nitrogen, the three $2p$ electrons occupy separate orbitals, minimizing electron$-$electron repulsion. In oxygen, one of the $2p$ orbitals contains a pair of electrons, and the repulsion between these paired electrons slightly weakens the attraction to the nucleus. This makes it easier to remove one electron from oxygen, so its ionization energy is lower than nitrogen's despite the higher nuclear charge.

          $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation:}}$\
          As the number of protons increases, the nuclear charge becomes greater, but the added electrons occupy the same principal energy level, so the shielding effect remains relatively constant. This means that each successive outer electron experiences a stronger effective nuclear charge. The increasing effective nuclear charge draws the outermost electrons closer to the nucleus, reducing atomic radius and strengthening the electrostatic attraction between the nucleus and the valence electron. Consequently, more energy is required to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms, resulting in the general rise in first ionization energy across the period.

     2.   lower $\checkmark$

          $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation:}}$\
          The first ionization energies of the third period elements are lower than those of their corresponding second period elements. Although the effective nuclear charge experienced by the outermost electron is approximately the same for elements in the same group, the valence electron in a third period atom occupies a higher principal energy level $(n = 3)$ and is therefore farther from the nucleus. The increased distance between the nucleus and the valence electron reduces the strength of the electrostatic attraction. Consequently, less energy is required to remove the outermost electron, so the third period elements have lower first ionization energies than their second period counterparts.
