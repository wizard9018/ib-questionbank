1.  1.  $\ce{X^{2+}}\textrm{(g)} \longrightarrow \ce{X^{3+}}\textrm{(g)} + \ce{e^-} \: \checkmark \: \checkmark$

        *Award M1 for correct species and charges.*\
        *Award M2 for correct state symbols.*\
        *Accept any letter for the element symbol.*

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Ionization energy is the energy required to remove one electron from an atom or ion in the ground state in the gas phase. The third ionization energy refers to the removal of the third successive electron. The element has already lost two electrons to form $\ce{X^{2+}},$ so the third ionization removes one more electron to produce $\ce{X^{3+}}.$ Both the reactant ion and the product ion must be in the gaseous state, as ionization energies are defined for isolated gas$-$phase species.

    2.  magnesium / $\ce{Mg}$ $\checkmark$\
        large jump   between $2^{\textrm{nd}}$ and $3^{\textrm{rd}}$ IE; $3^{\textrm{rd}}$ electron  removed from inner energy level $\checkmark$\
        two valence electrons $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The successive ionization energy data shows a relatively small increase between the 1st and 2nd ionization energies, followed by a much larger increase between the 2nd and 3rd. This indicates that the first two electrons are removed from the outermost energy level, while the third electron is removed from a lower energy level that is closer to the nucleus and more tightly held. An element in period 3 with exactly two valence electrons is in Group 2, which is magnesium. The remaining eight electrons occupy the $n = 2$ and $n = 1$ energy levels, each showing a step increase as electrons are removed from progressively lower levels.

    3.  each successive electron is removed from a more positively charged ion $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        After each electron is removed, the ion carries a greater positive charge. The next electron must be pulled away from this increasingly positive ion, which requires more energy.

    4.  $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6 \: \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Magnesium has 12 electrons, with the electron configuration $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2.$ Removing the two $3s$ valence electrons produces $\ce{Mg^{2+}},$ which has the configuration $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6.$ This is isoelectronic with neon and represents a complete noble$-$gas core.

2.  1.  drop in ionization energy from $\ce{Mg}$ to $\ce{Al} \: \checkmark$\
        $3p$ electron in aluminium is partially shielded by the filled $3s$ subshell $\checkmark$\
        drop in ionization energy from $\ce{P}$ to $\ce{S} \: \checkmark$\
        fourth electron in the $3p$ subshell experiences increased electron$-$electron repulsion due to pairing $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The general trend across period 3 is an increase in first ionization energy due to increasing nuclear charge. Two irregularities in this trend provide evidence for $s$ and $p$ sublevels. The first occurs between magnesium and aluminium. In magnesium, the electron removed is from a $3s$ orbital, while in aluminium it is from a $3p$ orbital. The $3p$ orbital is at slightly higher energy and is partially shielded by the filled $3s$ subshell, so less energy is needed to remove the electron. The second irregularity occurs between phosphorus and sulfur. Phosphorus has three unpaired electrons in three separate $3p$ orbitals, but sulfur has a fourth $3p$ electron that must pair with one of the existing electrons. The repulsion between two electrons sharing the same orbital lowers the energy needed to remove one, producing the observed decrease. These two deviations from the expected trend are direct experimental evidence that the $n = 3$ energy level contains both $s$ and $p$ sublevels with different energies.

    2. Ar has highest effective nuclear charge, same number of shielding electrons $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Argon has 18 protons, the largest nuclear charge of any period 3 element. Its outer $3p$ electrons are shielded by the same inner electron core $(1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6)$ as the other period 3 elements. The combination of the highest nuclear charge and constant inner shielding means that argon's outer electrons experience the greatest effective nuclear charge in the period, requiring the most energy to remove.
