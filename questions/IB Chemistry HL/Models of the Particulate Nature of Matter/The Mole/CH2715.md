# CH2715

**Maximum mark: 11**

1. A student required $100.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ of a $1.00 \times 10^{-3} \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$ copper(II) sulfate working standard for a colorimetric analysis. A stock solution was first prepared by dissolving hydrated copper(II) sulfate, $\ce{CuSO4.5\,H2O},$ to make $250.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ of $0.1000 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$ solution. The tolerances of the available glassware are shown.

    | $\mathbf{Apparatus}$ | $\mathbf{Tolerance\textbf{ / cm}^3}$ |
    :-:|:-:
    | $1.00 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ pipette | $\pm 0.02$ |
    | $10.00 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ pipette | $\pm 0.05$ |
    | $100.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ volumetric flask | $\pm 0.20$ |

    **Table 1:** Tolerances of the volumetric glassware available in the laboratory.

    $\mbox{}\\[-25pts]$

    1. Calculate the mass of $\ce{CuSO4.5\,H2O}$ required to prepare the stock solution. $M_\textrm{r}(\ce{CuSO4.5\,H2O}) = 249.72.$ [1 mark]

    2. Describe how the stock solution is prepared from the weighed solid. [3 marks]

    3. Suggest why the hydrated salt is preferred to anhydrous $\ce{CuSO4}$ for preparing a standard solution. [1 mark]

2. The working standard can be prepared from the stock by two routes:

    $\mbox{}\\[-15pt]$

    -   **Route 1:** pipette $1.00 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ of stock into a $100.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ volumetric flask and fill to the mark.
    -   **Route 2:** pipette $10.00 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ of stock into a $100.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ volumetric flask and fill to the mark; then pipette $10.00 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ of this solution into a second $100.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ volumetric flask and fill to the mark.
    $\mbox{}\\[15pt]$

    1. Show that both routes give a final concentration of $1.00 \times 10^{-3} \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}.$ [2 marks]

    2. Calculate the total percentage uncertainty in the concentration of the standard prepared by **Route 1.** [1 mark]

    3. Calculate the total percentage uncertainty in the concentration of the standard prepared by **Route 2.** [2 marks]

    4. Deduce, with a reason, which route should be used to prepare the working standard. [1 mark]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.
    1. $m = 6.24 \textrm{\ g} \: \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Use the concentration and volume in $\textrm{dm}^3$ to find the moles of the hydrated salt.

        $n = 0.2500 \textrm{\ dm}^3 \times 0.1000 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3} = 0.02500 \textrm{\ mol}$

        Using the molar mass of the hydrated salt, $m = nM.$

        $m = 0.02500 \textrm{\ mol} \times 249.72 \textrm{\ g mol}^{-1} = 6.24 \textrm{\ g}$

    2. any **three** of the following:\
        $\mbox{}\\[-23pts]$

        -   dissolve the solid in less distilled water than the final volume $\checkmark$
        -   transfer the solution and all washings to a $250.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ volumetric flask $\checkmark$
        -   make up to the mark with distilled water  $\checkmark$
        -   stopper and invert $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Every step protects the amount of solute or the final volume, the two quantities that define concentration. The washings matter because any solution left in the beaker is weighed solute that never reaches the flask; filling to the mark calibrates the volume against the flask's certified graduation; inverting ensures the sample pipetted later is representative of the whole. A line drawn on a beaker cannot reproduce the $\pm 0.2 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ tolerance of the volumetric flask.

    3. anhydrous $\ce{CuSO4}$ absorbs water from the air, its mass does not give a reliable amount $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        A standard solution requires the weighed mass to convert exactly into an amount of substance. The pentahydrate has a definite, stable composition, so every gram contains a known amount of copper. The anhydrous salt gains mass from atmospheric moisture during weighing, and the gain is uncontrolled; the composition of what is on the balance is then unknown.

2.
    1. **Route 1:** $0.1000 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}\times \dfrac{1.00 \textrm{ cm}^3}{100.0 \textrm{ cm}^3} = 1.00 \times 10^{-3} \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3} \: \checkmark$
        $\mbox{}\\[10pt]$
        **Route 2:** $0.1000 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3} \times \dfrac{10.00 \textrm{ cm}^3}{100.0 \textrm{ cm}^3} \times \dfrac{10.00 \textrm{ cm}^3}{100.0 \textrm{ cm}^3} = 1.00 \times 10^{-3} \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3} \: \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Diluting a solution does not change the amount of solute, so the amount taken from the stock equals the amount in the diluted solution:

        $c_1V_1 = c_2V_2 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad c_2 = c_1 \times \dfrac{V_1}{V_2}$

        where $V_1$ is the volume pipetted and $V_2$ is the volume of the flask. Each dilution therefore multiplies the concentration by the ratio of these two volumes.

        For Route 1, one dilution is made:

        $c_2 = 0.1000 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3} \times \dfrac{1.00 \textrm{\ cm}^3}{100.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3} = 0.1000 \times 10^{-2} = 1.00 \times 10^{-3} \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$

        For Route 2, the first dilution gives an intermediate solution, and the second dilution is made from that solution:

        $c_{\textrm{intermediate}} = 0.1000 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3} \times \dfrac{10.00 \textrm{\ cm}^3}{100.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3} = 1.000 \times 10^{-2} \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$

        $c_2 = 1.000 \times 10^{-2} \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3} \times \dfrac{10.00 \textrm{\ cm}^3}{100.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3} = 1.00 \times 10^{-3} \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$

        Route 1 dilutes by a factor of 100 in one step; Route 2 dilutes by a factor of 10 twice.

    2. $2.2\%$ $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The concentration after dilution is $c_2 = c_1 \times \dfrac{V_{\textrm{pipette}}}{V_{\textrm{flask}}},$ a product and quotient, so the percentage uncertainties of the two volumes are added:

        pipette: $\dfrac{0.02 \textrm{\ cm}^3}{1.00 \textrm{\ cm}^3} \times 100\% = 2.0\%$

        flask: $\dfrac{0.20 \textrm{\ cm}^3}{100.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3} \times 100\% = 0.20\%$

        total: $2.0\% + 0.20\% = 2.2\%$

    3. one dilution: $0.70\%$ $\checkmark$ \
        $1.4\%$ $\checkmark$

        *Award [2] for correct final answer.*

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Each dilution uses the $10.00 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ pipette and a $100.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ flask:

        pipette: $\dfrac{0.05 \textrm{\ cm}^3}{10.00 \textrm{\ cm}^3} \times 100\% = 0.50\%$

        flask: $\dfrac{0.20 \textrm{\ cm}^3}{100.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3} \times 100\% = 0.20\%$

        one dilution: $0.50\% + 0.20\% = 0.70\%$

        The final concentration, $c_{\textrm{stock}} \times \dfrac{10.00}{100.0} \times \dfrac{10.00}{100.0},$ is a product and quotient of four measured volumes, so their percentage uncertainties are added. The two dilutions contribute equally:

        total: $0.70\% + 0.70\% = 1.4\%$

    4. Route 2 **AND** percentage uncertainty is smaller / solution concentration more precise $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Fewer steps does not mean lower uncertainty; the total depends on the size of each contribution. Route 1 is dominated by a single 2.0% term. Route 2 doubles the number of operations, but each carries a small relative uncertainty, and $2 \times 0.70\% = 1.4\% < 2.2\%.$ Serial dilution is used for very dilute standards because it avoids delivering a very small volume with a pipette.
