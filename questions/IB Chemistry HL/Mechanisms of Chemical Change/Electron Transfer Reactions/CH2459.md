# CH2459

Which species has the greatest tendency to be oxidised?

$\mbox{}\\[-15pts]$

| $\mathbf{Half{\text{--}}reaction}$ | $\mathbf{E^{\minuso}\,/\,\textbf{V}}$ |
|:-:|:-:|
| $\ce{X^{2+}}\textrm{(aq)} + 2\ce{\,e-} \rightleftharpoons \ce{X}\textrm{(s)}$ | $+0.21$ |
| $\ce{Y+}\textrm{(aq)} + \ce{e-} \rightleftharpoons \ce{Y}\textrm{(s)}$ | $+0.37$ |
| $\ce{Z^{3+}}\textrm{(aq)} + 3\ce{\,e-} \rightleftharpoons \ce{Z}\textrm{(s)}$ | $+0.12$ |

-   A.$\hspace{1.em}$Z

-   B.$\hspace{1.em}$Y

-   C.$\hspace{1.em}$X

-   D.$\hspace{1.em}\ce{Z^{3+}}$

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

**答案：A**

$\textrm{\underline{Explanation:}}$\
Half$-$reactions in data tables are written as reductions by convention. The more positive the $E^{\minuso}$ value, the greater the tendency for that reduction to occur. Conversely, the species on the right$-$hand side of the half$-$reaction with the least positive (or most negative) $E^{\minuso}$ has the greatest tendency to be oxidised.

Z has $E^{\minuso} = +0.12\textrm{ V},$ the lowest value in the table, so Z metal is most easily oxidised among the three metals. It would act as the anode in a cell paired with either X or Y.  $\ce{Z^{3+}}$ is the oxidised form of Z. It has already lost electrons and cannot be further oxidised under normal conditions.

Y has the highest $E^{\minuso}$ $(+0.37\textrm{ V})$ and therefore the greatest tendency to be reduced, not oxidised.
