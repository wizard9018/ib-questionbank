# CH0915

**Maximum mark: 8**

When compound **X** dissolves in water, the concentration of $\ce{H3O+}$ changes.

The following graph shows how the concentration changes as more compound **X** is dissolved.

![CH0915_Image1](images/CH0915_Image1.png)

1.  Compound **X** is a Brønsted-Lowry acid. State **one** piece of evidence from the graph that supports this conclusion. [1 mark]

2.  Explain how the graph indicates that compound **X** is a weak acid. [2 marks]

3.  The colour of compound **X** in water depends on the pH of the solution. This colour change makes it a suitable indicator for the titration of hydrochloric acid against dilute aqueous sodium hydroxide.

    1.  Explain why the pH of the colour change is linked to the $pK_a$ of compound **X**. [2 marks]

    2.  Suggest a value for the $pK_a$ of compound **X**, giving a reason for your answer. [2 marks]

    3.  Suggest why only a small amount of indicator should be added to the analyte in an acid-base titration. [1 mark]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  Increasing [**X**] causes $\ce{[H3O+]}$ to increase OR the graph shows a positive trend / correlation (so **X** must donate hydrogen ions). $\checkmark$

    *Award [0] if a specific feature of the graph is not described*

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation:}}$

    A Brønsted-Lowry acid is a species that donates a hydrogen ion in a chemical reaction. If a Brønsted-Lowry acid reacts with water, the hydrogen ion bonds with the $\ce{O}$ atom of water to produce the $\ce{H3O+}$ ion.

    The graph shows that increasing the concentration of **X** in solution increases the concentration of $\ce{H3O+}$ ions, so **X** must be donating $\ce{H+}$ ions. Thus, **X** is a Brønsted-Lowry acid.

2.  $\ce{[H3O+]}$ is less than [**X**] OR $\ce{[H3O+]}$ is only a (small) fraction of [**X**] OR the graph is not linear OR the gradient/slope of the graph is less than 1 $\checkmark$

    so **X** only partially dissociates OR some **X** units do not dissociate OR only some / a fraction of the **X** units donate protons / hydrogen ions / $\ce{H+}$ ions $\checkmark$

    *Award M1 if the fraction $\dfrac{\ce{[H3O+]}}{[\ce{X}]}$ is calculated for a specific data point; \
    Award M1 for a similar description indicating that [**X**] increases at a greater rate than $\ce{[H3O+]}$; \
    Award M2 if a general description of weak acid dissociation is given*.

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation:}}$

    An acid dissociates to form a $\ce{H+}$ (or $\ce{H3O+}$) ion and a deprotonated species, which is typically an anion.

    A weak acid is one that only partially dissociates, so that some of the original acid molecules are not converted to $\ce{H+}$ ions and anions.

    For example, ethanoic acid $\ce{(CH3COOH)}$ dissociates to form $\ce{H+}$ and ethanoate $\ce{(CH3COO^-)}$ ions, but the $\ce{CH3COO-}$ ions readily recombine with the $\ce{H+}$ ions to regenerate the $\ce{CH3COOH}$ molecules. Thus, only a fraction of the $\ce{CH3COOH}$ molecules are dissociated at equilibrium.

    Compound **X** must be a weak acid, as $\ce{[H3O+]}$ for a strong acid would be near or equal to the initial value of [**X**] and the graph would be linear.

3.
    1.  The colour change occurs when pH $= pK_a (\pm 1)$ $\checkmark$

        because the concentration of **X** and its anion / conjugate base are equal at this point OR **X** is half-dissociated at this point OR **X** becomes (much) less concentrated than its anion / conjugate base at this point $\checkmark$

        *Do **not** award M1 if the colour change is not explicitly linked to the pH matching the $pK_a$; \
        Do **not** award M2 if the relative concentrations of **X** and its conjugate base are not compared; \
        Do **not** award M2 for reference to the half-equivalence point if this term is not defined*.

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation:}}$

        If half of an acid $\ce{HA}$ dissociates, the concentrations of the acid and its conjugate base $\ce{A-}$ are equal. Thus, $K_a = \dfrac{\ce{[H+][A-]}}{\ce{[HA]}} = \dfrac{\ce{[H+][HA]}}{\ce{[HA]}} = \ce{[H+]}$.

        Likewise, at the point of half-dissociation, or half-equivalence point, the pH and $pK_a$ are equal. As the pH increases, [**X**] decreases until, when pH $= pK_a$, it becomes less concentrated than its conjugate base. The colour change occurs because the colour of the conjugate base becomes more strongly visible than that of the original acid.

    2.  7 $\checkmark$

        because the experiment is a strong acid - strong base titration OR a strong acid is reacted with a strong base (to produce a neutral solution) OR the conjugate acid and conjugate base are both weak $\checkmark$

        *Award M1 for any single value or range of values between 6 and 8; \
        Do **not** award M2 if the acid/base strength is given for only one acid, base or acid-base pair; \
        Do **not** award M2 if the relative strengths of the reactants or conjugate species are not compared*.

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation:}}$

        Hydrochloric acid $\ce{(HCl)}$ is a strong acid because the chloride $\ce{(Cl^-)}$ ion formed by its dissociation is a weak conjugate base.

        The chloride ion reacts very slowly with $\ce{H+}$ ions, so very little $\ce{HCl}$ is regenerated at equilibrium: the compound fully dissociates.

        Likewise, sodium hydroxide $\ce{(NaOH)}$ is a strong base with a weak conjugate acid: all of the salt dissociates into separate ions.

        The reaction of a strong acid and a strong base gives a neutral solution, with a pH close to 7. The colour change of the indicator occurs in the range pH $= pK_a (\pm 1)$, so the $pK_a$ of the indicator must be between $6$ and 8.

    3.  The indicator (is an acid so) could react with the base OR the indicator could increase the concentration / number of $\ce{H+}$ / $\ce{H3O+}$ ions (in solution) $\checkmark$

        *Award [0] if the ability of the indicator to act as an acid is not described*.

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation:}}$

        The indicator is itself an acid, so can react with sodium hydroxide to form a salt and water. The use of a large indicator concentration would increase the concentration of $\ce{H+}$ ions available for reaction with the titrant, so the measured titre would be too large and the concentration of the acid analyte would be overestimated.
