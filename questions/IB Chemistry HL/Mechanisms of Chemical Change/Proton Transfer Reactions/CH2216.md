## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.
    1.  proton donor $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Explanation:}}$

        The Bronsted$\text{--}$Lowry theory states that acids are proton donors. Malic acid can act as a proton donor due to the presence of carboxylic groups in its structure.

        $\ce{H2C4H4O5(aq) + H2O(l) <=> HC4H4O5-(aq) + H3O+(aq)}$

    2.  $\ce{H2C4H4O5(aq) + H2O(l) <=> HC4H4O5-(aq) + H3O+(aq)}\ \checkmark$

        partial dissociation $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Explanation:}}$

        Malic acid does not undergo a complete dissociation into its ions, it establishes an equilibrium as shown here:

        $\ce{H2C4H4O5(aq) + H2O(l) <=> HC4H4O5-(aq) + H3O+(aq)}$

        The equilibrium constant is small, $K \ll 1,$ indicating that very little of the acid hydrolyzes to form its conjugate base and hydronium ion.

    3.  $\ce{HC4H4O5-}\ \checkmark$

        $\ce{HC4H4O5-(aq) <=> H+(aq) + C4H4O5^2-(aq)}\ \checkmark$

        $\ce{HC4H4O5-(aq) + H+(aq) <=> H2C4H4O5(aq)}\ \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Explanation:}}$

        The conjugate base is produced after donating a hydrogen ion.

        $\ce{\underset{\large acid}{H2C4H4O5(aq)} + \underset{\large base}{H2O(l)} <=> \underset{\large conjugate base}{HC4H4O5-(aq)} + \underset{\large conjugate acid}{H3O+(aq)}}$

        In the case of the diprotic acid, the conjugate base produced  $\ce{HC4H4O5-}$ can both donate and accept protons. It is amphiprotoic as shown here:

        ***acidic behavior (proton donor):***

        $\ce{HC4H4O5-(aq) <=> H+(aq) + C4H4O5^2-(aq)}$

        ***basic behavior (proton acceptor):***

        $\ce{HC4H4O5-(aq) + H+(aq) <=> H2C4H4O5(aq)}$

2.
    1.  $M_r = 134.10\ \checkmark$

        $[\ce{H2C4H4O5}] = 3.60\times 10^{-2}\text{ mol dm}^{-3}\ \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Explanation:}}$

        To determine the molar concentration of malic acid in the apple juice sample, it is  necessary to calculate its relative molar mass, $M_r.$ Malic acid has the molecular formula $\ce{H2C4H4O5};$ therefore,

        $M_r = (6)(1.01) + (4)(12.01) + (5)(16.00) = 134.10$

        In $1\text{ dm}^3,$ the juice contains 4.83 g of malic acid, the molar concentration can be found by converting from mass to amount:

        $C = \dfrac{4.83\text{ g dm}^{-3}}{134.10\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 3.60\times 10^{-2}\text{ mol dm}^{-3}$

    2.  prediction is incorrect $\checkmark$

        malic acid is diprotic and weak, $[\ce{H+}] \neq 3.60\times 10^{-2}\text{ mol dm}^{-3}\ \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Explanation:}}$

        The student's prediction rests on the assumption that each molecule of malic acid donates one proton completely, so that the hydronium ion concentration equals the concentration of the acid $([\ce{H+}] = 3.60\times 10^{-2}\text{ mol dm}^{-3}).$ The pH calculated on that basis is indeed $\text{pH} = -\log(3.60\times 10^{-2}) = 1.44.$

        That overlooks two key facts:
        -   Malic acid is diprotic. If it were to dissociate fully, it would supply two protons per molecule, doubling $[\ce{H+}]$ to $7.2\times 10^{-2}\text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ and lowering the pH.

        -   Both acidic protons are weakly dissociated. The first ionisation constant of malic acid is small $(K_a \ll 1)$ so $[\ce{H+}] \ll 3.6\times 10^{-2}.$ Contribution from the second dissociation raises [H+] only marginally, so the pH is well above 2.

        Because malic acid is weak and because even its first proton is only partially released, the actual pH of the stock solution will be considerably higher than 1.44. The student's prediction therefore underestimates the pH.

3.
    1.  not possible, concentrations are unknown, not enough information $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Explanation:}}$

        The pH values alone are insufficient to label the bottles with confidence. Although nitric acid is a strong monoprotic acid and malic acid is a weak diprotic acid, their measured pH depends not only on intrinsic acid strength but also on the concentration of each sample. A sufficiently dilute solution of the strong acid could exhibit a higher pH than a more concentrated solution of the weak acid; likewise, the larger volume of Bottle A tells us nothing about its concentration. Because neither concentration nor total amount (in moles) of the substances is provided, the numerical pH values cannot distinguish which bottle contains nitric acid and which contains malic acid. Further analytical testing, for example, a titration or a metal$-$reactivity test would be required.

    2.  $\mbox{}\\[-25pts]$
        ![CH2216ms](images/CH2216ms.png)

        both axes should be correctly labelled: vertical axis as pH and horizontal axis as volume $\checkmark$

        initial $\text{pH} \approx 2$ **AND** $\text{pH} = 7$ at the  equivalence point $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Explanation:}}$

        A properly drawn curve must have both axes labelled precisely: the $y-$axis as pH and the $x-$axis as volume of NaOH added / $\textrm{cm}^3.$ It will contain three recognizable regions. The curve starts in the strongly acidic range (here about pH 2). Here, hydronium ions are in excess and adding NaOH will produce only a slight rise in pH.  As the number of moles of $\ce{OH-}$ approaches the initial moles of $\ce{H+},$ the net hydronium concentration falls sharply. The result is an almost vertical segment in the graph. For any strong acid/strong base pair, the midpoint of this vertical jump is the equivalence point and must be drawn at pH 7. Beyond the equivalence point, there is an excess of hydroxide ions. The curve flattens into an upper plateau as the solution pH approaches the pH of the NaOH solution.
