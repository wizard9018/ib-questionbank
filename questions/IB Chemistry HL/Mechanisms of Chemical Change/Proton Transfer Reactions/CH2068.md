# CH2068

A student produced the following curve upon titrating $30.0\text{ cm}^3$ of nitric (V) acid, $\ce{HNO3},$ with $0.150\text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ sodium hydroxide, NaOH.

![CH2068titration](images/CH2068titration.png)

1.  Determine the concentration of the nitric (V) acid, $\ce{HNO3}.$ [1 mark]

2.  Sketch, on the graph above, the pH curve expected if the concentration of NaOH is doubled. [3 marks]

3.  State and explain one similarity and one difference in the pH curve if the nitric (V) acid were replaced with nitric (III) acid, $\ce{HNO2}$ of the same concentration.  [3 marks]

4.  Identify an indicator in Section 18 of the IB Chemistry data booklet that could be used to determine the equivalence point of the titration of $\ce{HNO2(aq)}$ and NaOH(aq). [1 mark]

5.  Using the formula HIn to represent the indicator, explain why the indicator changes colour during the titration.    [2 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  $C_\ce{acid} = \dfrac{C_\ce{base}V_\ce{base}}{V_\ce{acid}} = \dfrac{(0.150\text{ mol dm}^{-3})(24.0\text{ cm}^3)}{30.0\text{ cm}^3} = 0.120\text{ mol dm}^{-3}\ \checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$

    Equivalence occurs when the moles of acid are equal (or stoichiometrically equivalent) to the moles of base.

    $n_\ce{acid} = n_\ce{base}$

    $C_\ce{acid}V_\ce{acid} = C_\ce{base}V_\ce{base}$

    The amount of base added at equivalence is equal to the product of the concentration of the base times the volume at equivalence, the steepest part of the titration curve $V_\ce{base} = 24.0\text{ cm}^3.$ Isolating $C_\ce{acid}$ and substituting in the values,

    $C_\ce{acid} = \dfrac{C_\ce{base}V_\ce{base}}{V_\ce{acid}} = \dfrac{(0.150\text{ mol dm}^{-3})(24.0\text{ cm}^3)}{30.0\text{ cm}^3)} = 0.120\text{ mol dm}^{-3}$

    Same pH at $V=0\ \checkmark$

    Equivalence at $12\text{ cm}^3\ \checkmark$

    Higher pH at $50\text{ cm}^3\ \checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$

    At the start of the titration, before any base is added, the pH is determined by the acid solution alone. The initial pH will be unaffected by a change in the concentration of the base.

    When the concentration of base doubles, the volume at equivalence will half. The same number of moles of base are required to neutralize the acid solution. The most notable change in the titration curve is a shift so that the steep rise occurs at $12.0\text{ cm}^3.$

    After equivalence, the pH will be higher as a more concentrated base is being added to the solution; $\ce{[OH-]}$ will be greater.

3.  ***Similarity:***  same equivalence point $\checkmark$

    ***Difference:*** higher initial pH / point of inflection at $12\text{ cm}^3$ / higher pH at equivalence $\checkmark$

    Nitric (III) acid is weak so it partially dissociates (higher initial pH) to create its conjugate base (higher pH at equivalence) forming a buffer solution around half$\text{--}$equivalence $\checkmark$

    *Note: M3 can be awarded if the difference noted in M2 is explained.*

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$

    ***Equivalence Point:***\
    The equivalence point occurs when the moles of acid and base are equal (or stoichiometrically equivalent). Since the straong acid $\ce{HNO3}$ and the weak acid $\ce{HNO2}$ have the same volume and concentration, they contain an equal number of moles of acid. As a result, the equivalence point for both titrations will occur at $24.0\text{ cm}^3.$

    ***Initial pH:***\
    The titration curves will look significantly different due to the difference in acid strength. Nitric (V) acid fully dissociates in water

    $\ce{HNO3 + H2O -> NO3- + H3O+}$

    where the concentration of $\ce{[H3O+]}$ is equal to the initial concentration of the acid. By contrast, nitric (III) acid  is a weak acid which only partially dissociates in water

    $\ce{HNO2 + H2O <=> NO2- + H3O+} \quad K_a = 4.3\times 10^{-4}$

    For a weak acid, the concentration of hydronium ions is much lower than the initial acid concentration. This results in a higher initial pH for the $\ce{HNO2}$ solution compared to $\ce{HNO3},$ as there are fewer hydronium ions present.

    ***Buffer region:***\
    Another key difference in the titration curves arises from the presence of a buffer region in the weak acid titration. As $\ce{HNO2}$ is  neutralized, a mixture of the weak acid $\ce{HNO2}$ and its conjugate base $\ce{NO2^-}$ forms, creating a buffer solution that resists pH changes. This is reflected in the titration curve as a flattened region before the equivalence point, where the gradient reaches a minimum at the half$-$equivalence point. In contrast, strong acid titration curves do not show a buffer zone, as there is no weak acid/conjugate base equilibrium present.

    ***pH at equivalence:***\
       The pH at the equivalence point differs significantly between the two titrations. For the strong acid, the reaction at equivalence is:

    $\ce{H3O+ + OH- -> 2 H2O}$

    Since $\ce{HNO3}$ completely dissociates, at equivalence, only $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$ remain in solution, neither of which affects pH. Therefore, the pH at the equivalence point is exactly 7.0.  For the weak acid $\ce{HNO2},$ the reaction at equivalence is:

    $\ce{HNO2 + OH- -> NO2- + H2O}$

    At the equivalence point, the solution contains $\ce{NO2-}$ ions, which act as a weak base and hydrolyze water:

    $\ce{NO2- + H2O <=> HNO2 + OH-}$

    This produces $\ce{OH-}$ ions, making the solution slightly basic. As a result, the pH at the equivalence point is greater than 7, typically around $8-9.$

4.  Phenol red or phenolphthalein $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$

    The optimal indicator should change color at the equivalence point of the titration. The equivalence point in this titration occurs above 7 $(\text{pH} = 8.14)$ so the indicator needs to change colors in alkaline solution.  In Section 18 of the IB Chemistry data booklet phenol red $(6.4-8.0)$ and phenolphtalein $(8.0-10.0)$ are the best options.

5.  $\ce{\underset{\large color 1}{HIn} + H2O <=> \underset{\large color 2}{In-} + H3O+}\ \checkmark$

    As $\ce{[H3O+]}$ increases, reaction shifts left to appear color 1; as $\ce{[H3O+]}$ decreases, reaction shifts right to appear color 2 $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further explanation:}}$

    An indicator is a weak acid that has different colors in its protonated, $\ce{HIn},$ and deprotonated, $\ce{In-},$ forms. During a titration, as the pH of the solution changes, the equilibrium shifts, causing a visible color change.

    $\ce{\underset{\large color 1}{HIn} + H2O <=> H3O+ + \underset{\large color 2}{In-}}$

    When the coecntration of hydronium is high, the equilibrium lies to the left, and the solution shows color 1. As base is adde, the equilibrium shifts to the right and color 2 dominates.  For a well chosen indicator, the equivalence point of the titration will occur when $\ce{[HIn]} = \ce{[In-]},$ or when the pH of the solution is equal to the p$K_a$ of the inidcator.
