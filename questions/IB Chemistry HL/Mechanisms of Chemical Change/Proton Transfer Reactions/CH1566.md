# CH1566

**Maximum mark: 18**

Hydrogen fluoride is a colourless gas. 

1.     Hydrogen fluoride is bubbled in water to form hydrofluoric acid.

    1.  State the chemical reaction of equilibrium taking place when the gas dissolves. [1 mark]

    2.  A titration of $100\text{\ cm}^3$ of the resulting solution is performed using $1.50\text{\ mol dm}^{-3}$ NaOH(aq) to determine the initial concentration of hydrofluoric acid. The NaOH(aq) volume used to reach the endpoint is $167.0\text{\ cm}^3.$ Deduce the concentration of hydrofluoric acid. Report your result to three significant figures. [2 marks]

    3.  The $\text{p}K_a$ of hydrofluoric acid is 3.17. Calculate the pH of the hydrofluoric acid solution. [3 marks]

    4.  Suggest a suitable acid$-$base indicator for the titration described in (a) (ii). [1 mark]

    $\ce{HF(g) <=> H+(aq) + F-(aq)}$

    1.  Deduce the effect of this addition on the magnitude of the equilibrium constant. [1 mark]

    

    2.  Explain the effect of this addition on the position of the equilibrium. [2 marks]

    3.  Predict and explain the effect of adding NaF on the pH of the solution [2 marks]

1.  

    1.  $\ce{HF(g) <=> HF(aq)} \hspace{0.5em}\checkmark$ 

        $\underline{\textrm{Explanation:}}$

        

        Hydrogen fluoride dissolves in water. It goes from the gaseous state into the aqueous state.  As a weak acid, HF will not dissociate in water to from ions.

    2. 

        $C_\ce{HF} = \dfrac{(1.50\text{\ mol dm}^{-3})(167.0\text{\ cm}^3)}{100\text{\ cm}^3}\hspace{0.5em}\checkmark$ 

        

        $2.51\text{\ mol dm}^{-3}\hspace{0.5em}\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Explanation:}}$

        

        The chemical reaction taking place is a neutralization reaction.

        $\ce{HF(aq) + NaOH(g) -> NaF(aq) + H2O(l)}$

        The endpoint should be close to the equivalence point in a titration curve. At the equivalence point, the amount in moles of HF is the same as the amount in moles of NaOH. We can use the relationships below.

        $n_\ce{HF} = n_\ce{NaOH}$        

        $C_\ce{HF} V_\ce{HF} = C_\ce{NaOH} V_\ce{NaOH}$

        $C_\ce{HF} \times 100\text{\ cm}^3 = 1.50 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3} \times 167.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3$

        $C_\ce{HF} = \dfrac{1.50 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}\times  167.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3}{100 \textrm{\ cm}^3}=2.51 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$

    3.  $K_a = 6.76\times 10^{-4}\ \checkmark$

        $[\ce{H+}] = \sqrt{(6.76\times 10^{-4})(2.51)} = 0.0412\text{\ mol dm}^{-3}\ \checkmark$

        $\text{pH} = - \log (0.0409) = 1.39\ \checkmark$

        *Note: Allow any pH from 1.37 to 1.41. Do not penalize students who apply the quadratic expression in their procedures.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Explanation:}}$

        

        HF should be considered a weak acid, and its partial dissociation must be analyzed, as shown below. 

        ||||||||

        |:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:

        R $\hspace{1em}$| $\ce{HF(aq)}$ |$\ce{<=>}$| $\ce{H+(aq)}$ | + | $\ce{F-(aq)}$ |

        I $\hspace{1em}$| $2.51$ || |||

        C $\hspace{1em}$| $-x$ || $+x$||$+x$|

        E $\hspace{1em}$| $2.51-x$ || $x$||$x$|

        

        $K_a = \dfrac{{[\ce{H+}][\ce{F-}]}}{[\ce{HF}]}$

        $K_a = 10^{-3.17} = 6.76 \times 10^{-4}$

        $6.76\times 10^{-4} = \dfrac{x^2}{2.51 -x}$

        We can neglect the dissociated amount when compared to the initial concentration.

        $6.76 \times 10^{-4} \approx \dfrac{x^2}{2.51}$

        $x \approx \sqrt{(6.76 \times 10^{-4})(2.51)}$ = 0.0412

        $ [\ce{H+}] = x = 0.0412 \text{\ mol dm}^{-3}$

        $\text{pH} = -\log (0.0412) = 1.39$

    1.  No effect over $K_c \ \checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Explanation:}}$

        

        The addition of a substance does not affect the $K_c$ magnitude. $K_c$ is temperature$\text{--}$dependent only.

    2.  increase in $\ce{F-}$ ions $\checkmark$

        shifts the equilibrium to the left/to the reactant side $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Explanation:}}$

            

        Sodium fluoride introduces $\ce{Na+}$ and additional $\ce{F-}$ to the solution. Adding fluoride ions disrupts the equilibrium, shifting the chemical equilibrium to the left. By doing so, the concentration of fluoride ions is decreased, and equilibrium is reached again.

    

    3.  The pH increases $\checkmark$

        The reaction shifts to the left/reactant side decreasing the $\ce{H+}\ \checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Explanation:}}$

        

        The shift to the left reduced not only the concentration of fluoride ions but also the concentration of hydrogen ions. If there is a decrease in the $\ce{H+},$ there must be an increase in the pH.

3.  Boiling points increase going down group 17 (from HCl to HI) $\checkmark$

    Greater molar mass /number of electrons going down group 17 $\checkmark$

    Greater London dispersion forces so harder to vaporize $\checkmark$

    HF is an exception to the trend with the highest boiling point. Molecules of HF can form strong hydrogen bonds between them $\checkmark$

    $\underline{\textrm{Explanation:}}$

    

    The trend is that the boiling points increase going down the group except for HF. As the molar mass (or the number of electrons) increases, the electron cloud gets bigger, and so does its polarizability. The greater the molar mass, the greater the London dispersion forces. More energy is needed to overcome attractive forces between molecules to transition from liquid to gas.

    Hydrogen fluoride does not follow the trend, and its boiling point is higher than expected, as it can form hydrogen bonds between its molecules. Hydrogen bonds can be produced when molecules present $\ce{F-H},$ $\ce{N-H},$ or $\ce{O-H}$ bonds within their structure. The high polarization of this bond allows the formation of strong intermolecular forces.
