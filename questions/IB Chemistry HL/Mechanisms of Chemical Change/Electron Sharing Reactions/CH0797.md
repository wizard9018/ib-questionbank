## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.   **2-chlorobutane**.

     2-chloro prefix; $\checkmark$

     Name ends with butane. $\checkmark$

     *Accept “2-chloro butane” and “2-chloro-butane”;*\
     *Do not award M1 if other prefixes are included.*

     $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

     The molecule contains four carbon atoms, so the stem of the name is *but-*. The carbon atoms are linked by single bonds, making it a derivative of an alkane, so the suffix is *-ane*. Finally, the compound is a chloroalkane with the functional group on the second carbon atom of the chain, so the name must begin with the prefix *2-chloro-*.

     Note that the carbon atoms are labelled from the end such that the numbered position of the functional group is as low as possible; thus, the compound is 2-chlorobutane rather than 3-chlorobutane.

2.
     1. The breaking of a (covalent) bond to produce (two) radicals / where one (bonding) electron is transferred to each atom in the bond. $\checkmark$

        *Award [0] if answer omits reference to a bond breaking process;\
        Award [0] if the formation of radicals is not stated or described.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        Fission is another term for a bond-breaking process. When a covalent bond is broken, the two bonding electrons may be transferred to one of the atoms in the bond or separated, such that each atom in the bond acquires an unpaired electron. The former process is termed heterolytic and typically occurs in solution, while the latter is termed homolytic and typically occurs in the gas phase. Homolytic fission produces radicals.

    2.  Initiation: ![CH0797-MSbii-1](images/CH0797-MSbii-1.png) $\checkmark$

        Propagation: ![CH0797-MSbii-2](images/CH0797-MSbii-2.png) $\checkmark$

        Termination: ![CH0797-MSbii-3](images/CH0797-MSbii-3.png) $\checkmark$

        All reaction labels correct $\checkmark$

        *Accept alternative representations of radicals, e.g. full structural formulas, provided the position of the removed H atom is clear;\
        Accept representation of product in the termination step as **A** or similar;\
        Accept representations of organic radicals with the single electron labelled at the end of the chain, provided the position of the removed H atom is clear;\
        Award M3 if structure of organic radical is based on a follow-through error from the propagation step;\
        Award M3 if the position of the removed H atom in the reacting organic radical is unclear;\
        Do **not** award M2 if the position of the removed H atom in the reacting organic radical is unclear;\
        Do **not** award M2 if the structure of the organic radical does not correspond to the final product, e.g. $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2\cdotp}$;\
        Do **not** accept equations in which the unpaired electrons of radical reactants and products are not labelled as single dots.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        The chlorination of an alkane is a free radical substitution process, and involves three steps.

        In the initiation step, the $\ce{Cl-Cl}$ bond of chlorine is broken by UV light to produce two chlorine radicals.

        In the propagation step, a chlorine radical reacts with the alkane to produce HCl and an alkyl radical.

        In the termination step, the alkyl and remaining chlorine radicals combine to form a new $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond, generating a chloroalkane as the only product.

        The unpaired electrons of the reactants and products are labelled as a single dot ($\cdotp$). In this reaction, the product is the 2-chloroalkane compound **A**, so the H atom must be removed from the second carbon atom of the chain in the propagation step, and the single dot should be labelled in this position.

    3.  Other isomers / other chloroalkanes / 1-chlorobutane could form;

        **OR**

        two alkyl / organic radicals could react together (in the termination step);

        **OR**

        the alkane could react with multiple chlorine radicals;

        **OR**

        two H atoms (in the alkane) could be replaced with Cl atoms. $\checkmark$

        *Accept similar statements indicating that other products could form;\
        Do not accept answers in which a specific alternative reaction product or pathway is not specified.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        The $\ce{C-H}$ bonds in the alkane are similarly reactive, so it is possible for the molecule to undergo multiple propagation and termination reactions to produce a compound with more than one $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond. It is also possible for a $\ce{C-H}$ bond on the first carbon atom of the chain to be replaced, producing an isomer of compound **A**, 1-chlorobutane.

        Finally, two alkyl radicals could combine in an alternative termination reaction to produce longer-chain molecules, such as octane, 3-methylheptane or 3,4-dimethylhexane. These longer-chain alkanes could also be chlorinated. The production of other alkanes and chloroalkanes would lower the yield of the desired product, compound **A**.

3.
     1. $\ce{NaOH}$ / $\ce{KOH}$ / $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$. $\checkmark$

        *Accept other hydroxide salts;\
        Award [0] if the formula of the salt is not given;\
        Award [0] if the formula does not include a cation with the correct stoichiometry.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        An alcohol is produced by replacing the Cl group with an OH group in a nucleophilic substitution reaction. The nucleophile in this reaction is the hydroxide ion, $\ce{OH-}$. This is supplied in the form of a hydroxide salt such as sodium hydroxide, NaOH.

    2.  Hydroxyl. $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        The functional group of an alcohol group is termed a hydroxyl group, rather than an alcohol group. The suffix -yl is included to indicate that the O atom forms a covalent bond with a C atom of the organic molecule.

    3.  The boiling point of compound **C** is higher (than that of compound **A**), $\checkmark$

        because compound **C** forms hydrogen bonds AND compound **A** forms (weaker) dipole-dipole forces. $\checkmark$

        *Do not award M1 if the identity of the compound with the higher boiling point is unclear;\
        Do not award M2 unless the intermolecular forces of both compounds are clearly identified*.

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        The boiling point of a compound is higher if more heat energy is needed to separate the molecules by breaking the intermolecular bonds between them. Compound **C** contains an OH group, so molecules of the compound can interact via strong hydrogen bonds.

        In compound **A**, the $\ce{C-Cl}$ is polar due to the electronegativity difference between the C and Cl atoms, so the $\delta^+$ C atom of one molecule can interact with the $\delta^-$ Cl atom of another. However, these dipole-dipole interactions are weaker than the hydrogen bonds of compound **C**, so the boiling point of compound **A** is lower.
