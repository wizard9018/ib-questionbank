1.  2 $\checkmark$\
    atom is bonded to four different groups $\checkmark$

    $\underline{\textrm{Further explanation:}}$\
    A chiral molecule is one that is not superimposable with its mirror image. A molecule is chiral if it contains a carbon atom bonded to four different groups.

    Carbon atom 2 is bonded to a $\ce{CH2Cl}$ group, an OH group, a $\ce{CH2CH2OH}$ group and an H atom, so is chiral. Each of the other C atoms is bonded to two H atoms, so cannot be chiral.

2.  reduction $\checkmark$

    $\underline{\textrm{Further explanation:}}$\
    Step 2 converts a carboxylic acid to an alcohol. This reaction requires the strong reducing agent lithium aluminium hydride.

        $\underline{\textrm{Further explanation:}}$\
        In the electrophilic addition reaction, $\ce{Cl2}$ approaches the alkene group and gains an induced dipole as the $\ce{Cl-Cl}$ bonding electrons are electrostatically repelled by the electron-rich $\ce{C=C}$ bond.

        The $\ce{C=C}$ bond breaks, donating a bonding electron pair to break the $\ce{Cl-Cl}$ bond and form a new $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond with the $\delta^+$ Cl atom. The other C atom of the alkene group is positively charged.

    2.  secondary carbocation is  more stable than primary  $\checkmark$\
        neighbouring alkyl groups stabilise positive charge by positive inductive effect / hyperconjugation $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further explanation:}}$\
        When the $\ce{C=C}$ bond breaks and a new $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond forms, a carbocation is produced. The positive charge is more stable if it forms on C atom 2 as this is a secondary C atom, with more alkyl groups to stabilise the charge.

        The stabilisation occurs via the positive inductive effect, otherwise known as hyperconjugation, in which electron density from neighbouring single bonds is donated to the positive charge. H atoms bonded to a cationic C atom cannot help to stabilise the charge because they form no additional bonds from which electron density can be donated. The alternative carbocation, featuring a positive charge on the primary C atom 1, would therefore be less stable.

    3.  water $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further explanation:}}$\
        The carbocation intermediate in this reaction must react with a nucleophile to produce a compound in which all the C atoms have four bonds. The ion could react with a chloride ion, generated when the $\ce{Cl-Cl}$ bond was broken in the first step of the reaction.

        However, if the reactants are dissolved in water, the solvent molecules are present at a much higher concentration than the chloride ions. Thus, the carbocation is more likely to accept a lone electron pair from the O atom of a water molecule to produce a $\ce{C-O}$ bond. The resulting $\ce{C-OH2^+}$ group is easily deprotonated by another water molecule to produce a hydroxyl ($\ce{C-OH}$) group.
