        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The electronic configuration of nitrogen can be determined from its number of electrons following the Aufbau principle. The atomic number of nitrogen is 7. Nitrogen has 7 protons and 7 electrons. Its electronic configuration is $1s^2\ 2s^2\ 2p^3.$

    2.  $\ce{NO2}\ \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        If N has oxidation state +4, it must be combined with two oxide ions, $\ce{O^2-}.$ As a result, the formula of the oxide is $\ce{NO2}.$

    3.  $\ce{NO2}$ is an acidic oxide / student is incorrect $\checkmark$\
        $\ce{2 NO2(g) + H2O(l) -> HNO3(aq) + HNO2(aq)}\ \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Nonmetal oxides are generally acidic while metal oxides are basic. Here, nitrogen (IV) oxide disproportionates in water to from the strong acid $\ce{HNO3}$ and the weak acid $\ce{HNO2}.$

    4.  $\ce{2 K(s) + F2(g) -> 2 KF(s)}\ \checkmark\ \checkmark$

        *Award M1 for the correct, balanced equation and M2 for the state symbols.*

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Element $\textbf{X}$ is fluorine, and diatomic fluorine is a gas at STP. Potassium is a metal at STP, and potassium fluoride, is an ionic solid.

    5.  reaction is faster with K than Li $\checkmark$\
        K is more reactive / has a larger atomic radius / lower ionization energy than Li $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further explanation:}}$\
        The reaction between potassium and fluorine is more vigorous than the reaction between lithium and fluorine. This is because reactivity in Group 1 metals increases as you move down the group. Potassium has a larger atomic radius than lithium, so its outermost electron is farther from the nucleus and less strongly attracted. As a result, potassium has a lower first ionization energy, making it easier to lose its outer electron. Since Group 1 metals react by losing an electron to form a positive ion, the lower ionization energy of potassium means it reacts more readily.

    *Award M1 for a very high $\textrm{IE}_2$ for lithium.*\
    *Award M2 for all $\textrm{IE}_2 > \textrm{IE}_1.$*\
    *Award M3 for increasing trend from Be to Ne.*

    $\textrm{\underline{Further explanation:}}$\
    The second ionization energy is the removal of an electron from a singly$\text{--}$charged cation:

    $\ce{X+(g) -> X^2+(g) + e-}$

    As such, it always requires more energy than the first ionization energy to overcome the additional attraction between the electron and the cation.

    Ionization energy increases across the periodic table because atoms have a constant number of shielding electrons but an increasing number of protons.  Generally, the second ionization energy will also increase across the period.

    Lithium is an important exception to this trend. Its electron configuration is $1s^2\ 2s^1.$ The first ionization removes the $2s$ electron, forming $\ce{Li+}$ with a stable, helium-like $1s^2$ configuration. The second ionization requires removing a $1s$ electron, which is much closer to the nucleus and experiences minimal shielding. As a result, the $\mathrm{IE}_2$ of lithium is high compared to the rest of the period.

3.  same shielding electrons, more protons, so $Z_\text{eff}$ increases across Period 2 $\checkmark$

    |$\hspace{0em}$|![CH2144cans](images/CH2144cans.png) |$\checkmark$
    |:-:|:-:|:-:|

    *Award M2 for an increasing trend from Group 1 to Group 18.*

    $\textrm{\underline{Further explanation:}}$\
    The effective nuclear charge is the net positive charge experienced by a valence electron. It is calculated as the atomic number minus the number of shielding electrons$\text{--}$those in lower energy levels.

    Across Period 2, the number of inner (core) electrons remains constant at two, while the nuclear charge  increases from 3 (for Li) to 10 (for Ne).  As a result, the effective nuclear charge increases across the period

    For example, lithium has $Z_\text{eff} = 3-2=1$ while neon has $Z_\text{eff} = 10-2=8.$ This produces a linear increase in the effective nuclear charge across the period.

4.  Period 3 elements have more protons and more shielding electrons, same $Z_{\textrm{eff}}$ in each Group $\checkmark$

    |$\hspace{0em}$| ![CH2144dans](images/CH2144dans.png) |$\checkmark\checkmark$
    |:-:|:-:|:-:|

    *Award M2 for a line that matches the line drawn in part (c).*

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Information:}}$\
    Period 3 elements have more protons and more inner (shielding) electrons than Period 2 elements, but within the same group, the effective nuclear charge remains the same. This is because although the nuclear charge increases, the number of shielding electrons also increases proportionally.

    For example, both sodium and lithium are in Group 1.

    Li: $\textcolor{red}{1s^2}\ 2s^1 \quad Z_\text{eff} = 3-\textcolor{red}{2} = +1$

    Na: $\textcolor{red}{1s^2\ 2s^2\ 2p^6}\ 3s^1 \quad Z_\text{eff} = 11-\textcolor{red}{10} = +1$

    As a result, the shape and position of the Period 3 curve closely match that of Period 2 when plotted against group number. This demonstrates that elements in the same group experience similar effective nuclear charges, despite being in different periods.
