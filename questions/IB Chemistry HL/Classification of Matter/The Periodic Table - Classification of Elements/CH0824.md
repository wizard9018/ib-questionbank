1.  Shielding is the phenomenon where the negatively charged electrons in an atom's inner shells weaken the positively charged nucleus effect by reducing the attraction between the nucleus and the outermost electrons in the atom's valence shell. $\checkmark$

    The additional electrons in the transition metals are added to the inner $3d$ orbital. $\checkmark$

    Although there is an increase in nuclear charge across the $d$ elements, the attraction of the nucleus for the valence electrons is decreased by the increased shielding generated by the additional d electrons. $\checkmark$

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

    Shielding affects trends like atomic radius by reducing the effective nuclear charge experienced by the valence electrons, which in turn affects the strength of the attraction between the electrons and the nucleus.

    Across period 4 of the periodic table, the atomic radii of scandium, titanium, and vanadium are all very similar, despite the fact that the number of valence electrons in the outermost shell increases from 2 to 5. This is because the additional electrons are added to the d orbitals, which are more shielded from the nucleus by the electrons in the inner shells.

    2.  The transition metals, which are found in the middle of the periodic table, have partially filled d orbitals that are involved in metallic bonding. $\checkmark$

        The partially filled $d$ orbitals allow for stronger and more complex bonding interactions compared to the $s$ orbitals of the group 1 and 2 metals. $\checkmark$

        -   Higher melting point
        -   Greater hardness/strength
        -   Increased electrical conductivity
        -   Increased ductility/malleability

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        Higher melting and boiling points: Transition metals tend to have higher melting and boiling points compared to group 1 and 2 metals due to the presence of partially filled d orbitals, which allow for stronger and more complex bonding interactions.

        Greater hardness and strength: Transition metals tend to be harder and stronger than group 1 and 2 metals due to the presence of partially filled d orbitals, which allow for stronger and more complex bonding interactions.

        Greater density: Transition metals tend to be more dense than group 1 and 2 metals due to their smaller atomic radii and higher atomic masses.

        Greater ductility and malleability: Transition metals tend to be more ductile and malleable than group 1 and 2 metals due to the presence of partially filled d orbitals, which allow for the formation of metallic bonds that can be easily deformed without breaking.

        Greater electrical conductivity: Both transition metals and group 1 and 2 metals are good conductors of electricity, but transition metals tend to have higher electrical conductivity due to the mobility of their partially filled d electrons.
