# CH1276

**Maximum mark: 11**

When a hydroxide salt is added to a solution of chloromethane in an organic solvent, an exothermic reaction occurs. The equation for this reaction is shown.

![CH1276](images/CH1276.png)

1.  The enthalpy change of the reaction, $\Delta H_r,$ was measured by performing the reaction in a calorimeter.

    A $100\,\text{cm}^3$ solution of hydroxide ions $(1.00\,\text{mol dm}^{-3})$ was added rapidly to a $100\,\text{cm}^3$ stirred solution of chloromethane $(0.50\,\text{mol dm}^{-3}),$ and the temperature of the mixture was measured. The results of the experiment are shown below.

    ![CH1276A](images/CH1276A.png)

    1.  Deduce the value of $\Delta T$ for this reaction. [1 mark]

    2.  The value of $\Delta H_r,$ in $\text{kJ mol}^{-1},$ is calculated using the equation

        $\Delta H_r = \left(\dfrac{-c \Delta T}{1000\, n}\right)(S)$

        The following table lists the values of parameters that **may** be used in the calculation.

        -   :::tableoptions
            | Parameter | Symbol | Value | Units |
            | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: |
            | Heat capacity of reaction mixture | $c$ | 2.23 | $\text{J K}^{-1}\,\text{g}^{-1}$ |
            | Moles of chloromethane | $n$ | 0.0500 | mol |
            | Volume of hydroxide solution | | 100 | $\text{cm}^3$ |
            | Volume of chloromethane solution | | 100 | $\text{cm}^3$ |
            | Density of reaction mixture | | 0.786 | $\text{g cm}^{-3}$ |
            | Molar mass of solvent | | 41.1 | $\text{g mol}^{-1}$ |
            | Molar mass of chloromethane | | 50.5 | $\text{g mol}^{-1}$ |

        To determine $\Delta H_r,$ the quantity of reaction mixture, $S,$ must be known.

        Calculate the value of $S,$ including its units. [3 marks]

    3. Calculate the value of $\Delta H_r,$ in $\textrm{kJ mol}^{-1}.$ [1 mark]

    4.  In a second experiment, the volume of hydroxide solution was halved, and the concentration was doubled. The volume and concentration of the chloromethane solution were not changed. The measured value of $\Delta T$ was larger than in the first experiment.

        Suggest why a larger value of $\Delta T$ was measured. [2 marks]

2.  The reaction mixture was sampled after $1\,\text{min}$ and analyzed by $\ce{^1H}$ NMR spectroscopy. The resulting spectrum contains two strong signals corresponding to the $\ce{CH3}$ protons of the product and unreacted chloromethane, as shown.

    ![CH1276B](images/CH1276B.png)

    The integral of the product signal at $3.065 \textrm{\ ppm}$ was used to calculate the yield of the reaction after $1\,\text{min}.$

    1.  Suggest why the calculated yield may not be reliable based on the information in the NMR spectrum. [1 mark]

    2.  State whether the error described in (i) is random or systematic, giving a reason for your answer. [2 marks]

    3.  The calculated yield after $1 \textrm{\ min}$ was $0.084\pm 0.003\,\text{mol dm}^{-3}.$

        Calculate the **percentage** uncertainty in the calculated yield. [1 mark]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.
    1.  $\Delta T = 33 - 25 = 8\,{}^\circ\text{C}\,\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The reaction is exothermic, but it does not occur instantaneously and heat is lost to the surroundings throughout. The temperature therefore rises to a maximum and then falls, once the rate of heat loss exceeds the rate at which the reaction releases heat. That maximum is lower than the temperature the mixture would have reached had no heat escaped, so using it directly would make the calculated enthalpy change too small.

        Extrapolating the straight line through the cooling data back to the time of mixing corrects for this loss. Here the extrapolated temperature is $33\,{}^\circ\textrm{C}$ and the initial temperature is $25\,{}^\circ\textrm{C},$ giving

        $\Delta T = 33\,{}^\circ\textrm{C} - 25\,{}^\circ\textrm{C} = 8\,{}^\circ\textrm{C}$

    2.  $V = 100 + 100 = 200 \textrm{\ cm}^3 \: \checkmark$\
    $S = 200 \times 0.786 = 157 \: \checkmark$\
    units: $\textrm{g}$ / grams $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    The units of $S$ follow from the rest of the equation. The heat capacity $c$ is given in $\textrm{J K}^{-1} \textrm{\ g}^{-1},$ so $c\Delta T$ has units of $\textrm{J g}^{-1},$ and dividing by 1000 converts this to $\textrm{kJ g}^{-1}.$ That is an energy per gram, so multiplying by $S$ yields an energy in kJ only if $S$ is the mass of the reaction mixture in grams.

        The two solutions are mixed, so the volume of the mixture is $100 + 100 = 200 \textrm{\ cm}^3.$ Multiplying by the density gives

        $S = 200 \textrm{\ cm}^3 \times 0.786 \textrm{\ g cm}^{-3} = 157 \textrm{\ g}$

        The molar masses and the amount of chloromethane in the table are not needed here; they are used at the next stage, when the energy released is divided by the amount of limiting reactant.

    3. $\Delta H_r =-56.0 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1} \: \checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Substituting the extrapolated temperature change and the mass of the reaction mixture into the equation gives the energy released per mole of chloromethane. The negative sign is already built into the equation and records that the reaction is exothermic: the mixture warms because energy is transferred out of the reacting system.

        $\Delta H_r = -\dfrac{2.23 \textrm{\ J g}^{-1}\textrm{K}^{-1} \times 8 \textrm{\ K}}{1000 \textrm{\ J kJ}^{-1} \times 0.0500 \textrm{\ mol}} \times 157 \textrm{\ g} = -56.0 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1}$

        The value is quoted per mole of chloromethane because chloromethane is the limiting reactant. The two solutions have equal volumes, but the hydroxide is twice as concentrated, so hydroxide ions are in excess and some remain unreacted when the chloromethane is used up.

    4.  mass / volume of the reaction mixture is smaller $\checkmark$\
        same amount of energy is released, so each gram of mixture absorbs more energy; $\Delta T$ is larger $\checkmark$

        *Do not award M2 unless the larger $\Delta T$ is linked to the smaller quantity of reaction mixture.*

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        Halving the volume of the hydroxide solution while doubling its concentration leaves the amount of hydroxide unchanged, and the chloromethane is untouched. The same amount of reaction therefore occurs and the same quantity of energy is released.

        That energy is now shared among a smaller mass of solution. The total volume falls from $200 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ to $150 \textrm{\ cm}^3,$ three$-$quarters of the original, so $S$ falls in the same proportion. With $\Delta H_r$ and $n$ unchanged, $\Delta T$ must rise by a factor of $\frac{4}{3}.$

2.
    1.  signals are not baseline$-$resolved /  peaks  overlap at base, so limits of each integral are uncertain $\checkmark$\
    **OR**\
    product signal is small, so uncertainty in integration limits is a large fraction of its integral $\checkmark$

        *Accept: the reactant signal is larger, so including a small part of its area in the product integral causes an overestimate of the calculated yield.*

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The integral of a signal is the area between the baseline and the curve, and is proportional to the number of protons in that environment. Measuring it requires a decision about where the signal begins and ends. Here the trace does not return to the baseline between the two peaks, so that decision is a judgement rather than a measurement, and different choices of limit give different integrals.

        The consequence falls unevenly. The chloromethane signal is much the larger of the two, so a small part of its area counted into the methanol integral shifts the product integral by a large proportion while barely affecting the reactant. The yield calculated from the product signal is therefore sensitive to where the boundary is drawn, and is likely to be overestimated.

    2.  systematic $\checkmark$

        repeating analysis gives same overlap, same excess area is added to  product integral each time $\checkmark$\
        **OR**\
        two signals always appear at the same chemical shifts, so product integral is inflated by same amount in every measurement $\checkmark$

        *Do not award M2 for a general definition of systematic error.*

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        A random error displaces individual measurements unpredictably, so repeats scatter above and below the true value and averaging reduces the effect. A systematic error displaces every measurement in the same direction, so averaging does not help.

        Re$-$running the spectrum on the same mixture reproduces the same two overlapping signals, so the same reactant area is counted into the product integral every time. The yield is overestimated in every repeat, which is what makes the error systematic rather than random.

    3.  % uncertainty = 4\%  $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The percentage uncertainty is the ratio of the absolute uncertainty to the measured quantity:

        $\textrm{\% uncertainty} = \dfrac{0.003 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}}{0.084 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}} \times 100\% = 4\%$
