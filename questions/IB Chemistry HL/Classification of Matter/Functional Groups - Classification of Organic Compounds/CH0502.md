## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1. carbonyl **OR** ester present (peak at $1700 \textrm{\ cm}^{-1}) \:\checkmark$\
    hydroxy **OR** carboxyl not present (no strong, broad peak above $2800 \textrm{\ cm}^{-1})\: \checkmark$\
    *Do not award M2 if the sugested peak is not consistent with the molecular formula, $\ce{C_9H_{10}O2}.$*

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    The IR spectrum shows a strong absorption near $1700 \textrm{\ cm}^{-1}, characteristic of a $\ce{C=O}$ stretch, so a carbonyl$-$containing functional group (such as a ketone or ester) is present.

    A broad $\ce{O-H}$ band typical of alcohols or carboxylic acids (usually spread over about $2500\textrm{--}3300\textrm{\ cm}^{-1})$ is absent. This indicates that there is no free hydroxy $\ce{-OH}$ or carboxyl $\ce{(-COOH)}$ group in the molecule.

2. 150: $\ce{C9H10O2^+}$ $\checkmark$\
    107: $\ce{OC6H4CH3^+}$ (loss of $\ce{CH3CO}) \: \checkmark$\
    91: $\ce{C6H4CH3+}$ (loss of $\ce{CH3COO}) \: \checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    The molecular formula $\ce{C_9H_{10}O_2}$ corresponds to a molecular mass of about 150, so the molecular ion peak at $m/z = 150$ is $\ce{C_9H_{10}O_2^+}.$

    The fragment at $m/z = 107$ is 43 mass units lighter than the molecular ion $(150 - 43 = 107).$ A fragment of 43 is characteristic of $\ce{CH3CO},$ so this fragment can be assigned to $\ce{C6H5CH3^+}.$

    The fragment at $m/z = 91$ is 59 units lighter than the molecular ion $(150 - 59 = 91).$ A fragment of 59 here corresponds to $\ce{CH3COO}.$ The fragment is $\ce{C6H4CH3+}.$

3. only two  signals / chemical environments for benzene hydrogens\
    **OR** \
     both signals are doublets (have one neighbouring $\ce{C-H})\, \checkmark$

     $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
     A $1,4$-disubstituted benzene ring has a high degree of symmetry. This makes the aromatic protons fall into two sets of equivalent protons, giving two distinct signals in the aromatic region.

    Moreover, each set of equivalent aromatic protons in a $1,4-$disubstituted ring generally appears as a doublet, because each proton (or equivalent set) has one neighbouring it is coupled to.

    The presence of two aromatic signals, each appearing as a doublet, is strong evidence that the two substituents on the benzene ring are in the 1 and 4 positions.

    If the benzene were $1,2-$ or $1,3-$ substituted there would be 4 distinct types of hydrogen.
