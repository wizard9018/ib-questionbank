# CH0727

**Maximum mark: 12**

The standard enthalpy of formation of gaseous nitric oxide, $\Delta H_f^{\ominus}$(NO(g)), is +90.3 kJ mol$^{-1}$.

1.  Write a reaction equation, including state symbols, corresponding to the standard enthalpy of formation of gaseous nitric oxide. [2 marks]

2.  Calculate the bond enthalpy of nitric oxide, using the value of $\Delta H_f^{\ominus}$(NO(g)) above and section 11 (*Section 12- 2025 Syllabus*) of the data booklet. [3 marks]

3.  In the Ostwald process, nitric oxide (NO) is reacted with oxygen and then water to produce aqueous nitric acid ($\ce{HNO3}$). The overall equation and enthalpy change of this reaction are given below.

    $\ce{4 NO(g) + 3 O2(g) + 2 H2O(l) -> 4 HNO3(aq)}\qquad \Delta H_1 = -576.4\,\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$

    Calculate the enthalpy of formation of aqueous nitric acid, $\Delta H_f^{\ominus}(\ce{HNO3(aq)}$), using the values of $\Delta H_f^{\ominus}(\ce{NO(g)}) = +90.3\,\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$, and $\Delta H_1$ and section 12 (*Section 13- 2025 Syllabus*) of the data booklet. [3 marks]

4.  The nitric oxide used in the Ostwald process is formed by reacting gaseous ammonia ($\ce{NH3}$) with oxygen. The equation and enthalpy change of this reaction are given below.

    $\ce{4 NH3(g) + 5 O2(g) -> 4 NO(g) + 6 H2O(g)}\qquad \Delta H_2 = -906.0\,\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$

    The yield of the Ostwald process may be lower than expected if some of the nitric oxide product reacts with the remaining ammonia. The equation for this side reaction is shown.

    $\ce{4 NH3(g) + 6 NO(g) -> 5 N2(g) + 6 H2O(g)}$

    $\newline \ \mbox{}\\[-25pts]$

    1.  Calculate the enthalpy change for the side reaction, $\Delta H_{side}$, using the values of $\Delta H_f^{\ominus}$(NO(g)) and $\Delta H_2$. [2 marks]

    2.  Suggest how the value of $\Delta H_2$ would differ if water were produced in the liquid phase. [2 marks]

    Molecular formulas AND stoichiometries correct; $\checkmark$

    *Accept N$_2$(g) + O$_2$(g) $\rightarrow$ 2NO(g);*\
    *Do **not** award M1 for reverse reaction*

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

    The standard enthalpy of formation is the enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is produced from its elements in their standard states, i.e. at 100 kPa and ambient temperature (typically 298 K). Nitrogen and oxygen are both diatomic gases in their standard states. Standard enthalpies of formation can be calculated for compounds in any state and under non-standard conditions (by incorporating the enthalpy change produced as the temperature or pressure varies or the compound undergoes changes of state), but here the enthalpy change is calculated for NO in its standard state, i.e. a gas. Strictly, the coefficient of NO in the equation should be 1, as the enthalpy of formation is calculated per mole of product. A molecule of NO contains only one atom each of nitrogen and oxygen, so the coefficients of N$_2$ and O$_2$ must both be $\frac{1}{2}$.

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

    According to Hess\'s Law, the enthalpy change of a reaction depends only on the enthalpies of the initial reactants and final products, and is not affected by the path taken. Therefore, the enthalpy change can be calculated by imagining that the reactants are first converted to atoms, which then form new bonds to generate the products. Bond enthalpies are given as positive values, corresponding to the energy that must be absorbed to break the bonds. Therefore, the enthalpy change for breaking the bonds in the reactants, an endothermic process, is calculated by adding the corresponding bond enthalpies, while the enthalpy change for bond formation in the products, an exothermic process, is calculated by subtracting the bond enthalpies. In the calculation of the total enthalpy change, it is important to remember to multiply each bond enthalpy term by the number of bonds broken or formed. In this question, the reaction enthalpy is known but the bond enthalpy of NO is unknown, so the equation must be rearranged by subtracting $\Delta H_r^{\ominus}$ from both sides and adding $E(\ce{N#O})$.

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

    The reaction has the same enthalpy change as a process in which the reactants are converted to their elements in their standard states, which are then converted to the aqueous nitric acid product. The reaction enthalpy change is therefore calculated as the enthalpy of formation of the products minus the enthalpy of formation of the reactants; the latter terms are subtracted because the conversion of the reactants to their constituent elements is the opposite of the formation process. In this question, the enthalpy of formation of nitric acid is unknown while the reaction enthalpy is known. Therefore, the equation must be rearranged to make the unknown enthalpy of formation the subject.

        as energy / heat is released during condensation (of water) *OR* the enthalpy change for formation of intermolecular / hydrogen bonds is negative / releases energy *OR* condensation / intermolecular bond formation is exothermic. $\checkmark$

        *Award M1 for an enthalpy cycle indicating that the formation of liquid water causes the magnitude of $\Delta H_2$ to increase;\

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        The enthalpy change for the chemical reaction would be the same, regardless of the physical state of the product. However, changes of state form or break intermolecular bonds, resulting in an additional change of enthalpy that must be accounted for in the total calculated enthalpy change. If water is produced as a liquid, the reaction enthalpy change must be added to the enthalpy change for converting gaseous water to a liquid, i.e. the enthalpy of condensation. Condensation involves the formation of intermolecular bonds (hydrogen bonds in water) so is exothermic, producing a negative enthalpy change. The total enthalpy change is therefore made more negative: more energy is released.
