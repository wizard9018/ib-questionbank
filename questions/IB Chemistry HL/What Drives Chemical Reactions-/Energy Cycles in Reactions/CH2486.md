# CH2486

**Maximum mark: 7**

Potassium chloride, $\ce{KCl},$ is an ionic compound formed from its elements.

1. Calculate the enthalpy change, in $\textrm{kJ},$ for the sublimation of potassium, using the data below. [2 marks]

    $\ce{K}\textrm{(s)} + \frac{1}{2}\ce{\,Cl2}\textrm{(g)} \longrightarrow \ce{KCl}\textrm{(s)}$|$ \Delta H_1 = -436.7\textrm{ kJ}$
    :-:|:--
    $\ce{KCl}\textrm{(s)} \longrightarrow \ce{K+}\textrm{(g)} + \ce{Cl-}\textrm{(g)} $|$\Delta H_2 = +715\textrm{ kJ}$
    $\ce{K+}\textrm{(g)} + \ce{e^-} \longrightarrow \ce{K}\textrm{(g)} $|$ \Delta H_3 = -418.7\textrm{ kJ}$
    $\ce{Cl2}\textrm{(g)} \longrightarrow 2\ce{\,Cl}\textrm{(g)} $|$\Delta H_4 = +242.6\textrm{ kJ}$
    $\ce{Cl}\textrm{(g)} + \ce{e^-} \longrightarrow \ce{Cl-}\textrm{(g)} $|$ \Delta H_5 = -349\textrm{ kJ}$

$\mbox{}\\[-15pts]$

2. Explain why $\Delta H_4$ is positive. [1 mark]

3. Outline why $\Delta H_2$ is larger than $\Delta H_4.$ [2 marks]

4. Predict, with a reason, how the magnitude of $\Delta H_3$ would change if potassium were replaced by sodium. [2 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1. $\Delta H = \Delta H_1 + \Delta H_2 + \Delta H_3 - \frac{1}{2}\Delta H_4 - \Delta H_5 \: \checkmark$\
    $\Delta H = +87.3\textrm{\ kJ} \: \checkmark$

    *Award [2] for correct final answer.*

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    The target equation is the sublimation of potassium:

    $\ce{K}\textrm{(s)} \longrightarrow \ce{K}\textrm{(g)}$

    Equations 1, 2 and 3 are used as written. Equation 4 is reversed and halved, and equation 5 is reversed. When the five manipulated equations are added, all chlorine-containing species, the electron and the $\ce{K+}$ ion cancel as intermediates:

    $\ce{K}\textrm{(s)} + \cancel{\frac{1}{2}\ce{\,Cl2}\textrm{(g)}} \longrightarrow \cancel{\ce{KCl}\textrm{(s)}}$|$ \Delta H_1 = -436.7\textrm{ kJ}$
    :-:|:--
    $\cancel{\ce{KCl}\textrm{(s)}} \longrightarrow \cancel{\ce{K+}\textrm{(g)}} + \cancel{\ce{Cl-}\textrm{(g)}} $|$\Delta H_2 = +715\textrm{ kJ}$
    $\cancel{\ce{K+}\textrm{(g)}} + \cancel{\ce{e^-}} \longrightarrow \ce{K}\textrm{(g)} $|$ \Delta H_3 = -418.7\textrm{ kJ}$
    $\cancel{\ce{Cl}\textrm{(g)}} \longrightarrow \cancel{\frac{1}{2}\ce{Cl2}\textrm{(g)}}  $|$-\frac{1}{2}\Delta H_4 = -\frac{1}{2}(+242.6\textrm{ kJ})$
    $\underline{\cancel{\ce{Cl-}\textrm{(g)}} \longrightarrow  \cancel{\ce{Cl}\textrm{(g)}} + \cancel{\ce{e^-}} } $|$ \underline{-\Delta H_5 = -(-349\textrm{ kJ})}$
    $\ce{K}\textrm{(s)} \longrightarrow \ce{K}\textrm{(g)}$|$\Delta H = +87.3 \textrm{\ kJ}$

    The positive value confirms that sublimation is endothermic, as energy must be supplied to overcome the metallic bonding in solid potassium.

2. bond breaking is endothermic $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    $\Delta H_4$ represents the dissociation of molecular chlorine into two gaseous atoms. The shared electron pair in the $\ce{Cl-Cl}$ bond holds the two atoms together, so energy must be absorbed from the surroundings to separate them. Bond breaking is always endothermic.

3.  in KCl, each ion is attracted to multiple oppositely charged ions in a giant ionic lattice $\checkmark$\
    in $\ce{Cl2},$ each molecule contains only a single covalent bond $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    $\Delta H_2$ is the lattice enthalpy of $\ce{KCl}$. In the $\ce{KCl}$ lattice, each $\ce{K+}$ ion is surrounded by six $\ce{Cl-}$ ions (and vice versa), with further electrostatic interactions extending throughout the three$-$dimensional lattice. Separating one mole of $\ce{KCl}$ into gaseous ions requires overcoming all of these cumulative electrostatic attractions, which requires $+715\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$.

    $\Delta H_4$ is the bond dissociation enthalpy of $\ce{Cl2}$. Each molecule contains only one covalent bond between two chlorine atoms. Breaking one mole of these bonds requires $+242.6\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$, which is much less than the energy required to overcome the multiple electrostatic interactions in the ionic lattice.

4. $|\Delta H_3|$ would increase $\checkmark$\
    $\ce{Na}$ has a smaller atomic radius so the electron is more strongly attracted to the nucleus $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    $\Delta H_3$ is the reverse of the first ionisation energy. Potassium's outermost electron occupies the fourth shell, while sodium's occupies the third. With fewer electron shells, the outermost electron in sodium sits closer to the nucleus and experiences a stronger electrostatic attraction. More energy is therefore needed to remove it, giving sodium a higher first ionisation energy. Since $\Delta H_3$ is the reverse of this process, its magnitude increases from $418.7\textrm{ kJ}$ for potassium to $495.8\textrm{ kJ}$ for sodium.
