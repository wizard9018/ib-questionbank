# CH2066

[Maximum marks: 10]

2$\text{--}$methylpropan$\text{--}2\text{--}$ol, $\ce{(CH3)3COH}$ is a common additive used to increase the octane rating of fuel.

1.  Write the balanced equation for the complete combustion of $\ce{(CH3)3COH}.$ [1 mark]

2.  Calculate the enthalpy of combustion of 2$\text{--}$methylpropan$\text{--}2\text{--}$ol using Section 12 of the IB Chemistry data booklet. [3 marks]

3.  The accepted value for the enthalpy of combustion is $-2644\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}.$ Suggest the best explanation for the difference between the accepted value and the value obtained in (b).    [1 mark]

4.  A student carried out an experiment to determine the enthalpy of combustion of 2$\text{--}$methylpropan$\text{--}2\text{--}$ol using a spirit burner as shown here, and collected the data below.

    final mass of spirit burner | $133.6\pm 0.1\text{ g}$

    Using Sections 1 and 2 of the IB Chemistry data booklet, calculate the experimental enthalpy of combustion. [3 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  $\ce{(CH3)3COH(l) + 6 O2(g) ->  4 CO2(g) + 5 H2O(l)}\ \checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$

    Complete combustion reacts an organic compound with oxygen to form carbon dioxide and liquid water.  The number of carbons in the organic compound is equal to the coefficient of carbon dioxide.  The number of hydrogens in the compound, divided by 2, gives the coefficient of water molecules.  The coefficient for oxygen can be deduced by arithmetic.

2.  broken: $\ce{3(C-C)} + \ce{9(C-H)} + \ce{(C-O)} + \ce{(O-H)} + \ce{6
(O=O)} = 8573\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\ \checkmark$

    formed: $\ce{8(C=O)} + \ce{10(O-H)} = 11062 text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\ \checkmark$

    $\Delta H = 8573 - 11062 = -2489\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\ \checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$

    The enthalpy of a chemical reaction can be estimated using the bond dissociation enthalpies as

    $\Delta H_{rxn} = \sum \text{bonds broken} - \sum \text{bonds formed}$

    Drawing the structural formulas for the reactants and products in the combustion reaction, it is possible to count the bonds which must be broken and those which must be formed.

    ![CH2066 equation](<images/CH2066 equation.png>)

    $\begin{aligned}
    \text{Broken} &= \ce{3(C-C)} + \ce{9(C-H)} + \ce{(C-O)} + \ce{(O-H)} + \ce{6(O=O)} \\
    &=  3(346) + 9(414) + (358) + (463) + 6(498) \\
    &=  8573\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} \\
    \\
    \text{Formed} &=  \ce{8(C=O)} + \ce{10(O-H)}  \\
    &= 8(804) + 10(463) \\
    &= 11062\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} \\
    \\
    \Delta H &=  8573 - 11062 = -2489\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} \\
    \end{aligned}$

3.  bond dissociation enthalpy calculations assume gas phase reactions $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$

    Both 2$\text{--}$methylpropan$\text{--}2\text{--}$ol and water are liquid. To get a more accurate measure of $\Delta H,$ add the enthalpy of vaporization of $\ce{(CH3)3COH(l)}$ $(\Delta H_{vap} = 40.4\text{ kJ mol}^{-1})$ and subtract five times the enethalpy of vaporization of water $(\Delta H_{vap} = 40.4\text{ kJ mol}^{-1})$ to get $\Delta H_c = -2651\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}.$

    $\begin{aligned}
    \ce{(CH3)3COH(g) + 6 O2(g) &-> 4 CO2(g) + 5 H2O(g)} &\quad & \Delta H = -2489\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} \\
    \ce{(CH3)3COH(l) &-> (CH3)3COH(g)} &\quad & \Delta H = -40.4\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} \\
    \ce{5 H2O(g) &-> 5 H2O(l)} &\quad & \Delta H = -40.4\times -5\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} \\ \hline
    \ce{(CH3)3COH(l) + 6 O2(g) &-> 4 CO2(g) + 5 H2O(l)} &\quad & \Delta H_c = -2651\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}
    \end{aligned}$

    This value is much closer to the literature value of $-2644\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}.$ This deviation is likely because the bond enthalpy values in Section 12 are averaged over representative molecules and not specific to the 2$\text{--}$methylpropan$\text{--}2\text{--}$ol.

4.  $n = \dfrac{134.2\text{ g} -133.6\text{ g}}{74.12\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.00809\text{ mol}\ \checkmark$

    $Q = mc\Delta T = (150\text{ g})(4.18\text{ J g}^{-1}\ ^\circ\text{C})\left[(50.0-23.2)\ ^\circ\text{C}\right] = 16803.6\text{ J}\ \checkmark$

    $\Delta H = -\dfrac{Q}{n} = - \dfrac{16.8036\text{ kJ}}{0.00809\text{ mol}} = -2080\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\ \checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$

    To find the experimental enthalpy of combustion, the change in temperature of a fixed mass of water and the mass of fuel burned are required.  The heat released by the fuel ia absorbed by the water, according to the relationship $Q = mc\Delta T$ where $Q$ is heat, $m$ is the mass of water, $c$ is the speficif heat capacity of water and $\Delta T$ is the change in temperature of the water.  Substituting the experimental values in,

    $Q_\ce{H2O} = mc\Delta T = (150\text{ g})(4.18\text{ J g}^{-1}\ ^\circ\text{C})\left[(50.0 - 23.2)\ ^\circ\text{C}\right] = 16803.6\text{ J}$

    If there is no heat lost to the surroundings, the heat absorbed by the water is equal and opposite to the heat released by the fuel.  Reporting in the answer in kJ, this gives:

    $Q_\ce{fuel} = -16.8036\text{ kJ}$

    The enthalpy of combustion of the fuel is an intensive property which relates the amount of heat released per mole of the fuel. The change in mass of the spirit burner is equal to the mass of the fuel which can be converted to moles using the molar mass $(M_r = 74.12).$

    $n_\ce{fuel} = \dfrac{134.2\text{ g} - 133.6\text{ g}}{74.12\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.00809\text{ mol}$

    $\Delta H_c = \dfrac{Q}{n} = -\dfrac{-16.8036\text{ kJ}}{0.00809\text{ mol}} = -2080\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$

5.  $\dfrac{0.02}{0.06}\times 100\% = 30\%\ \checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$

    The percent uncertainty is equal to the absolute uncertainty in a measurement divided by the measurement:

    $\%\text{ uncertainty} = \dfrac{\text{absolute uncertainty}}{\text{measured value}}\times 100\%$

    The mass of fuel is indirectly measured as the difference in mass of the spirit burner before and after the experiment. As the values must be subtracted, their absolute uncertainty must be added.

    If: $y = a\pm b \quad \text{then:}\quad \Delta y = \Delta a + \Delta b$

    So

    If: $m = 134.2 - 133.6 = 0.6\text{ g} \quad \text{then:}\quad \Delta m = 0.1 + 0.1 = 0.2\text{ g}$

    The percent uncertainty is

    $\%\text{ uncertainty} = \dfrac{0.2\text{ g}}{0.6\text{ g}}\times 100\% = 33.3\% \longrightarrow 30\%$

6.  $\%\text{ uncertainty} > \%\text{ error},$ random error dominates $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$

    The random error can be estimated with the percent uncertainty. This must be greater than the value in part (e) as other variables also include uncertainty.

    The systematic error can be estimated with the percent error.  The experimental value of $-2080\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$ can be compared to the literature value:

    $\%\text{ error} = \left|\dfrac{\text{experimental} - \text{literature}}{\text{literature}}\right|\times 100\%$

    $\left|\dfrac{-2080 - (-2644)}{-2644}\right|\times 100\% = 21\%$

    The random error is $> 33\%,$ and is greater than the systematic error $21\%.$ Random error dominates.
