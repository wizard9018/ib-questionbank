## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.
    1.  Standard enthalpy / heat of formation (of aqueous hydrogen peroxide). $\checkmark$

        *Award [0] if “standard” is omitted*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        $\Delta H_{A}^{\ominus}$ is the enthalpy change when hydrogen peroxide is formed from gaseous $\ce{H2}$ and gaseous $\ce{O2}$, and the symbol $\ominus$ indicates that the reaction takes place under standard conditions, i.e. 100 kPa and ambient temperature (typically 298 K). The reaction is therefore the formation of hydrogen peroxide from its elements in their standard states, meaning that $\Delta H_{A}^{\ominus}$  is the standard enthalpy of formation of the compound.

    2. $\Delta H_{A}^{\ominus} = \Delta H_{B}^{\ominus} + \dfrac{1}{2}\Delta H_{C}^{\ominus} - \Delta H_{dec}^{\ominus}$

        **OR**

        $\Delta H_{A}^{\ominus} = \Delta H_{B}^{\ominus} - \Delta H_{dec}^{\ominus}$

        **OR**

        $\Delta H_{A}^{\ominus} = -285.8 (+ 0) + 95.1\,\checkmark$

        $\Delta H_{A}^{\ominus} = -190.7\,\text{kJ mol}^{-1}\,\checkmark$

        *Award M2 if answer based on follow-through error from previous step;\
        Award [1] if value of $\Delta H_{A}^{\ominus}$ given without workings;\
        Do not award M2 for $-$191 ($kJ \ mol^{-1}$).*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        Hess\'s Law states that two processes have the same enthalpy change if their initial and final states are the same. The value of $\Delta H_{A}^{\ominus}$ can therefore be calculated by drawing an alternative path around the enthalpy cycle, beginning at the same reactants and ending at the same products. The arrows representing $\Delta H_{B}^{\ominus}$ and $\Delta H_{C}^{\ominus}$ are in the correct direction, but the arrow representing $\Delta H_{dec}^{\ominus}$ must be reversed in order to reach the correct final state. Thus, in the final calculation of $\Delta H_{A}^{\ominus}$, $\Delta H_{B}^{\ominus}$ and $\Delta H_{C}^{\ominus}$ are added and $\Delta H_{dec}^{\ominus}$ is subtracted.

2.
    1.  To correct (the measured temperature change) for heat loss / cooling $\checkmark$

        *Accept similar answers*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        If heat is lost to the environment, the measured value of $\Delta T$ is smaller and the value of $q$ is underestimated. In this question, an underestimate in $q$ would lead to an underestimate in the calculated value of $n$ in part (ii), since n is directly proportional to $q$. The cooling of the solution appears to be approximately linear, so the trend can be extrapolated to the point of addition to estimate what the temperature would have been if all the heat had been released immediately and no cooling had taken place. This extrapolation should produce a more accurate estimate of $\Delta T$. The estimate is not fully reliable, however, as the cooling trend at the point of addition may not match the linear cooling observed later in the process.

    2.  $\Delta T = (26.8 - 21.0) = 5.8\,{}^\circ \text{C (from graph)}\,\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        To estimate the effect of cooling, a straight line is drawn through the data points that obey a linear cooling trend. This line is extrapolated to the time of addition, and the temperature is read off on the y-axis as shown in the diagram. The value of $\Delta T$ is the difference between this temperature and the temperature when the solid was added. The heat released, $q$, is calculated from $\Delta T$ using the equation $q = mc\Delta T$. The heat released by each mole of reactant is given by $-\Delta H_{dec}^{\ominus}$, so the number of moles $n$ is calculated by dividing the total amount of heat $q$ by $-\Delta H_{dec}^{\ominus}$. Finally, the concentration of reactant is the number of moles divided by the volume in $dm^3$; the volume in the question is given in units of cm$^3$, so this value must be divided by 1000.

    1.  Enthalpy change = $BE(\ce{O-O}) - 0.5\times BE(\ce{O=O})\,\checkmark$

        Enthalpy change = $(144 - 0.5 \times 498) = -105\,\text{kJ mol}^{-1}\,\checkmark$

        *Award M1 if correct numerical values used instead of enthalpy terms;\
        Award M2 if answer is based on a single follow-through sign or stoichiometry error in previous step, providing method is otherwise correct;\
        Award [1] if enthalpy change is given with no workings shown*.

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        The breaking of bonds absorbs energy, so is associated with a positive enthalpy change, while the formation of bonds releases energy and produces a negative enthalpy change. In the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide, the central $\ce{O-O}$ bond is broken, absorbing an amount of energy equal to the bond enthalpy. An $\ce{O-H}$ bond is also broken, but this is reformed when water is formed: the net change in the number of $\ce{O-H}$ bonds is therefore zero, and these do not contribute to the total enthalpy change. Finally, the O atoms released as the $\ce{O-O}$ bonds break recombine to form $\ce{O=O}$ bonds, releasing energy. The amount of energy released is only half the O=O bond enthalpy, as the decomposition of one mole of hydrogen peroxide produces only half a mole of oxygen gas. The total enthalpy change is the amount of energy absorbed minus the amount of energy released, i.e. the $\ce{O-O}$ bond enthalpy minus half the O=O bond enthalpy.

    2.  Bond enthalpies are average values

        **OR**

        the calculation does not include the enthalpy change for solvation (of $\ce{H2O2}$) / condensation (of $\ce{H2O}$) / changes of state $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        The enthalpy of bonds is slightly affected by other bonds in the molecule, and also by parameters such as the temperature, pressure and intermolecular forces (e.g. solvation). Bond enthalpies are therefore average values calculated from a range of molecules, and are unlikely to be exactly equal to the bond enthalpies for the molecules of interest. The enthalpy change of the reaction may also be affected by physical processes taking place during the reaction, since intermolecular forces also release energy when formed and absorb energy when broken. In the decomposition of gaseous hydrogen peroxide, the reactants and product are all gases so the contribution of intermolecular forces is likely to be small. However, in the liquid-phase reaction, the conversion of hydrogen peroxide to water will result in a change in the hydrogen bonding between molecules, altering the net enthalpy change for the process.
