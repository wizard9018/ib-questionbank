1.
    1.  The enthalpy change when (one mole of) a substance / compound is formed from its constituent elements in their standard states. $\checkmark$

        *Do **not** accept answer if the term “enthalpy change” or similar is not used\
        Do **not** accept answer if elements in their standard states are not specified as the reacting species*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        The standard state of an element is the material it forms at ambient pressure, i.e. 100 kPa, and a temperature of 298 K (SATP). For example, the standard state of oxygen is $\ce{O2}$(g). Enthalpies of formation must be calculated with reference to standard states because changes of state also involve enthalpy changes.

    2.  $\mbox{}\\[-14pt]
        \begin{aligned}
        \Delta H_r^{\ominus} &= \sum \Delta H_f^{\ominus}(\text{products}) - \sum \Delta H_f^{\ominus}(\text{reactants}) \\
        &= \Delta H_f^{\ominus}(\ce{CaCl2}) + \Delta H_f^{\ominus}(\ce{H2O(l)}) + \Delta H_f^{\ominus}(\ce{CO2}) - \Delta H_f^{\ominus}(\ce{CaCO3}) - 2\Delta H_f^{\ominus}(\ce{HCl}) \checkmark
        \end{aligned}$

        $\begin{aligned}
        \Delta H_f^{\ominus}(\ce{CaCO3}) &= \Delta H_f^{\ominus}(\ce{CaCl2}) + \Delta H_f^{\ominus}(\ce{H2O(l)}) + \Delta H_f^{\ominus}(\ce{CO2}) - 2\Delta H_f^{\ominus}(\ce{HCl}) - \Delta H_r^{\ominus} \\
        &= -878.6 - 285.8 - 393.5 + 2 \times 167.2 + 16.6\,\checkmark \\
        &= -1206.9 (\text{kJ mol}^{-1})\,\checkmark
        \end{aligned}$

        *Accept answers in which state symbols are omitted;\
        Award M1 if the enthalpy terms are replaced with the correct values;\
        Award M1 if the known enthalpy terms are summed to the correct value of $-1223.5 \ (kJ \ mol^{-1});$\
        Award M1 if the term $- \Delta H_f^{\ominus}$(HCl) is included instead of $-2 \Delta H_f^{\ominus}$(HCl);\
        Award M1 and M2 if the equation for $- \Delta H_r^{\ominus}$ is not stated explicitly but is correctly rearranged;\
        Award M2 if the term $+2 \times 167.2$ is expressed as $+334.4$;\
        Award M3 if answer is based on a single follow-through sign or stoichiometry error in previous steps, providing method is otherwise correct;\
        Award [1] if correct answer is given with no or incorrect workings shown;\
        Do not award M2 if the term $- \Delta H_f^{\ominus}$(HCl) is included instead of $- 2\Delta H_f^{\ominus}$(HCl);\
        Do not award M2 if the separate enthalpy contributions are not clearly stated with the correct signs.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        The enthalpy change can be calculated by imagining that the reactants are first converted to their constituent elements in their standard states, which are then converted to the products. The enthalpy change for this process is the standard enthalpy of formation of the products minus the standard enthalpy change of formation of the reactants. The enthalpy change for each species must, however, be multiplied by its stoichiometry in the reaction equation. In this question, the reaction enthalpy is known but the enthalpy of formation of the reacting $\ce{CaCO3}$  is unknown, so the equation must be rearranged by subtracting $\Delta H_r^{\ominus}$ from both sides and adding $\Delta H_f^{\ominus}(\ce{CaCO3}$).

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

    The enthalpy profile shows how the total enthalpy of the molecules in the reaction mixture changes during the reaction. The enthalpy increases initially, as the reactants must be given sufficient energy to collide and react successfully: this additional energy is called the activation energy, and may be provided e.g. by heating. The enthalpy change of the reaction is given by the difference between the enthalpies at the start (i.e. for the reactants) and at the end (i.e. for the products). It is negative if the reaction is exothermic and positive if the reaction is endothermic.

3.
    1.  $\dfrac{Number \ of \ moles \ of \ CO_2 \  produced}{\ce{CaCO3} \ reacted}$

        $n = \dfrac{mass}{M_r} = 0.14(27)\,\text{mol}\,\checkmark$

        $q = -\Delta H_r^{\ominus} \times n$

        **OR**

        $q = 16.6 (kJ) \times 0.143\,\text{mol}$

        **OR**

        $q = 2.3(7)\,\text{kJ} = 2370\,\text{J}\,\checkmark$

        $\Delta T = \dfrac{q}{(m \times c)}$

        **OR**

        $\Delta T = \dfrac{2370}{(100 \times 4.18)}\,\checkmark$

        $\Delta T = +5.7\,{}^\circ\text{C}\,\checkmark$

        Award M3 if value of $c = 4.2\, J\, K^{-1}\, mol^{-1}$ is used;\

        Award M3 if other units for q, m and/or c are used, provided they are consistent;\
        Award M3 and M4 if the calculation $\Delta T$ is not shown but the final value is correct;\
        Accept $\Delta T$ = 5.5 OR 5.6 $^\circ$C if based on explicit rounding errors from previous steps;\
        Accept values of $\Delta T$ based on explicit follow-through error in calculation of n;\
        Do not award M2 if the units of q are omitted and no other workings are shown;\
        Do not award M3 if the separate terms in the equation are not clearly shown;\
        Do not award M4 if the final answer is given to more or less than 1 decimal place*.

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        As shown in the reaction equation, each mole of $\ce{CaCO3}$ produces 1 mole of $\ce{CO2}$. Thus, the moles of reacted $\ce{CaCO3}$ is equal to the moles of $\ce{CO2}$ produced, and this is given by the mass of $\ce{CO2}$ divided by the molar mass of 44 g mol$^{-1}$. Although some $\ce{CO2}$ dissolves in the water, most is released as a gas, so the mass of $\ce{CO2}$ is approximately equal to the recorded decrease in the mass of the flask. The value of $\Delta H_r^{\ominus}$ indicates that each mole of $\ce{CaCO3}$ releases 16.6 $kJ$ to the environment, as the value is negative. Therefore, the total energy released, $q$, is given by the number of moles reacted times 16.6 $kJ$. To calculate $\Delta T$, the equation $q = mc\Delta T$ must be rearranged by dividing both sides by $mc$. The value of $q$ must also be multiplied by 1000 to convert the units of kJ to J, as the units of energy in $q$ and $c$ (4.18 J K$^{-1}$ mol$^{-1}$) must match.

    2.  Heat is lost / transferred to the flask / air / remaining solid / carbon dioxide / produced gas / surroundings / environment **OR** the flask is not insulated. $\checkmark$

        *Do **not** accept contradictions of the given assumptions, e.g. the mass or specific heat capacity of the solution changes*.

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        $\Delta T$ is proportional to q, which should be proportional to $\Delta H_r^{\ominus}$. However, not all of the heat released by the reaction is absorbed by the solution, and some heat is also absorbed by the solid (which has its own value of $m$ and $c$). The temperature therefore does not increase by the theoretical amount, and the value of $q$ calculated from $\Delta T$ is in fact only the amount of energy absorbed by the solution, not the total energy released in the reaction. The experiment should be designed to minimise this error, e.g. by insulating the reaction flask.

    3.  Some of the carbon dioxide dissolves / reacts with water. $\checkmark$

        *Do **not** accept descriptions of instrumental limitations, e.g. the balance is imprecise*.

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        If some gas dissolves in the water, its mass is not lost from the reaction flask. The recorded mass loss is therefore less than the true mass of carbon dioxide produced, resulting in an underestimate of the number of moles of reactant consumed, $n$. In this question, $\Delta H_r^{\ominus}$ is known, so an underestimate of n would cause the calculated value of $q$ to be too low, along with the predicted value of $\Delta T$. More usually, however, $\Delta T$ is measured and used to calculate q and then $\Delta H_r^{\ominus}$. Since $\Delta H_r^{\ominus}$ = $\dfrac{q}{n}$, an underestimate of $n$ would result in an overestimate of $\Delta H_r^{\ominus}$, as $q$ would be divided by a smaller number.
