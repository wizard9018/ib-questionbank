# CH2563

In an exothermic calorimetry experiment, a student forgets to account for the heat loss to the surroundings. How will this affect the calculated enthalpy change?

-   A.$\hspace{1.em}$The calculated value will be more exothermic than the literature value.

-   B.$\hspace{1.em}$The calculated value will be less exothermic than the literature value.

-   C.$\hspace{1.em}$The calculated value will be endothermic instead of exothermic.

-   D.$\hspace{1.em}$The calculated value will be approximately equal to the literature value.

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

**答案：B**

$\textrm{\underline{Explanation:}}$\
In any calorimetry experiment, the calorimeter is not perfectly insulated. Energy released by the exothermic reaction is distributed between the water (which the thermometer measures), the equipment (calorimeter walls, stirrer, thermometer itself), and the surroundings. Only the energy absorbed by the water contributes to the measured temperature change.

When heat loss to the surroundings is not accounted for, the thermometer records a smaller temperature increase than the total energy released would produce. Since $q = mc\Delta T,$ a smaller $\Delta T$ gives a smaller $q,$ which in turn gives a smaller $|\Delta H|$ when divided by the number of moles.

The calculated enthalpy change is therefore less exothermic (less negative, closer to zero) than the literature value. The sign remains negative: the reaction is still detected as exothermic but its magnitude is reduced. This is the most significant systematic error in simple calorimetry and is the primary reason that experimental enthalpies of combustion, measured with spirit burners, are typically $30$ to $60\%$ lower in magnitude than the accepted values.

Graphical extrapolation techniques (linear cooling corrections) can partially compensate for this heat loss by projecting the temperature back to the moment of mixing or ignition.
