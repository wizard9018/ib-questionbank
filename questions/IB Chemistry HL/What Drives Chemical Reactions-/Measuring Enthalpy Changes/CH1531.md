# CH1531

When $50.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ of $2.00 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$ $\ce{HNO_3}$(aq) is mixed with $50.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ of $2.00 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$ NaOH(aq), the temperature of the solution increases by 10 K. What will be the temperature change, in $^\circ$C, when $150 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ of $2.00 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$ $\ce{HNO3}\textrm{(aq)}$ is mixed with $50.0 \textrm{\ cm}^3$ of $2.00 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3}$ of NaOH(aq)?

- A.$\hspace{1em}$20

- B.$\hspace{1em}$10

- C.$\hspace{1em}$2.5

- D.$\hspace{1em}$5.0

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

**答案：D**

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$: \

The student must understand that a neutralization reaction releases heat according to the amount of acid/base neutralized. The heat evolved is gained by the surrounding aqueous solution.  The temperature change will be affected by the amount of solution.

The neutralization reaction involved is shown below.

$\ce{HNO3}\textrm{(aq)} + \ce{NaOH}\textrm{(aq)} \longrightarrow \ce{H2O}\textrm{(l)} + \ce{NaNO3}\textrm{(aq)} $

In the first experiment, the amount of acid and base can be calculated.

$n = CV$ 

$n_\ce{NaOH} = 2.00 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3} \times 5.00 \times 10^{-2} \textrm{\ dm}^3 = 0.100 \textrm{\ mol}$

$n_\ce{HNO3} = 2.00 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3} \times 5.00 \times 10^{-2} \textrm{\ dm}^3 = 0.100 \textrm{\ mol}$

Since the moles of the acid and base are equal, the amount neutralized is 0.100 mol.

In the second experiment, the amount of NaOH is the same.  More of the acid is added.

$n_\ce{HNO3} = 2.00 \textrm{\ mol dm}^{-3} \times 1.50 \times 10^{-1} \textrm{\ dm}^3 = 0.300 \textrm{\ mol}$

As the acid and base react in a $1:1$ ratio, we can infer that the acid is in excess and the base is the limiting reagent. The base will limit the neutralization and the amount of heat evolved from it.  Again, 0.100 mol are neutralized.

As a result, the heat evolved is the same as in the first experiment described. 

*q=mc$\Delta$T*

However, the heat evolved will be absorbed by 200 cm$^3$ of the mixture rather than by 100 cm$^3$. As a result, the temperature change in the solution will be smaller. Moreover, as the same heat  is evolved while the volume of the solution doubles (meaning that the mass in the equation doubles), the temperature change is halved.  

For the second experiment, $\Delta$T = 5.0 K or 5.0 $^\circ$C. Temperature changes are equal either in Celsius or the Kelvin scale.
