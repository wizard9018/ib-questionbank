# CH2565

A $10.25\textrm{ g}$ sample of anhydrous calcium chloride was added to $100.0\textrm{ g}$ of water in a solution calorimeter, and the following data was recorded. Assume that the calcium chloride was added to the calorimeter at $t = 0\textrm{ min}$ and that the specific heat capacity of the solution is $4.18\textrm{ J g}^{-1}\,{}^\circ\textrm{C}^{-1}.$

![CH2565](images/CH2565.png)

1. Calculate the temperature change for the dissolution, showing your working. [2 marks]

2. State, with a reason, whether this reaction is exothermic or endothermic. [2 marks]

3. Calculate the molar enthalpy of solution of calcium chloride, in $\textrm{kJ mol}^{-1}.$ [2 marks]

4. Outline one assumption made in using the extrapolated temperature rather than the highest recorded temperature. [1 mark]

5. Predict, giving a reason, how the experimental enthalpy of solution would compare with the literature value. [2 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1. $\Delta T = 22.5\,{}^\circ\textrm{C} \:\checkmark$\
    extrapolation of the linear cooling section back to $t = 0$ shown $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    The initial temperature $(20.0\,{}^\circ\textrm{C})$ is read from the graph at $t = 0\textrm{ min}.$ The final temperature $(42.5\,{}^\circ\textrm{C})$ is obtained by extrapolating the linear cooling portion of the curve back to $t = 0,$ not by reading the highest recorded temperature.

    ![CH2565ms](images/CH2565ms.png)

    $\Delta T = 42.5\,{}^\circ\textrm{C} - 20.0\,{}^\circ\textrm{C} = 22.5\,{}^\circ\textrm{C}$

    The extrapolation corrects for heat lost to the surroundings while the dissolution was still occurring.

2. temperature of the solution increases $\checkmark$\
    reaction is exothermic $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    When calcium chloride dissolves, the system (the dissolving process) releases energy to the surroundings (the water). This transfer of energy raises the temperature of the solution. An exothermic process is defined as one in which energy flows from the system to the surroundings, corresponding to a negative $\Delta H.$

3. $q = 10.4 \textrm{\ kJ}\:\checkmark$\
    $\Delta H = -112 \textrm{\ kJ mol}^{-1}\:\checkmark$

    *Award [2] for final answer.*

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    The total mass of the solution is the sum of the mass of the water, $100 \textrm{\ g},$ and the calcium chloride, $10.25 \textrm{\ g}.$

    $m = 110.25 \textrm{\ g}$

     The heat absorbed by the solution is the product of the total mass, the heat capacity and the change in temperature:

     $ q_{solution} = mc\Delta T$

     $q_{solution}= 110.25\textrm{ g} \times 4.18\textrm{ J g}^{-1}\,{}^\circ\textrm{C}^{-1} \times 22.5\,{}^\circ\textrm{C} = 10369 \textrm{ J} = 10.4\textrm{ kJ}$

    The heat lost by the reaction is equal in magnitude but opposite in sign to the heat gained by the solution:

    $q_{rxn} = -q_{solution} = -10.4 \textrm{\ kJ}$

    The enthalpy of the reaction is based on the moles of calcium chloride that reacted:

    $n = \dfrac{10.25\textrm{ g}}{110.98\textrm{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.09236\textrm{ mol}$

    The enthalpy change of the reaction is

    $\Delta H_{rxn} = \dfrac{q_{rxn}}{n} = \dfrac{-10.4\textrm{ kJ}}{0.09236\textrm{ mol}} = -112\textrm{ kJ mol}^{-1}$

    The negative sign is required because the dissolution is exothermic.

4. rate of heat loss is assumed to be constant (linear)  $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    Extrapolation draws a straight line through the cooling data back to the time of mixing. This assumes that the rate at which the solution loses heat to the surroundings does not change during the experiment. In practice, the rate of heat loss depends on the temperature difference between the solution and its surroundings. This is a reasonable approximation over a short time interval where the temperature does not change dramatically.

5. experimental value is less exothermic (smaller $|\Delta H|) \:\checkmark$ \
    heat is lost to the surroundings, not all the energy is detected as a temperature rise $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    Even with extrapolation, a coffee$-$cup calorimeter is not perfectly insulated. Some energy is absorbed by the polystyrene cup, the thermometer, and the surrounding air rather than by the solution. Each of these losses reduces the observed $\Delta T,$ leading to a smaller calculated $q$ and therefore a less exothermic $\Delta H$ than the literature value.
