# CH1350

Which statement is correct for $\ce{C2F2}$ and $\ce{C2F4}?$

-   A.$\hspace{1em}$The carbon-to-carbon bond in $\ce{C2F2}$ is longer than that of $\ce{C2F4}.$

-   B.$\hspace{1em}\ce{C2F2}$ has stronger London dispersion forces than $\ce{C2F4}.$

-   C.$\hspace{1em}$The electron domain geometry around carbon in $\ce{C2F2}$ and $\ce{C2F4}$ is the same.

-   D.$\hspace{1em}$The $\ce{F-\hat{C}-F}$ angle in $\ce{C2F2}$ is greater than in $\ce{C2F4}.$

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

**答案：D**

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:

To compare these two molecules, the student must be able to deduce their Lewis structures. Their structures are shown below.

![CH1350_Image1](images/CH1350_Image1.png)

Triple bonds are shorter than double bonds as the attraction between bonded nuclei increases with an increasing number of electrons. As a result, $\ce{C2F2}$ has a shorter carbon-to-carbon bond.

Both molecules are nonpolar. Fluorine is more electronegative than carbon, and the $\ce{C-F}$ bonds are polar. However, the bond polarities cancel.

![CH1350_Image2](images/CH1350_Image2.png)

These two molecules have London dispersion forces only. However, London dispersion forces increase with increasing molar mass. As more electrons are involved, there is a greater tendency for the random motion of electrons to cause a temporary dipole, creating temporary attractions between molecules. In this case, $\ce{C2F4}$ has two more fluorine atoms in its structure, so it has a higher molar mass and, thus, stronger London dispersion forces.

The electron domain geometry is different in these two. $\ce{C2F4}$ has a trigonal planar electron geometry around carbon, whereas $\ce{C2F2}$ has a linear geometry around carbon.

Applying the VSEPR theory to figure out the geometry around the carbons, we can deduce that in $\ce{C2F2}$ the $\ce{F-\hat{\ce{C}}-F}$ angle is $180 \degree$ whereas the same angle in $\ce{C2F4}$ is $120 \degree .$

This question is categorized as a medium as the student must deduce the Lewis structure, identify multiple bonds, apply the VSEPR theory to deduce the electron domain geometry, compare angles in these molecules, figure out the presence of dipole moment and identify the intermolecular forces present and their relative strength.
