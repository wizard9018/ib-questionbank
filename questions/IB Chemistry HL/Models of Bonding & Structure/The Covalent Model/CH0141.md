# CH0141

**Maximum mark: 5**

Kekul$\text{\'e}$ proposed in 1865 that the structure of benzene was an alternating ring of single and double bonds between 6 carbons.  Discuss whether his structure is accepted, giving two pieces of physical and two pieces of chemical evidence.

$\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
Kekul$\text{\'e}$'s proposed structure of benzene, consisting of alternating single and double bonds in a six$-$membered ring, is no longer accepted. Experimental evidence shows that all six carbon$-$carbon bonds in benzene are equivalent, whereas Kekul$\text{\'e}$'s model predicts alternating bond lengths and strengths. X$-$ray diffraction experiments show that each $\ce{C-C}$ bond has a length of about 139 pm, intermediate between a single and a double bond, and spectroscopic measurements confirm that all bonds have the same energy, indicating a delocalized $\pi-$electron system rather than localized double bonds. The molecule is also perfectly planar and symmetrical, with bond angles of $120\degree,$ consistent with a resonance$-$stabilized hexagon rather than alternating single and double bonds.

Chemically, benzene behaves very differently from alkenes. It undergoes electrophilic aromatic substitution reactions rather than addition reactions and therefore does not decolorize bromine water, showing that its $\pi-$system is unusually stable and resistant to disruption. Furthermore, the enthalpy of hydrogenation of benzene is much less exothermic than expected for a structure containing three isolated $\ce{C=C}$ bonds; this difference, known as the resonance energy, provides quantitative evidence for the stabilization associated with electron delocalization. Together, these physical and chemical properties confirm that benzene's true structure is a resonance hybrid with fully delocalized $\pi-$electrons, not an alternating pattern of single and double bonds as Kekul$\text{\'e}$'s proposed.
