# CH0712

**Maximum mark: 12**

Two common minerals found in sedimentary rocks are calcite and quartz.

1.  Calcite has the molecular formula $\ce{CaCO3}$ and contains the anion X.

    1.  State the name of anion X. [1 mark]

    2.  Draw the Lewis structure of anion X, including all atomic charges and bonding and non-bonding electron pairs. [3 marks]

    3.  State the molecular geometry of anion X. [1 mark]

    4.  Suggest why the bond angles of anion X are all exactly equal. [3 marks]

2.  Quartz has the molecular formula $\ce{SiO2}$.

    1.  Explain why the bonding in quartz differs from that in calcite based on the electronegativities given in section 8 (*Section 9- 2025 Syllabus*) of the data booklet. [2 marks]

    2.  Both calcite and quartz can be melted. Explain the electrical conductivities of the two materials after they are melted. [2 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.
    1.  Carbonate. $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        The anion is the negatively charged ion in the salt. Calcium is a metal so usually loses electrons when it forms ions, meaning that the calcium ion in $\ce{CaCO3}$ must be a cation. The -ate suffix of carbonate denotes that it is a covalent compound of oxygen with another element and that the oxidation state of the central atom is maximized. The less common -ite suffix is less common, and indicates that the central atom is bonded to fewer oxygen atoms so has a lower oxidation state. For example, sulfate has the formula $\ce{SO4^2-}$ and the oxidation state of S is 6+, while sulfite has the formula $\ce{SO3^2-}$ and the oxidation state of S is 4+.

        Do **not** award M3 if positions of negative charges are ambiguous;\
        Do **not** award M3 for negative charges drawn as $\delta$- or (-) to indicate delocalization;\
        Do **not** award M3 for negative charges drawn outside brackets to indicate delocalization.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        The Lewis structure shows the positions of all valence electrons in the molecules, as well as the formal charges of the atoms and the formal bonds between them. C is in group 14 so has four valence electrons, while O is in group 16 so has 6. Single covalent bonds are formed between the C atom and two O atoms by the sharing of single valence electron pairs. One O atom can also form a second bond by sharing an additional pair of valence electrons, but this provides the C atom with a stable octet. The remaining O atoms would have only seven valence electrons if the molecule were neutral, so electrons are removed from the calcium ions and donated to the singly bonded O atoms to produce negatively charged -O- groups. In this configuration, there are three non-bonding electron pairs on the two singly bonded O atoms and two non-bonding electron pairs on the doubly bonded O atom. In reality, the carbonate ion shows resonance: the double bond is shared across the three bonding positions and the two negative charges are shared between the three O atoms. However, resonance cannot be shown in a Lewis structure.

    3.  Trigonal planar $\checkmark$

        *Do not accept “trigonal”*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        The central C atom is surrounded by three bonding domains: a double bond and two single bonds. Since there are no lone pairs on the C atom, there are three electron domains in total. Therefore, the molecular and electron domain geometries are both trigonal planar.

    4.  The bond angles are 120$\degree$ $\checkmark$

        due to resonance/delocalization $\checkmark$

        (an effect) in which the double bond is shared between all bond positions (such that the bond angles are all equivalent) $\checkmark$

        *Award M3 for similar descriptions of resonance;*\
        *Do not award M2 if the terms “resonance” or “delocalization” are not used.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        A trigonal planar molecule has ideal bond angles of 120$\degree$. However, different bonds provide different amounts of electrostatic repulsion, so the true bond angles for a molecule containing two or more different bonds would be slightly different and not exactly equal to 120$\degree$. In the carbonate ion, any three of the O atoms could form a double bond with the C atom, as each has the same number of valence electrons to contribute. Therefore, the double bond is shared between all three positions, as are the two negative charges on the O atoms. The bonds are, therefore, equivalent (all have a bond order of 1$\frac{1}{3}$, and the bond angles are equal).

2.
    1.  The electronegativity difference for Si and O is (much) smaller than that for Ca and O; $\checkmark$
so the bonding in $\ce{SiO2}$ is (polar) covalent (whereas the bonding in $\ce{CaCO3}$ is ionic).

        *Award M1 for a calculation of the electronegativity differences in the two materials;\
        Do **not** award M1 for a comparison of the electronegativities of Si and Ca unless the importance of these values relative to the electronegativity of O is also described;\
        Do **not** award M1 if the electronegativities of the elements are stated but not compared;\
        Do **not** award M2 if the term “covalent” is not used.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        The type of bonding is determined by the electronegativity difference of the interacting elements. In both materials, O has the higher electronegativity (3.4). The electronegativity of Si is 1.9, giving an electronegativity difference of $3.4 - 1.9 = 1.5$, while the electronegativity of Ca is 1.0, giving an electronegativity difference of $3.4 - 1.0 = 2.4$. Therefore, the Si$-$O bond is much more covalent than the Ca$-$O bond. In fact, $\ce{SiO2}$ is a giant covalent solid, whereas $\ce{CaCO3}$ forms an ionic lattice, albeit with covalent bonds between the C and O atoms of the $\ce{CO3^2-}$ anion.

    2.  (Liquid) $\ce{CaCO3}$ is (electrically) conductive *AND* $\ce{SiO2}$ is (electrically) insulating; $\checkmark$

        because (liquid) $\ce{CaCO3}$ contains / $\ce{SiO2}$ does not contain ions **AND** ions can carry a current / move in response to an applied voltage. $\checkmark$

        *Award M2 for a description of conductivity in an ionic liquid if M1 is not awarded;\
        Do **not** award M1 if the properties of both materials are not explicitly stated;\
        Do **not** award M1 if $\ce{SiO2}$ is implied to be partially conductive, e.g. $\ce{SiO2}$ is less conductive than $\ce{CaCO3}$.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        A material is electrically conductive if it contains charged particles that can move in response to an applied voltage, allowing the material to complete a circuit. Although the ions in a salt are charged, solid salts such as $\ce{CaCO3}$ are not conductive because the ions are fixed in place and cannot move when a voltage is applied. Melting the solid breaks some of the bonds between the ions, so they can move more freely and carry a current. $\ce{SiO2}$ is a covalent material, so does not contain ions in either the solid or the liquid form. The atoms in the material can move more freely in the liquid but are neutral in charge, so the material remains non-conductive.
