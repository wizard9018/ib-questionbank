## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  F is more electronegative / less electropositive than I

    **OR**

    I is less electronegative / more electropositive than F $\checkmark$

    F is $\delta^-$ / I is $\delta^+$

    **OR**

    F attracts (bonding) electron density more strongly / I attracts (bonding) electron density less strongly

    **OR**

    electron density is more concentrated around the F atom / less concentrated around the I atom $\checkmark$

    because the F atom is smaller / the I atom is larger / the F atom has more shells / the I atom has fewer shells

    **AND**

    valence / outer (shell) / bonding / shared electrons are closer to the F nucleus / further from the I nucleus $\checkmark$

    *Award M2 for a diagram showing the asymmetric distribution of electron density/charge in the bond;\
    Award M3 for a diagram showing the relative sizes/shell numbers of the atoms **AND** indicating the relative attractions of valence electrons to the atomic nuclei;\
    Do **not** award M1 if the electronegativities of the atoms are not explicitly compared;\
    Do **not** award M3 if the answer does not indicate that polarity is due to valence/bonding electron distributions.*

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

    Covalent bonds are polar if the shared electrons in the bond are more strongly attracted to one atom than the other. Atoms that attract electrons more strongly are described as more electronegative.

    F is higher in group 17 than I, so has fewer electron shells, meaning that the valence electrons are closer to the nucleus. Thus, when an atom of F bonds to an atom of I, the bonding electrons are more strongly attracted to the F nucleus: the F atom is more electronegative.

    The difference in electronegativity causes electron density to be more concentrated around the F atom, giving it a partial negative ($\delta^-$) charge. The I atom has a $\delta^+$ charge.

    1.  Square pyramidal $\checkmark$

        *Do not accept “pyramidal”*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        The I atom has 7 valence electrons and forms 5 bonds, so must have a lone pair (2 electrons). There are 6 electron domains in total, so these must form an octahedral geometry. However, the molecular or bonding geometry refers only to the arrangement of bonds, not lone pairs. Four of the bonds lie approximately in the same plane, and one bond lies perpendicular to this plane, giving the molecule a square pyramidal shape if the F atoms are joined by imaginary lines.

    3.  Lone / non-bonding (electron) pairs repel more strongly than bonding (electron pairs) $\checkmark$

        so the bond angle is less than 90 $^\circ$ $\checkmark$

        *Ignore values given for bond angles, provided comparison with 90$\degree$ is correct;\
        Do **not** award M2 if the bond angle is not compared with 90$\degree$.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        Valence electron pairs repel each other electrostatically, and bonds and lone pairs are arranged to minimize this repulsion. Lone electron pairs are closer to the nucleus than bonding pairs, so repel other valence electrons more strongly. The four $\ce{I-F}$ bonds closest to the lone pair are repelled more by the lone pair than by the fifth $\ce{I-F}$ bond, causing them to approach closer to this $\ce{I-F}$ bond than they would in an octahedral compound. The bond angle is, therefore, less than 90 $^\circ$: the true value is 81.9 $^\circ$.

3. IF$_5$ forms dipole-dipole interactions (because it is asymmetric and polar) $\checkmark$

    IF$_7$ is symmetrical/pentagonal bipyramidal **AND** it is, therefore, non-polar / has no net dipole $\checkmark$

    IF$_7$ interacts via London / dispersion / induced dipole forces, **AND** these are weaker / easier to break than the (dipole-dipole) interactions of IF5 $\checkmark$

    *Do **not** award M3 if the relative strengths of the interactions in IF$_7$ and IF$_5$ are not explicitly compared*

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

    IF$_5$ and IF$_7$ both contain polar $\ce{I-F}$ bonds. In IF$_5$, the bonds are arranged asymmetrically: one side of the molecule features an $\ce{I-F}$ bond, while the other features a lone pair. The molecule, therefore, has a net dipole, possessing a partial negative ($\delta^-$) charge near the axial F atom and a partial positive ($\delta^+$) charge on the I atom. The electrostatic attractions between these charges are termed dipole-dipole forces, and they are relatively strong: there is too little heat energy to break them completely at room temperature, so IF$_5$ is a liquid. In IF$_7$, the $\ce{I-F}$ bonds are arranged symmetrically, so their dipoles cancel out to produce a molecule with no net dipole. The non-polar IF$_7$ molecules interact via London forces, wherein random small accumulations of electron density in one molecule repel electrons in another molecule to induce a small, transient dipole. These forces are weaker than permanent dipole interactions and more easily broken, so IF$_7$ is a gas.
