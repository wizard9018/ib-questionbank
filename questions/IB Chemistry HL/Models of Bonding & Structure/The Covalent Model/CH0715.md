## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1. Different forms of the same element / different (bonding) arrangements of the atoms of an element in the same physical state. $\checkmark$

 *Do **not** accept answers if the materials are not described as forms of the same element;\
 Do **not** accept answers if the materials are not described as being in the same physical state.*

 $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

 Allotropes arise when the atoms of an element can be connected by bonds in a number of different ways. Allotropes are usually giant covalent materials, although some elements (e.g. phosphorus) can form both simple covalent and giant covalent allotropes.

2. Graphite. $\checkmark$

 *Accept graphene.*

 $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

 Carbon atoms can form single bonds with each other to produce diamond, a three-dimensional lattice, or adopt trigonal planar arrangements where each atom shares four bonding electron pairs with three others. Allotropes with this structure include graphene, graphite, carbon nanotubes, and buckminsterfullerene. Boron nitride contains extended layers, so is most similar to graphite, which consists of multiple flat layers of carbon atoms. A single layer of carbon atoms in graphite is referred to as graphene.

3. The B atom only shares six / three pairs of valence/bonding electrons OR the B-N bonds (of a B atom) only contain six / three pairs of electrons, $\checkmark$

 so, it does not have a complete octet of electrons (in its outer shell) / does not obey the octet rule. $\checkmark$

 *Award M1 for a Lewis or dot-and-cross diagram showing the number of electrons in the B-N bonds;\
 Do **not** award M1 if the number of bonding/valence electrons is not stated;\
 Do **not** award M2 if the answer does not reference the octet rule.*

 $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

 Boron is in period 2 of the periodic table, so a B atom must have eight electrons (a complete octet) in its outer shell for that shell to be complete. However, boron is in group 13, so has only three valence electrons with which to form covalent bonds. The diagram shows that each B atom forms three single bonds with N atoms, resulting in a total of six shared electrons in its outer shell. The atom, therefore, has fewer than eight electrons in its outer shell and does not appear to obey the octet rule.

4.
 1. Trigonal planar. $\checkmark$

 *Do **not** accept “trigonal”;\
 Do **not** accept “planar”.*

 $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

 The N atom has three bonding domains, as shown in the diagram. If these are arranged in a plane, each bond angle must span a third of a circle, making it equal to $360\,\degree \times \frac{1}{3} = 120\,\degree$. A structure with this bond angle is described as trigonal planar.

 2. The lone pair of the N atom is shared with the B atom to form the dative bond; $\checkmark$

 Dative bonding must be present because there are only three electron domains around the N atom / all the electrons of the N atom must be involved in bonding / a lone pair on the N atom would produce a tetrahedral (electron domain) geometry. $\checkmark$

 *Award M1 for a similar description of dative bonding between B and N atoms;\
 Award M2 for similar arguments indicating that the lone pair on N is not compatible with a trigonal planar geometry;\
 Do **not** award M1 if the source of the lone pair in the dative bond is not stated.*

 $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

 The N atom appears to have four electron domains in the diagram: three single bonds and one lone pair. If the diagram is accurate, these should form a tetrahedral arrangement with a bond angle of slightly less than 109.5 $\degree$. The fact that the observed bond angle is 120 $\degree$ indicates that the electron domain geometry is, in fact, trigonal planar, and that the lone pair on the N atom must actually be involved in bonding. The lone pair is shared with the B atom, giving it a complete octet of electrons and producing a fourth covalent bond. A bond of this type, in which the pair of bonding electrons originate from only one of the atoms, is termed a dative covalent bond.

 3. Resonance/delocalization occurs (in the bonding arrangement), $\checkmark$

 as the dative/double bond is shared between the three bond positions. $\checkmark$

 *Award M1 for a similar description of dative bonding between B and N atoms;\
 Award M2 for similar arguments indicating that the lone pair on N is not compatible with a trigonal planar geometry;\
 Award M2 for similar definitions of resonance;\
 Award M2 for a diagram illustrating the resonance in boron nitride;\
 Do **not** award M1 if the terms “resonance”, “delocalization” or “delocalized” are not used.*

 $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

 The lone pair of the N atom can be shared with any of the three bonded B atoms, but only one dative bond can form in total. Since the B atoms are all equally able to form a dative bond, the bond is shared over the three bond positions, and each bond is partway between a single and a double bond (the bond order is 1$\frac{1}{3}$). Thus, all the bonds are the same length. This effect is called resonance and may be represented by a series of three structures with the double bond drawn in each of the three possible positions. The shared pair of bonding electrons is referred to as delocalized, as it is not positioned between a single pair of bonded atoms.

5. The layers in graphite / the carbon allotrope interact by London / dispersion / induced dipole forces. $\checkmark$\
The layers in boron nitride interact by dipole-dipole forces. $\checkmark$

 Dipole-dipole forces are stronger than dispersion forces, **AND** this makes it harder for the layers to slide over each other/separate / break apart (in response to an applied force). $\checkmark$

 *Award M1 for a correct description of the London forces in graphite;\
 Award M2 for a correct description of the interactions between $\delta$+ and $\delta$- atoms in adjacent layers;\
 Award M2 for a diagram illustrating the dipole-dipole interactions between layers;\
 Do **not** award M3 unless the sliding of layers is described and the interlayer interactions in graphite and boron nitride are compared.*

 $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

 Both graphite and boron nitride are relatively soft because the layers of atoms are only linked by van der Waals forces. Thus, the layers in both materials can slide over each other or become separated if a force is applied. Graphite contains only carbon atoms, so the bonds are non-polar, and the layers are linked by weak London forces only. B$-$N bonds are polar in boron nitride because the N atoms are more electronegative than the B atoms. Dipole-dipole forces arise due to electrostatic attraction between the $\delta -$ N atoms in one layer and the $\delta +$ B atoms in another. These forces are stronger than the London forces in graphite. Therefore, the layers in boron nitride are less able to move over each other in response to a force.
