# CH2718

**Maximum mark: 6**

A student used paper chromatography to investigate the dyes in the coloured coating of two brands of candy. Spots of each extract were placed on an origin line and the chromatogram was developed in a sealed chamber with an aqueous salt solution as the mobile phase. The developed chromatogram is shown in **Figure 1.**

![CH2718a](images/CH2718a.png)

**Figure 1:** Developed chromatogram of the extracts from the two candy coatings. Lane 1 is the extract from the first brand and lane 2 the extract from the second.

1. State why the origin line is drawn in pencil rather than in ink. [1 mark]

2. Outline why the origin line must be above the level of the mobile phase when the paper is placed in the chamber. [1 mark]

3. Calculate the retardation factor, $R_\textrm{f},$ of each dye. [2 marks]

4. Deduce, with a reason, which dye has the stronger affinity for the mobile phase. [1 mark]

5. The extract from one brand produced a single spot in this solvent, but the student suspects it contains two different dyes. Suggest one modification to the experiment that could separate them. [1 mark]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1. ink dissolves in the solvent  **AND** graphite is insoluble $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    Pencil marks are graphite, a form of carbon that is insoluble in the solvent, so a pencil line stays in place and adds nothing to the chromatogram. Ink is itself a mixture of soluble dyes, so an ink line would dissolve in the solvent as it passes, separate on the paper and add extra spots that could be confused with the dyes from the samples.

2.  spots / analytes would dissolve into the (bulk) mobile phase instead of travelling up the paper $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    Separation happens only while the solvent moves through the spots by capillary action. If the origin line starts below the liquid surface, the samples dissolve into the solvent in the chamber and no spots develop on the paper.

3. $R_\textrm{f}(\textrm{A}) = 0.80$ $\checkmark$ \
    $R_\textrm{f}(\textrm{B}) = 0.25$ $\checkmark$

    *Accept 0.79 to 0.81 for A and 0.24 to 0.26 for B.*

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    The retardation factor is the distance travelled by the spot divided by the distance travelled by the solvent front, both measured from the origin line. It is a ratio of lengths, so it has no units and can never exceed 1: a spot cannot move further than the solvent that carries it.

    ![CH2718ms](images/CH2718ms.png)

    Spot A: $R_f = \dfrac{6.4 \textrm{\ cm}}{8.0 \textrm{\ cm}} = 0.80$

    Spot B: $R_f = \dfrac{2.0 \textrm{\ cm}}{8.0 \textrm{\ cm}} = 0.25$

4. dye A **AND** it has the higher $R_\textrm{f} \: \checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
     Each dye continually partitions between the moving solvent and the stationary paper surface. The further a spot travels, the larger the fraction of time the dye spends in the mobile phase. $R_\textrm{f} = 0.80$ means dye A moved at 80% of the speed of the solvent front. With an aqueous mobile phase, the higher $R_\textrm{f}$ also identifies dye A as the more polar of the two, since the polar solvent carries the polar dye preferentially.

5. any **one** of the following:\
    $\mbox{}\\[-25pts]$

    -   use a different solvent $\checkmark$
    -   two$-$dimensional chromatography / rotate  paper $90{}^\circ$ and develop with a second solvent $\checkmark$
    -   use a different stationary phase / thin$-$layer chromatography $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    One spot in one solvent shows only that the two candidate dyes have equal $R_\textrm{f}$ values in that solvent, not that they are the same substance. Two substances rarely share $R_\textrm{f}$ values in two different solvent systems, so a second development, especially at right angles to the first, can separate them.
