## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  *electron domain geometry:* tetrahedral $\checkmark$\
    *molecular shape:* bent / V$\text{--}$shaped $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    The Lewis structure of $\ce{H2Se}$ should have $2\times 1 + 6 = 8$ valence electrons. Selenium is the central atom with single bonds to the two hydrogen atoms and two nonbonding pairs on the selenium atom.

    ![Screenshot 2025-03-10 at 12.41.04 PM](<images/Screenshot 2025-03-10 at 12.41.04 PM.png>)

    There are four electron domains around selenium: two bonding and two nonbonding pairs. According to the valence shell electron pair repulsion theory (VSEPR), the electron domain geometry is tetrahedral to maximize the distance between the electron pairs. Since two are nonbonding pairs, the shape of the molecule is bent or V$\text{--}$shaped.

    ![CH2064ans2](images/CH2064ans2.png)

2.  nonpolar molecules are attracted London dispersion forces which increase with molecular mass / molecular size / number of electrons $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    The boiling points of the Group 14 hydrides increase down the group. This is because as you move down Group 14 the molecular mass increases, leading to stronger London dispersion forces (temporary dipole$\text{--}$induced dipole interactions).    Larger atoms have more electrons, making them more polarizable, which further strengthens these intermolecular forces.

    Since boiling point is determined by the strength of intermolecular forces, more energy is required to overcome the stronger London dispersion forces in heavier hydrides, leading to a higher boiling point.

3.  electronegativity difference decreases down  group $\checkmark$\
    partial charges are smaller, bond dipole is weaker $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    The dipole moment of the Group 17 hydrides decreases down the group. The dipole moment is directly related to the difference in electronegativity between hydrogen and the halogen. Fluorine is the most electronegative element, so HF has the most polar bond and the largest dipole moment.

    Moving down the group, the electronegativity of the halogen decreases, reducing the bond polarity and thus the dipole moment.

4.  water forms ***four*** strong intermolecular interactions, while HF and $\ce{NH3}$ form only two  $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    In water, the geometry allows the molecule to be a hydrogen bond acceptor to two adjacent molecules and a hydrogen bond donor to another two adjacent hydrogen bonds.

    ![CH2064h2o](images/CH2064h2o.png)

    Water acts as both a donor and acceptor for two bonds, forming a highly interconnected network of strong hydrogen bonding. The hydrogen bonds are oriented on a line through a lone pair on the acceptor molecule to the $\ce{O-H}$ bond on the donor molecule.

    In ammonia, only one hydrogen can be used as a hydrogen bond donor and the single lone pair can accept one hydrogen bond. Similarly, in hydrogen fluoride, only one lone pair can accept a hydrogen bond and the single hydrogen can be a hydrogen bond donor. There is a balance between the number of total acceptors and donors in the liquid.

5.  $-67 < T_b <-2\ \checkmark$\
    value should be accepted $\checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
    Going down a group, the trend shows the boiling points increasing due to the increase in molecular mass / size / number of electrons.  The boiling point should be higher than $-67^\circ\text{C.}$ Going across a period, Group 17 hydrides have a lower boiling point than the Group 16 hydrides so the boiling point should be less than $-2^\circ\text{C}.$ The value $-35^\circ\text{C}$ should be accepted as it is consistent with the patterns and trends in the data.

    3.  molecular symmetry and lone pair effects must also be considered when evaluating dipole moment $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$\
        The Van Arkel$\text{--}$Ketelaar triangle categorizes bonding based on percent ionicity and electronegativity difference but does not account for molecular geometry, which is essential in determining dipole moment. Even if a molecule has polar bonds, its overall dipole moment depends on whether the individual bond dipoles cancel due to symmetry. For example, tetrahedral molecules like $\ce{SiH4}$ have polar bonds but no net dipole moment.
