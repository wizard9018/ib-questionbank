## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  Amide $\checkmark$

    *Do **not** accept “peptide”*

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

    The amine group reacts with the carboxylic acid group to produce a new C-N bond, with water as a by-product. The new functional group is similar to an ester but with the $\ce{C-O-C}$ linkage replaced by a C-NH-C linkage. This functional group is called an amide.

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

    Amides are produced from amine $\ce{(-NH2)}$ and carboxylic acid $\ce{(-COOH)}$ groups. Each molecule of **A** contains a carboxylic acid group at one end and an amine group at the other. Depending on the pH, these functional groups may be ionized. At neutral pH, for example, the carboxylic acid group donates a hydrogen ion to the basic amine group to produce a carboxylate $\ce{(-COO^-)}$ anion and ammonium $\ce{(-NH_3^+)}$ cation. The rest of the molecule is unaffected by the condensation reaction.

3.  The carboxylic acid and hydroxyl (groups) may react $\checkmark$

    to produce an ester group $\checkmark$

    *Award M1 if “alcohol” used in place of “hydroxyl”*

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

    The amine and hydroxyl groups in **A** are both nucleophilic: they may both donate a lone electron pair to the carbon atom of the carboxylic acid group, producing a new bond and eliminating water as a by-product.

    If the hydroxyl group attacks the carboxylic acid group, an ester is formed. Under acidic conditions, the basic amine group may accept a hydrogen ion to form a non-reactive ammonium ion, such that a reaction with the hydroxyl group becomes more likely.

    A mixture of the two reactions may also occur, producing a polymer with a combination of amide and ester linkages.

4.  The amide group can form hydrogen bonds with water (in both polymers) $\checkmark$

    **B** contains hydroxyl groups, so it can form additional hydrogen bonds (making it more soluble) $\checkmark$

    *Award M1 for diagram showing hydrogen bonds between amide group and water; \
    Award M2 for diagram showing hydrogen bonds between hydroxyl group and water; \
    Award M2 if “alcohol” used in place of “hydroxyl”; \
    Award [1] maximum if phrase “hydrogen bond” not used.*

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

    When a compound dissolves in water, the strong hydrogen bonds between water molecules are broken and replaced with solute-solvent interactions. Interactions between the solute molecules themselves must also be broken. The compound is soluble if the energy released by the formation of water-solute interactions can compensate for the energy absorbed when the original bonds are broken.

    In **B**, the amide and hydroxyl groups can both form hydrogen bonds with water: both groups feature a $\delta^+$ H atom that can interact with the lone pair of the water O atom, and they also feature a lone pair that can interact with the $\delta^+$ H atoms of water.

    Compound **C** also contains amide groups, so it is still able to dissolve by forming strong hydrogen bonds with water. However, it lacks the hydroxyl groups of **B**, so fewer hydrogen bonds can be formed. Thus, **C** is less soluble in water than **B**.
