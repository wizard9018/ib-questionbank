## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  Tertiary, $\checkmark$

    because the carbon atom is bonded to three carbon atoms. $\checkmark$

    *Award M2 if the difference between primary, secondary and tertiary atoms is correctly described, even if the labelled carbon atom is incorrectly categorised.*

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

    The terms primary, secondary and tertiary are used to indicate whether a carbon atom is attached to one, two or three other carbon atoms. The labelled carbon atom is attached to three other carbon atoms so is tertiary. The number of groups around the carbon atom is important because it determines the reactivity of that atom; for example, tertiary carbon atoms are less able to undergo nucleophilic substitution reactions, because the attached carbon atoms are relatively large and act as a barrier to incoming nucleophiles.

2.
    1.  Alkene group drawn with explicit C=C bond; $\checkmark$

        Each C atom of $\ce{C=C}$ bond linked to one H atom and one $\ce{CH3}$ group. $\checkmark$

        ![CH0799-MS](images/CH0799-MS.png)

        *Accept both geometric isomers;\
        Award [2] if correct skeletal structural formula drawn;\
        Award [1] if the full structural formula of butane, but-1-ene or butyne is correctly drawn.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        An alkene is a compound containing a C=C double bond. When an alkene undergoes addition polymerisation, the $\ce{C=C}$ bond breaks and each C atom in the bond forms a new single $\ce{C-C}$ bond with an alkene C atom in another molecule. The product is a long-chain alkane with a repeating structure.

        In polymer **P**, each C atom in the main chain is bonded to a methyl $\ce{(CH3)}$ group and an H atom, so each alkene C atom in compound **A** must also be bonded to these groups.

        Compound **A** therefore contains a chain of four carbon atoms, with the $\ce{C=C}$ bond between the second and third atoms.

    2.  Compound A = but-2-ene $\checkmark$

        Isomers = but-1-ene

        **AND**

        2-methylpropene / 2-methylprop-1-ene $\checkmark$

        *Award M1 for but-1-ene or 2-methylpropene / 2-methylprop-1-ene if name is based on a follow-through error from (i);\
        Award M2 for but-2-ene and its isomer if names are based on a follow-through error from (i);\
        Award [1] if two or three compounds are correctly named but compound **A** is not clearly identified;\
        Do **not** award M2 if only one isomer is correctly named.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        Structural isomers are compounds that contain the same atoms bonded in a different arrangement. They have the same molecular formula. Compound **A** is an alkene so its name ends with the suffix -ene. The name begins with the prefix but-, as there are four carbon atoms in the chain of the molecule. Finally, the number 2 is included to indicate that the alkene group begins at the second carbon atom from the nearest end of the chain.

        The isomer but-1-ene also contains a linear chain of four carbon atoms, but with the alkene between the first and second atoms.

        Conversely, 2-methylpropene contains only three carbon atoms in its longest chain: the fourth carbon atom is present as a methyl ($\ce{CH3}$) group attached to the second atom of the propyl chain. The position of the alkene in 2-methylpropene can be omitted, as only one position is possible.

    3.  (A change from) yellow / orange to colourless. $\checkmark$

        *Accept suitable alternative descriptions for the colour of aqueous bromine, e.g. red-orange;\
        Award [0] if the colours before and after the reaction are not stated;\
        Award [0] if the direction of the colour change is not indicated.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        Aqueous bromine, or bromine water, is a solution of elemental bromine ($\ce{Br2}$) in water. Dilute aqueous bromine is yellow, while more concentrated solutions are orange. Bromine reacts with the alkene group in compound **A** by electrophilic addition, causing the C=C double bond and $\ce{Br-Br}$ single bond to break and two new $\ce{C-Br}$ bonds to form. The product is a dibromoalkane. This compound is colourless, so the yellow / orange colour of the solution is lost as $\ce{Br2}$ is consumed.

    4.  The (molecular) formula is $\ce{C8H16}$

        **OR**

        the empirical formula is $\ce{CH2}$

        **OR**

        there are 2 H atoms per C atom $\checkmark$

        Combustion (analysis) reveals the empirical formula / the number of H atoms per C atom / the percentage composition $\checkmark$

        The compounds (**A** and **B**) have the same empirical formula / the same number of H atoms per C atom / the same percentage composition $\checkmark$

        *Award M1 if the relative numbers of C and H atoms in compound **B** are indicated;\
        Award M2 if the answer refers to the measurement of relative quantities of elements;\
        Do **not** award M3 if the answer does not compare the relative quantities of elements in the compounds.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        When an organic compound undergoes complete combustion, $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ are the only products. Since C is found only in $\ce{CO2}$ and H is found only in $\ce{H2O}$, the relative numbers of $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ molecules can be used to determine the C/H ratio of the original compound.

        Compounds **A** ($\ce{C4H8}$) and **B** ($\ce{C8H16}$) have different molecular formulas but the same empirical formula of $\ce{CH2}$, since they are both linear alkenes. Compounds with the same empirical formula produce the same relative numbers of $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ molecules, so they cannot be distinguished.
