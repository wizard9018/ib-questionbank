# CH2102

[Maximmum marks: 6]

Silver reacts with fluorine to form silver fluoride. Silver fluoride has a high melting point and exhibits low volatility.

1.  State the equation, including state symbols, showing the synthesis of silver fluoride from its elements. [1 mark]

2.  Write an equation, including state symbols, that shows the process associated with the lattice enthalpy of silver fluoride. [1 mark]

3.  Consider two more common fluorides of Group 1 metals, sodium fluoride and potassium fluoride.

    1.  Using Section 10 of the IB Chemistry data booklet, predict how the melting point of sodium and potassium fluoride would compare with that of silver fluoride.  Explain your reasoning.[2 marks]

    2.  Suggest two other factors that must be considered in correctly ranking the melting points of these solids.[2 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  $\ce{Ag(s) + \frac{1}{2} F2(g) -> AgF(s)}\ \checkmark$

    *Accept balanced equation with whole number coefficients 2, 1, 2.*

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$

    Silver metal reacts with the diatomic gas fluorine to produce an ionic compound.  Section 9 shows that the preferred charge of silver ions is $1+$ and the preferred charge of fluoride ion is $1-,$ the two ions react in a $1:1$ ratio to form the ionic salt AgF.

2.  $\ce{AgF(s) -> Ag+(g) + F-(g)}\ \checkmark$

    $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$

    Lattice enthalpies, in Section 16 of the IB Chemistry data booklet, express the energy associated with the reaction

    $\ce{M_aX_b(s) -> a M^{b+}(g) + b X^{a-}(g)}$

    where the gaseous ions of a crystal are separated to infinite distance. For silver (I) fluoride, $a$ and $b$ are both 1 so,

    $\ce{AgF(s) -> Ag+(g) + F-(g)}$

3.
    1.  NaF > AgF > KF $\checkmark$

        melting point decreases as cation size increases $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Explanation:}}$

        The melting point of an ionic compound is primarily determined by the strength of the electrostatic attraction between its ions.  The strength of the ionic bonds can be modeled using Coulomb's law,

        $F \propto \dfrac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$

        where $q_1$ is the charge on the cation, $q_2$ is the charge on the anion, and $r$ is the distance between their centers (or the sum of the ionic radii).

        For each of these compounds the charges are equal so the strength of the ionic bond will be affected by the size of the cation: the largest cation having the weakest ionic bond and the lowest melting point.  Section 9 of the data booklet gives the ionic radii: $r_\ce{Na+} = 102\text{ pm},$ $r_\ce{K+} = 138\text{ pm},$ and $r_\ce{Ag+} = 115\text{ pm}.$ Based on this data, the melting points are expected to follow the pattern NaF > AgF > KF.

    2.  % ionicity / covalent character / polarizability / difference in electronegativity $\checkmark$

        crystal packing / number of nearest neighbors / coordination number $\checkmark$

        $\textrm{\underline{Further Information:}}$

        If the cation and anion have a smaller difference in electronegativity, the bond may have some covalent character rather than being purely ionic. This can weaken the ionic attraction and result in a lower melting point. For example, $\ce{Ag}$ is less electronegative than $\ce{Na},$ and its bond with F may be slightly less ionic, reducing the melting point of AgF compared to NaF.

        Even if ionic radii are similar, differences in how ions pack in the crystal lattice can affect the overall stability and melting point. More efficient packing generally leads to stronger lattice forces and higher melting points.
