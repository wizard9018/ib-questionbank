## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.
    1. (The melting point decreases because) the size of the metal atom / atomic radius / ionic radius increases down the group, $\checkmark$

        so the metal ions/nuclei are less strongly attracted (electrostatically) to the delocalized electrons. $\checkmark$

        *Do **not** award M2 unless the electrons responsible for metallic bonding are described as delocalized;\
        Do **not** award M2 if the attraction between ions/nuclei and electrons is not explicitly described.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        The alkali metals all have one electron in their outer shell. This valence electron is shared between all the atoms in the material, producing a sea of delocalized electrons surrounding an ordered lattice of metal ions. Metallic bonding results from the electrostatic attraction between the delocalized valence electrons and the positive metal ions. The number of inner shells is greater for elements further down the group, so the atomic/ionic radius is larger, and the delocalized valence electrons are further from the nucleus. Thus, the electrostatic attraction between the nuclei and the delocalized electrons is weaker for the heavier elements, resulting in a weaker metallic bond and a lower melting point.

    2.  The bonding in LiBr is (much) more covalent / less ionic (than the bonding in NaBr). $\checkmark$

        *Award [0] if the bonding in LiBr is stated to be (purely) covalent / not ionic*.

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        The electronegativities of Li, Na and Br are 1.0, 0.9 and 3.0, respectively. The bonding in LiBr should be slightly more covalent than the bonding in NaBr, as the electronegativity difference is smaller. However, the diagram shows that there is much more sharing of electron density in LiBr, indicating that the bonding is much more covalent. Covalent materials have lower melting points than purely ionic materials, so LiBr has a lower melting point than NaBr, in contrast to the trend observed for the rest of the group 1 metals. Between NaBr and CsBr, the melting point decreases because the metal ion becomes larger and less densely charged, such that it is further from the bromide ion and less strongly attracted to it. The anomalous behavior of Li is not due to the slightly larger electronegativity of this metal but because the lithium ion is so small and densely charged that it can polarize the large bromide ion to create a region of high electron density between the ions.

2.
    1.  Lithium / the metal contains delocalized electrons which are mobile / can move in response to an applied voltage / can carry a current; $\checkmark$

        Lithium bromide / the salt contains ions which are not free to move / cannot move in response to an applied voltage / cannot carry a current. $\checkmark$

        *Do **not** award M1 unless the charge carrier is stated **AND** its mobility described;\
        Do **not** award M2 unless the charge carrier is stated **AND** its mobility described;\
        Award [1] if the charge carriers and charge mobility in a metal and a salt are both correctly described, but the identities of the materials being described are ambiguous.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        A material is conductive if it contains charged particles that can move in response to an applied voltage, producing a current. Metals are conductive because the valence electrons of the atoms are delocalized, meaning that they are free to move throughout the material. Solid salts contain ions, which are charged, but these are fixed in place by ionic bonds so cannot move to give rise to a current. Thus, solid salts are electrical insulators.

    2.  The ionic bonds are broken, **AND** ion-dipole interactions/forces form (with water); $\checkmark$

        The ions in the solution can move (in response to an applied voltage). $\checkmark$

        *Award M1 for a description of an ion-dipole interaction, e.g. the bromide ion interacts with a $\delta +$ H atom;\
        Award M1 for a diagram illustrating an ion-dipole interaction;\
        Do **not** award M1 unless the bonding before **AND** after dissolution is described.*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:

        Although ionic bonds are very strong, the ions in a salt become separated in water and the solid dissolves. Dissolution is possible because the ionic bonds are replaced by a large number of moderately strong ion-dipole forces: the bromide ion is attracted electrostatically to the $\delta +$ H atoms of the water molecules, and the lithium ion is attracted to the $\delta -$ O atom.
