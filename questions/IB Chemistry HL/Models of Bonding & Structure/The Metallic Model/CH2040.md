# CH2040

Increasing which factor will increase the strength of a metallic bond?

-   A. $\hspace{1em}$The quantity of delocalized electrons

-   B. $\hspace{1em}$The density of the metal

-   C. $\hspace{1em}$The electronegativity of the metal atoms

-   D. $\hspace{1em}$The number of energy levels in the metal atom

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

**答案：A**

$\textrm{\underline{Explanation:}}$

The strength of a metallic bond is determined by the electrostatic attraction between the positive metal cations and the sea of delocalized electrons that move freely throughout the metallic structure. Several factors influence the strength of this attraction, including the number of delocalized electrons, charge density, and atomic radius.

The strength of a metallic bond increases with an increasing number of delocalized electrons. Transition metals, in particular, exhibit stronger metallic bonding than main group metals due to the greater availability of delocalized electrons. Consider magnesium and nickel: both commonly form 2+ ions and have similar ionic radii (72 pm *vs.* 69 pm, respectively), yet their metallic bonding strengths differ significantly. Magnesium melts at $650.0^\circ\text{C},$ while nickel, with its stronger metallic bonding, has a much higher melting point of $1455^\circ\text{C}.$

While metallic bonding strength affects density, density itself does not directly determine bond strength. Density depends on atomic mass and packing structure, not just metallic bonding. Consider lead and aluminum: lead is much more dense than aluminum  but has a much lower melting point.

Electronegativity is a measure of an atom's ability to attract bonding electrons in a covalent bond, not a metallic bond. Since metals readily lose electrons to form the delocalized electron sea, their electronegativity is low and does not play a significant role in determining metallic bond strength.

As the number of energy levels (electron shells) increases, atomic radius increases, and the electrostatic attraction between the nucleus and the delocalized electrons weakens. This actually leads to weaker metallic bonding. For example, Cs has weaker metallic bonds than Li due to its larger atomic size, despite being in the same group.
