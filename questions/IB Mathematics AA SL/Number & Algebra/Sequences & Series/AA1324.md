# AA1324

**Maximum mark: 6**

A geometric sequence has a first term of $81$, and the sum of the first $4$ terms is $195$.

1.    Find the value of the common ratio, $r$. [2 marks]

The fourth term of this geometric sequence is the sixth term of an arithmetic sequence.

Let $S_n$ denote the sum of the first $n$ terms of the arithmetic sequence.

It is also known that $S_{20}=S_{11}$.

2.    Find the largest possible value of $S_n$. [4 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1. We can use the formula for the sum of the first $n$ terms of a geometric sequence where $S_4=195$, $u_1=81$ and $n=4$, to form an equation.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    S_n&=u_1\left(\frac{r^n-1}{r-1}\right)\\[12pt]

    195&=81\left(\frac{r^4-1}{r-1}\right)\tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    By graphing the L.H.S. and R.H.S. as two functions

    ![AA1324-a](images/AA1324-a.JPEG)

    we find

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    r&=0.6666...\\[6pt]

    &=0.667\tag*{\textbf{A1}}\\[6pt]

    \bigg(&=\frac{2}{3}\bigg)

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Note, you could have also solved the equation $81+81r+81r^2+81r^3=195$ on your G.D.C.

2. As we need to find the maximum value of the sum of the arithmetic sequence, we must find the first term of the arithmetic sequence, $a$, and the common difference, $d$.

    We will begin by finding the fourth term of the geometric sequence.

    Using the general term of a geometric sequence we get

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    u_4&=81 \times \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{4-1}\\[6pt]

    &=24

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    We can use the result above to form an equation for the sixth term of the arithmetic sequence

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    24&=a+(6-1)\times d\\[6pt]

    24&=a+5d \hspace{2em}[1]\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Using the formula for the sum of the first $n$ terms of an arithmetic sequence, $S_n=\frac{n}{2}(2a+(n-1)d)$ we can rewrite the given relation in terms of $a$ and $d$

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    S_{20}&=S_{11}\\[6pt]

    \frac{20}{2}(2a+19d)&=\frac{11}{2}(2a+10d)  \hspace{2em}[2]\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    We now have two linear equations $[1]$ and $[2]$, which we can solve using our G.D.C.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    a&=36&d=&-\frac{12}{5}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    We can now find $S_n$ in terms of $n$

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    S_n&=\frac{n}{2}\bigg[2(36)+(n-1)\left(-\frac{12}{5}\right)\bigg]\tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    We could simplify this expression. However, as this is a paper $2$ question, it is better practice to not simplify unless you are required to.

    

    We need to find the maximum value of $S_n$ remembering the fact that this represents a sequence and is only valid for positive integer values of $n$.

    

    Using a table of values is a better approach than graphing, to find the maximum value.

    | $n$ | $S_n$ |

    | --- | --- |

    |&nbsp; $13$ &nbsp;|&nbsp; $280.8$ &nbsp;|

    |&nbsp; $14$ &nbsp;|&nbsp; $285.6$ &nbsp;|

    |&nbsp; $15$ &nbsp;|&nbsp; $\textcolor{red}{288}$ &nbsp;|

    |&nbsp; $16$ &nbsp;|&nbsp; $\textcolor{red}{288}$ &nbsp;|

    |&nbsp; $17$ &nbsp;|&nbsp; $285.6$ &nbsp;|

    |&nbsp; $18 $ &nbsp;|&nbsp; $280.8$ &nbsp;|

    We can see that the $15$th and $16$th terms both give the same maximum value of $S_n$.

    Hence the maximum value of $S_n$ is $288$.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Note that graphing the function $y=\dfrac{x}{2}\bigg[2(36)+(x-1)\left(-\frac{12}{5}\right)\bigg]$ on your G.D.C. gives the maximum point $(15.5, 288.3)$. If you gave the answer as $288.3$ then you would not receive the final A$1$ mark as this is not a valid value for the sum of the sequence as $n$ must be a whole number.

    

    This is why, often when working with sequences, it is better practice to use a table of values.
