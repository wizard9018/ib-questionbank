Any combination of the following may be considered:
- Social discrimination and segregation
    - The Hispanic American movement emerged in the mid-20th century as a response to exclusion, exploitation, and cultural marginalization.
    - Social dynamics, including racism, segregation, and rapid population growth, spurred grassroots organizing, as women and youth expanded their activism.
    - Underfunded, struggling schools with high dropout rates were common in Hispanic communities and drove activism like the East LA Walkouts of 1968.
- Economic exploitation and labor inequality
    - Immigration policies, particularly the Bracero Program, underscored the contradictions between reliance on immigrant labor and their limited legal protections.
    - Hispanic communities were largely dependent upon agricultural labor and unskilled industrial work, which provided poor wages, long hours, and unsafe working conditions.
    - Economic struggles in low-wage sectors fueled union activism, most notably among the United Farm Workers (UFW), led by César Chávez and Dolores Huerta.
- Political marginalization and lack of representation
    - Hispanics/Latinos were underrepresented in political institutions from local to national levels
    - Faced barriers to voting, including literacy tests and intimidation, into the 1960s.
    - Moments of progress, such as the Civil Rights Act (1964) and the Voting Rights Act (1965), initially focused on African Americans, driving the creation of new political organizations like La Raza Unida Party (1970).
- Cultural identity and Chicano nationalism
    - The ideas of the Chicano Movement emphasized cultural pride, indigenous heritage, and bilingual education, thereby shaping a shared Hispanic identity.
- Wider context of the Civil Rights and Anti-War Movements in the US
    - Politically influenced by African American civil rights, groups such as LULAC and MAPA fought for political representation and reform.
    - Many young Hispanic/Latino people served in Vietnam, but returned to continued marginalization, driving activism.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Economic and political inequality were the driving factors in the emergence of the Hispanic and Latino Civil Rights movements in the US. Under policies like the Bracero Program, employers benefited while workers remained vulnerable and powerless against abuse ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Alongside these economic struggles, Hispanic and Latino communities also faced significant political marginalization. Across local governments and national institutions, they were consistently underrepresented and had their voices excluded from decision-making processes that directly affected their lives ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). This lack of representation perpetuated policies that ignored or undermined their needs, reinforcing cycles of disenfranchisement. Political invisibility and economic exploitation were not accidental, but rather the product of systemic barriers deliberately designed to limit participation ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). These limits would also serve to inspire the emergence of the broader Hispanic and Latino Civil Rights Movement in the US ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$).

The history of Hispanic and Latino labor in the U.S. is tied to economic exploitation and systemic inequality. Immigration policies such as the Bracero Program (1942-1964) reveal the contradictions of relying on immigrant labor while providing limited protections. Created to recruit Mexican agricultural workers during WWII, it supplied cheap labor but institutionalized disposable treatment. Braceros faced exploitative contracts, had their rights denied, and endured poor living conditions. During this era, Hispanic communities were largely dependent on agricultural labor and unskilled industrial work. These sectors offered meager wages, long hours, and unsafe working conditions. Agricultural workers faced exposure to pesticides, inadequate housing, and limited access to healthcare. Industrial jobs in factories and meatpacking plants were similarly grueling, with little opportunity for advancement ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). This economic reality reinforced cycles of poverty, restricted educational opportunities, and perpetuated systemic inequality across generations. Although reliance on Hispanic labor was undeniable, the economic system consistently undervalued their contributions, treating them as a cheap workforce rather than as citizens deserving of dignity and rights ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Out of these struggles emerged union activism, most notably the United Farm Workers (UFW). Founded in the 1960s by César Chávez and Dolores Huerta, the UFW became a powerful symbol of resistance to exploitation. Chávez and Huerta mobilized farmworkers to demand fair wages, safer working conditions, and recognition of their humanity. Through strikes, boycotts, and grassroots organizing, the UFW brought national attention to the plight of agricultural workers. Their activism underscored the broader struggle for labor rights in America, linking Hispanic workers' fight to the larger civil rights movement ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Campaigns such as the grape boycott ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$) demonstrated the power of collective action to challenge entrenched systems of inequality. Although victories were often partial and hard-won, the union's work laid the foundation for future labor movements and highlighted the resilience of Hispanic communities amid economic exploitation ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In the political realm, voting rights were among the most contested issues. Well into the 1960s, Hispanics and Latinos faced barriers such as literacy tests, poll taxes, and outright intimidation at the polls. These measures suppressed participation among working-class and immigrant communities, many of whom had limited access to formal education due to economic hardship ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). The denial of voting rights left Hispanic communities with little influence over policies that shaped their economic and social realities. Even when eligible to vote, many were discouraged by hostile environments that treated their participation as unwelcome or illegitimate ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Nonetheless, progress emerged during the broader civil rights era, though it often focused on African American disenfranchisement. However, this progress also laid the foundation for Hispanic activists to build on ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Inspired by the era's momentum, new political organizations arose to represent Latino interests more directly. One of the most notable was the La Raza Unida Party, ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$) founded in 1970. The party sought to challenge the two-party system by advocating for issues specific to Mexican Americans and other Latino groups, including education reform, labor rights, and political representation ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Though its influence was geographically and temporally limited, La Raza Unida symbolized a growing awareness of the need for independent political power among Hispanic communities ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

The creation of such organizations reflected a broader shift: Hispanic and Latino activists recognized that economic justice could not be achieved without political empowerment. The struggles of farm workers, factory laborers, and marginalized communities were closely tied to a lack of political representation. By organizing politically, they sought to break the cycle of exploitation and disenfranchisement and to assert their right to participate fully in American democracy ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Historians interpret these developments differently. Mae Ngai argues that programs such as the Bracero Program created a racialized labor hierarchy that left Mexican workers vulnerable. She links reliance on immigrant labor and the denial of rights to broader exploitation, thereby maintaining inequality and limiting upward mobility for Latinos. Conversely, David G. Gutiérrez highlights Hispanic communities' agency in resisting these systems. While acknowledging exploitation, he points to the resilience of groups such as the United Farm Workers and La Raza Unida Party, viewing their struggles as catalysts for social change tied to the civil rights movement. Their views reveal a tension: Ngai emphasizes systemic exclusion and exploitation, while Gutiérrez focuses on grassroots efforts to transform labor and politics ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$).

The struggles against economic exploitation and political marginalization highlight the systemic challenges that Hispanic and Latino communities faced in the mid-20th-century United States. The Bracero Program exposed the contradictions of a nation that relied on immigrant labor yet failed to protect workers. While agricultural and industrial work sustained Hispanic communities, it also brought poverty and hardship. Union leaders such as César Chávez and Dolores Huerta responded with activism, demonstrating the power of collective action to challenge exploitation. Political marginalization limited their influence on policy, but progress such as the Civil Rights and Voting Rights Acts opened new possibilities and inspired groups like La Raza Unida Party. These struggles reveal Hispanic resilience and determination to fight for economic justice and political empowerment, laying a foundation for future generations and keeping the pursuit of equality central to America ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
