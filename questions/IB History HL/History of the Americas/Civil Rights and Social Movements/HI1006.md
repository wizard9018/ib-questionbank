# HI1006

To what extent did the United States governmental institutions further the rights of indigenous people between 1945 and 1980?

There are **three** fundamental responses to the question above: 

 - A response that rejects the role of governmental institutions in helping to further the rights of indigenous people. 

 - A response that supports the impact of governmental institutions in helping indigenous people.

 - A response that argues a balance of the two previous arguments. 

The very strongest responses are most likely to argue the third, which takes the most balanced view. The third choice is also the most likely to score the highest amount of points in the areas of analysis and focus.

Possible arguments supporting the role of the United States government in supporting indigenous peoples' rights:

 - Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968: Part of President Johnson's 1968 Civil Rights Act; This led to the appointment of a Native American as the head of the BIA and was designed to protect people living on the reservations from white and tribal dictatorships. Ultimately, the Indian Civil Rights Act helped people on the reservations gain access to better health and housing services, education and poverty benefits.

 - Indian Self-Determination Act: Under President Nixon, the special legal status of Native tribes was restored.

 - United States v. Wheeler: Land was returned to the Narragansetts of Rhode Island, and $\text\textdollar$100 million was offered to the Sioux, who demanded land instead for the dishonorable nature of the purchase of the Black Hills in South Dakota.

 - Boldt Decision (United States v. Washington): Ensured that tribes had the right to 50$\%$ of the fishing along with Washington state, recognizing the two as equal partners in the maintenance of the fishing industry.

Other possible reasons for increased indigenous rights that can be considered:

 - American Indian Movement (AIM): AIM was a militant Red Power group that looked to gain power and influence in the United States by bringing attention to the treatment of Native Americans. They monitored police discrimination against natives, opened survival schools for displaced natives to learn about tribal culture and languages, and gained publicity through protest marches, litigation, and occupations.

 - National Congress of American Indians: Inspired by the work of the NAACP, the NCAI worked for the survival of Native American identity by suing the state and federal government when they went back on their promises

 - Protest movements: groups such as the National Indian Youth Council embraced the Red Power movement, rejecting assimilation. Natives staged sit-ins to protest state governments. One famous protest was the occupation of Alcatraz in response to the loss of San Francisco's Indian Center.

 - Increased media coverage of Native American protests led to more empathy from the American public. The Trail of Broken Treaties, a march from San Francisco to Washington, D.C., heavily publicized the violation of longstanding treaties between the government and the Native Americans. The occupation of Wounded Knee was inspired by the murder of an indigenous man by a white man who was charged with manslaughter. This occupation led to several deaths and more attention to the Native American plight. 

 - Books, such as Bury My Heart at Wounded Knee (1970) and House Made of Dawn (1968), raised awareness of the mistreatment of Native Americans.

Arguments against the impact of the United States government in supporting indigenous people: 

 - Termination Policy: Under President Eisenhower, the government attempted to end federal recognition of tribes and transfer the jurisdiction of tribal lands to the states. This policy encouraged assimilation and relocation.

 - The Indian Relocation Act was meant to provide jobs to indigenous people in cities and urban areas. Job training would be provided to them. However, many were faced with discrimination and had difficulty assimilating. Over a third of the people involved with this program returned to the reservations. 

 - The Indian Claims Commission, while awarding compensation for lost lands, meddled in reservation business. For example, the head of the commission, Dillon Myer, sold Pueblo land without permission

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:

\

In the years following World War II, several marginalized groups fought for equality in the United States. The indigenous community, often overlooked or pushed aside throughout United States history, sought to secure more independence and humane treatment. To some extent, government policies were created that helped or furthered the rights of indigenous people. However, indigenous activism most encouraged the United States to adopt these policies ($\checkmark\, \text{ focus}$). 

Under Presidents Truman and Eisenhower, the government enacted policies that appeared to help indigenous people but were more focused on assimilation ($\checkmark\, \text{ organization}$ $\checkmark\, \text{ focus}$). For example, the Indian Claims Commission (ICC) was responsible for paying reparations to tribes for land that was taken away from them by the government ($\checkmark\, \text{ examples}$). However, the ICC would value the land at its cost from the time it was taken and would pay cash, rather than return the land to the tribes ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). This shows that the government did not account for the cultural significance of the land and instead was more focused on paying a monetary value that was usually much less than what the land was worth ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$). While the United States government may have had good intentions, ultimately, the ICC did not help to further the rights of indigenous people. Similarly, the Relocation Act promised job training and employment to Native Americans but in urban centers rather than on reservations ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). Most of the people who took advantage of these programs began to feel isolated from their communities and their cultural background, while also being unable to assimilate into their new cities ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). While the program may have had good intentions, the government did not follow through with enough support to actually help the people who had moved from their homes ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$). Some historians, such as Vine Deloria, Jr., recognize the relocation program as disastrous and an attempt to eliminate cultural bonds ($\checkmark\, \text{ perspectives}$). However, it is important to note that many Native Americans who found their way to the cities would eventually thrive and many of them were founders of the Pan-Indian movement that helped usher in actual positive change in the late 1960s and early 1970s ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$).

The government did eventually make some meaningful changes for the indigenous people ($\checkmark\, \text{ focus}$). For example, the Supreme Court sided with Native Americans in two court decisions, The United States v. Washington and United States v. Wheeler ($\checkmark\, \text{ examples}$). The United States v. Wheeler, also known as the Boldt decision, acknowledged that the tribes of Washington should be equal partners with the state regarding the fishing industry. United States v. Wheeler ordered a return of land to the Narragansett people of Rhode Island ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). In addition, when President Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act into law, it included a portion geared explicitly toward furthering the rights of Native Americans. The Indian Civil Rights Act extended constitutional rights to people living on reservations and brought better educational, job, and healthcare opportunities to the reservations as well ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). While this was received better than the Relocation Act, some argued it was a move away from the sovereignty of the tribes. Deloria stated that the ICRA specifically was like trying to force reservations into a system designed by $\text{\textquotedblleft}$non-Indians” ($\checkmark\, \text{ perspectives}$). However, historian Robert Clinton acknowledged how progressive the act was in avoiding complete government control of the reservations. The evidence suggests that the government did truly make changes that would benefit the indigenous population, though not without some issues ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$).

However, it can be argued that many of these changes would not have taken place had it not been for Native American assertiveness and increased organization ($\checkmark\, \text{ focus}$). During the 1960s and 1970s, following the negative changes made during the Truman and Eisenhower administrations, natives began to mobilize more effectively. As noted earlier, the pan-Indian movement grew from natives who had relocated to cities for career training and met with poverty ($\checkmark\, \text{ examples}$). Groups such as the American Indian Movement and the National Congress of American Indians formed to fight for more rights and to garner more attention to their cause ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). They staged sit-ins, protests, and marches to fight perceived injustices against their community. The Occupation of Alcatraz by the Indians of All Tribes was particularly effective in garnering attention to broken land treaties between the tribes and the United States government. This event directly encouraged future land restorations, such as Blue Lake in New Mexico ($\checkmark\, \text{ examples}$ $\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$). The Trail of Broken Treaties was a cross-country march that forced government officials to meet with organization leaders to address sovereignty issues following the Indian Civil Rights Act. Several historians, notably Kent Blansett, argue that the high visibility of these protests forced the hand of the government and produced more tangible change, such as Nixon's Self-Determination Act ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$ $\checkmark\, \text{ perspectives}$).

While the United States government made some movement toward righting wrongs with the indigenous population, the most significant change was achieved as a result of the mobilization of indigenous people following failed government policies and abuses. Their activism forced the government to address its failed assimilation policies, reshaping the relationship between the tribes and the United States ($\checkmark\, \text{ conclusion}$).
