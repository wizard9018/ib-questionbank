# HI1235

“Social issues were the main cause of the emergence of social movements in the Americas.” Discuss with reference to **one** social movement in the Americas after 1945.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Social movements in the Americas after the Second World War emerged amid widespread social discontent over the war's effects on humanity and the call for change. In both the USA and Latin America, the 1950s and 1960s signalled growing concerns of distinct social groups about economic inequalities, challenges to inclusive political participation, the marginalisation of minority groups, and the traditional gender-based roles assigned to men and women ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). For instance, civil society organisations across the Americas started to raise awareness and mobilise in relation to the relegated place of women in social life despite the significance of their contributions to the development of their countries ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). This essay posits that social issues were a primary cause of the emergence of feminist movements in the Americas after 1945. However, it also argues that political factors were key to their early development, especially in the context of radical changes in political regimes in the region during the Cold War ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$ $\checkmark\, \text{organisation}$).

Social issues partly caused the emergence of feminist movements in the Americas ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$ $\checkmark\, \text{organisation}$). After the Second World War, rapid changes carried out by governments aimed at boosting economic development brought about the intensification of industrialisation in the Americas ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). These changes were significant in the gender-based organisation of social life across countries in the region, as women began working in factories and partially shifted away from their traditional domestic responsibilities ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Nonetheless, the differentiated working conditions continued reproducing inequalities between men and women ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$). This was noteworthy in the formation of influential feminist movements that called for the end of wage discrimination and gender-based exploitation. In the USA, the publication of Betty Friedan's “The Feminine Mystique” and the leadership of labour rights advocate Esther Peterson were key to advancing nationwide demands for equal pay rights in the late 1950s. Similarly, the Argentinian Front for Women's Struggle movement advanced all-encompassing demands for better conditions of women in the workplace and at home in the late 1970s ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). The marked hierarchical system in the workplace thus prompted the early developments of feminist movements as a response to women's exclusion from particular businesses and roles ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

Moreover, feminist movements also developed against the backdrop of institutional restrictions on the freedom of choice and expression of women ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$ $\checkmark\, \text{organisation}$). For example, women's organisations in the USA and Mexico began mobilising for their liberation in the late 1960s and early 1970s. One salient aspect of the call for mobilisation was linked to the significance of women's will to control their own sexual life ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Framed by demands for equality enshrined in sexual rights, the National Organization for Women in the USA and the Women in Solidarity Action in Mexico spearheaded public protests and political actions to bring about change. More specifically, these organisations sought changes in the legal framework in their countries against deeply ingrained issues such as sexual assaults at home and the lack of support in their decision to use contraceptive methods or access an abortion ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). The emergence of these organised movements, therefore, highlighted the negative impacts of structural conditions grounded in national policies that did not guarantee women's protection, thereby driving social issues and inspiring social movements ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

However, political factors were also paramount to the development of feminist movements in the Americas ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$ $\checkmark\, \text{organisation}$). Although women's participation in decision-making at the state level was limited or non-existent by the 1950s, their growing influence in the political realm through campaigning and mass mobilisations paved the way for the emergence of feminist movements ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). For instance, the active militant role of women in the M-26-7 during the Cuban Revolution during the 1950s contributed to the movement's achievements under Fidel Castro and Ernesto $\lq$Che' Guevara in 1959. As the Cuban leaders praised the significance of women in the socialist cause and placed gender equality at the centre of social policies, the creation of the state-led Federation of Cuban Women (FMC) under the leadership of former revolutionary Vilma Espín in 1960 represented a watershed moment ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). The political will of the Cuban state allowed for the institutionalisation of the FMC as an organisation reflective of feminist principles linked to sexual education, health, and the legalisation of abortion. Although the initiative stemmed from the Cuban state's attempt to further control social groups and consolidate its power, the FMC was reflective of the significance of women in the view of the regime under Castro ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In conclusion, social factors were key to the early development of feminist movements in the Americas from the 1950s onwards. The effects of the Second World War in societies across the region created conditions for increased gender inequality, which partly caused the formation of organised groups seeking better living conditions for women. This was evident in both public and private spheres as feminist movements demanded better working conditions in factories and the protection of their sexual rights. However, political factors were also significant in the emergence of feminist movements. For instance, measures such as the ones implemented by the Cuban state aimed at enhancing the role of women paved the way for the development of an institutional framework that enabled conditions for the advancement of gender equality principles reflective of feminist movements ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$ $\checkmark\, \text{organisation}$).
