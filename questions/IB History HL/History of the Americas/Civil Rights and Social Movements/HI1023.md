# HI1023

“The counterculture movement in the Americas during the 1960s and 1970s was always peaceful.” To what extent do you agree with this view?
[15 marks]

Guidance:
- Students should address the demands of the question by acknowledging the strengths of the claim that the “counterculture movement was always peaceful” and counterclaims that indicate how, when, where, or why it became violent.
- Responses should include clear examples chosen from the Americas to illustrate arguments. Examples may be taken from one or a variety of countries.

Indicative content:
- Definition and historical context of the counterculture movement in the 1960s. The counterculture movement could be broadly defined as a series of public demonstrations by different social groups, mostly youth, that opposed social inequalities, the effects of the capitalist and consumerist society on people and the environment, and the negative impacts of the Cold War on civilians.
- The counterculture movement's firm opposition to the political polarisation and destruction of social fabric caused by the Cold War.
- Accounts of the peaceful principles of the counterculture movement alongside specific demands for social change (e.g. anti-war, gender and racial equality, freedom of expression, socioeconomic equality, anti-imperialism).
- Cultural manifestations of the movements in the arts:
    - Artistic productions in California and New York.
    - Popular events such as the Monterrey Pop (1967) and Woodstock (1969) festivals.
    - The Onda literature movement in Mexico supporting the freedom of expression of the youth.
    - Critique of consumerism by the Arte Destructivo visual arts movement in Argentina.
- Violent groups in the USA:
    - The Black Liberation Army (1970s) acted as an opposition armed group against race-based violence and systemic inequalities by bombing key government infrastructure and attacking police officers.
    - The Symbionese Liberation Army (early 1970s) robbed banks and kidnapped wealthy people.
    - The Weather Underground organisation (late 1960s) mainly attacked government infrastructure to express firm opposition to the Vietnam War and US imperialism.
- Violent groups in Latin America:
    - The People's Guerrilla Group (early 1960s) reclaimed land for peasants in rural Mexico and resisted state repression through direct confrontation.
    - The Tupamaros (mid-1960s) robbed banks and foreign companies, kidnapped political and economic elite members, attacked police officials, and committed terrorist attacks in Uruguay.
    - The Montoneros (1970s) engaged in targeted attacks against the government and military officials and infrastructure in attempts to subvert US direct influence in national politics.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
The counterculture movement emerged in the 1960s against the backdrop of the Cold War's polarised world and grew critical of both international conflicts and social inequality in countries across the Americas ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). It could be argued that this movement was essentially peaceful because it was mainly comprised of youth circles favouring a peaceful,  non-violent approach to bringing about profound changes in society ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$). As reflected in artistic demonstrations, the movement proved to be effective in gaining nationwide support in different countries. However, it should be acknowledged that the counterculture movement also developed as a platform for violent demonstrations, particularly in the 1970s, in response to peaceful undertakings' limited impact ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$).

By definition, the counterculture movement that emerged in the 1960s in the USA was peaceful ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). The values put forward by youth groups were aligned with messages of peace and love alongside non-violent demonstrations ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$). For instance, arts-based mobilisations served as peaceful platforms through which different groups revealed their political militancy. The creation of the Peace Tower by the Artist Protest Committee in Los Angeles in 1966 and the joint efforts of musical artists in the Woodstock festival in 1969 showcase the arts' significance in amplifying youth voices against US military actions in the Vietnam War ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). In Latin America, a wide range of grassroots organisations had the common goal of subverting the traditional values they felt were hindering human development. For example, the Onda literature movement in Mexico advocated for freedom of expression, especially of young individuals who wanted to create alternative fashion trends inspired by indigenous costumes ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Similarly, the Arte Destructivo in Argentina in the early 1960s drew from its counterpart in London to contest the destruction of humanity and nature associated with both the nuclear arms race and the increasing materialism and consumerism in society. These manifestations demonstrate altogether that peaceful resistance through art was reflective of a growing call for cultural revolution within and across countries in the Americas and beyond ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

Moreover, groups that worked for social justice took the peaceful approach within the counterculture movement ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). In the USA, the influences of the principle of non-violence in the Civil Rights Movement in the 1950s and the Free Speech Movement in 1964 were paramount for furthering grassroots activism in educational institutions in the country ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$). With higher enrolment rates in the 1960s and 1970s, universities served as platforms for anti-establishment demonstrations of students demanding freedom of assembly and collaboration with wider causes such as racial and gender equality, environmentalism, and anti-war protests ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). In Latin America, organised networks were built to address pressing issues nationally. For example, the Dutch-led organisation Solidaridad was vital in promoting both economic development in Brazilian slums and supporting human rights defenders under the dictatorial regime in El Salvador in the 1970s ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Social justice tackled through concrete actions addressing people's problems was, therefore, another peaceful pillar of the counterculture movement ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

However, some organisations also attempted to achieve social changes through violent means ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Although claiming similar grounds to peaceful manifestations, revolutionary movements in Latin America resorted to violence to bring about structural sociopolitical changes in the Cold War context ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$). For instance, the creation of guerrilla groups was prompted by both discontent over socioeconomic inequality and violence exerted by the US-aligned central governments ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). The Mexican peasant-led People's Guerrilla Group in the early 1960s, the student-led guerrillas of Tupamaros in Uruguay, and the Montoneros in Argentina in the late 1960s carried out attacks against political and economic elites they viewed as responsible for letting foreign influence affect their nation's autonomy, overlooking policies favouring integrated development of marginal groups, and safeguarding human rights ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Similarly, some organisations in the USA further challenged a political system that was not meeting the social demands raised by the counterculture movement ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). During the 1970s, the sustained racial inequality was redressed by the Black Liberation Army and the white-led Weather Underground group through terrorist attacks such as bombings of governmental buildings and direct confrontation with law enforcement officials. Although most of these violent groups in Latin America and the US were short-lived, they symbolised an alternative and radicalised approach to peaceful manifestations that were regarded as unsuccessful in creating conditions for profound social changes ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In conclusion, the counterculture movement in the 1960s and 1970s was, in principle, peaceful. The values put forward by youth groups through the arts in the USA and Latin America were popular and gained support from different social sectors. Nonetheless, the ineffectiveness of this approach was reflected in the creation of violent groups that were determined to subvert racial inequality, challenge the continued US pro-war actions in Vietnam and broader Cold War scenario, undermine the US's entrenched foreign influence, and contest the state violence and socioeconomic inequality in Latin America ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
