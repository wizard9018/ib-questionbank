# HI0961

Evaluate the extent to which Source C represents typical attitudes of Southern white leaders in 1963.
[5 marks]

$\\$

| |  | |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
|**Source C** $\hspace{3em}$ | $\hspace{3em}$ |  Inaugural address of Governor George Wallace. Delivered at the Capitol in Montgomery, Alabama, January 14, 1963. |

[Source: Wallace, G. (1963, January 14). *Inaugural address of Governor George Wallace*, which was delivered at the Capitol in Montgomery, Alabama. Retrieved July 13, 2024 from https://digital.archives.alabama.gov/cdm/%20singleitem/collection/voices/id/2952/re.  Public domain.]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:
\
Any **two** of the following with supporting evidence and a conclusion. Both typical and atypical attitudes must be addressed in the response.

Ways the speech represents attitudes of many Southern white leaders:
- Commitment to segregation: Wallace's demand for “segregation now… segregation forever” echoes the positions of many Southern governors, legislators, and law enforcement officials.
- Use of Confederate legacy: Invoking Jefferson Davis and “the Anglo-Saxon Southland” reflects common appeals to Southern identity and the “Lost Cause” narrative.
- States' rights rhetoric: The speech frames federal civil rights legislation as “tyranny,” a view widely shared among white Southern elites who saw desegregation as federal overreach.
- Political expedience: Many white leaders maintained segregation to gain political support among white voters who opposed integration.
- Opposition to civil rights demonstrations: Like Wallace, many officials resisted civil rights campaigns with legislative, rhetorical, or violent means (e.g., Ross Barnett in Mississippi, Lester Maddox in Georgia).

Ways the speech does not fully represent all Southern white leaders:
- Extremity of language: Wallace's explicit and theatrical rhetoric went further than many white leaders who maintained segregation more subtly or pragmatically.
- Internal division: Some Southern white leaders were beginning to quietly moderate their stance by 1963 due to national pressure (e.g., Tennessee's Governor Buford Ellington cautiously cooperated with federal law).
- Private reluctance: Some leaders may have privately opposed civil rights but avoided the kind of defiant posturing Wallace embraced in public.
- Fear of federal consequences: Certain officials complied with court rulings or federal mandates not out of agreement but to avoid federal intervention (e.g., some school boards in Virginia).
- Urban vs rural divide: White political leaders in more urbanised Southern areas were sometimes more pragmatic about gradual integration due to economic pressures.
\
[5 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Wallace's declaration of “segregation now, segregation tomorrow, segregation forever” captures the deep resistance to racial integration that dominated Southern political life. His speech aligns with the views of other prominent figures such as Mississippi's Governor Ross Barnett, who similarly resisted desegregation, including standing in the way of James Meredith's enrollment at the University of Mississippi ($\checkmark$). The speech’s appeal to “freedom-loving blood” and the Confederacy also mirrors the cultural and ideological defences many leaders used to justify maintaining segregation and opposing federal mandates ($\checkmark$).

However, not all Southern leaders were as extreme in tone or as openly defiant. Some, like Arkansas Governor Orval Faubus, had begun to moderate their language under federal pressure by 1963, even if they still resisted integration ($\checkmark$). Others complied with court rulings reluctantly, without the fiery appeals to Southern heritage that Wallace embraced. Furthermore, Wallace's theatrical delivery and explicit invocation of white supremacy were not universally adopted, especially by leaders in more economically progressive areas ($\checkmark$).

In conclusion, while Wallace's speech was emblematic of mainstream white Southern political resistance to civil rights, its intensity and symbolism represent the most vocal and hardline segment rather than a complete picture of Southern leadership attitudes in 1963 ($\checkmark$).
