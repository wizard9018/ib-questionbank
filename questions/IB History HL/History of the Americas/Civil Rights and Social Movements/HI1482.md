# HI1482

Examine the impact and significance of feminist movements in one country of the Americas.
[15 marks]

General Guidance:
- The question requires students to investigate both the concrete effects and the broader, longer-term importance of feminist movements.
- The response must focus on one country of the Americas and strong responses will focus on clearly documented feminist activism in that country.
- The best responses will distinguish clearly between impact (legal, political, social, economic and cultural change) and significance (how far the movements were transformative, lasting or limited) and evaluate different perspectives on how much really changed.
- Evidence of significance may include durable legal frameworks, mass entry of women into education, work and politics, reshaped cultural norms, and international linkage.
- Limitations may include: persistent wage gaps, under-representation in positions of power, conservative backlash, and the exclusion of poorer women and women of colour.

Possible suitable examples include:
- **United States** - feminist movements and second-wave feminism, 1960s onward
    - Legal: Equal Pay Act (1963); Title VII of the Civil Rights Act (1964) banning sex discrimination in employment; Title IX (1972) requiring gender equity in federally funded education; Roe v. Wade (1973) establishing a constitutional right to abortion.
    - Political: National Organization for Women (NOW, 1966); the campaign for the Equal Rights Amendment (ERA), passed by Congress in 1972.
    - Social/cultural: expansion of reproductive choice (the contraceptive pill; legalised abortion); consciousness-raising; changing attitudes to marriage, work and domestic roles; *Ms.* magazine (1972); figures such as Betty Friedan and Gloria Steinem.
    - Significance: Transformative long-term shift in women's legal status, education and workforce participation.
    - Limitations: The Equal Rights Amendment failed ratification by the 1982 deadline (falling short of 38 states), exposing the limits of the movement and the strength of conservative backlash (such as Phyllis Schlafly's STOP ERA campaign). Mainstream (liberal, largely white, middle-class) feminism neglected working-class women and women of colour, prompting Black feminist and intersectional responses.
- **Mexico** - post-war suffrage and “Second Wave” from 1970 on
    - Women gained the municipal vote (1947) and full federal suffrage (1953), first voting in national elections in 1955.
    - Second-wave groups from the early 1970s (e.g. Mujeres en Acción Solidaria) campaigned on reproductive rights, violence against women and legal equality; a 1974 constitutional reform enshrined legal equality between men and women.
    - The 1975 UN World Conference on Women in Mexico City increased the visibility of feminist demands and strengthened links between Mexican, Latin American and international women's movements.
    - **Significance:** Suffrage and legal reform integrated women into formal politics. Hosting the 1975 conference gave Mexican and Latin American feminism international visibility and linked national activism to a global agenda.
    - **Limitations:** Gains were constrained by PRI dominance, machismo and the Catholic Church; reproductive rights advanced slowly (Mexico City decriminalised abortion only in 2007), showing significant but incomplete change.
- **Argentina** - post-war suffrage and increased presence in politics
    - Female suffrage law (Law 13,010, 1947), championed by Eva Perón; women first voted in the 1951 national elections.
    - The Female Peronist Party (Partido Peronista Femenino, founded 1949) mobilised women politically on an unprecedented scale.
    - **Significance:** Rapidly incorporated women into national political life and tied women's rights to Peronism, giving them mass political weight. Later gendered activism shows a longer trajectory via the Madres de Plaza de Mayo under the dictatorship.
    - **Limitations:** Gains were bound to a populist regime, so were partly instrumentalised and vulnerable to political instability; autonomous feminist organisation was limited.
\
[15 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Since the mid-twentieth century, feminist movements in the United States have reshaped the legal, political and cultural place of women, yet the extent of that transformation remains contested. ($\checkmark$ focus) Emerging from the frustrations of educated, middle-class women in the post-war period and drawing organisational lessons from the civil rights movement, the “second wave” of American feminism from the 1960s onwards secured a series of concrete legal victories while also provoking a powerful conservative backlash. ($\checkmark$ knowledge) The movement made substantial gains in legal protections, education and employment, even though its gains were uneven and, in the case of the Equal Rights Amendment, incomplete. ($\checkmark$ organization)

The most measurable impact of American feminism was legislative and legal. ($\checkmark$ focus) The Equal Pay Act of 1963 made it illegal to pay men and women different wages for equal work, and Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibited employment discrimination on the basis of sex, giving women a legal weapon against workplace inequality. ($\checkmark$ examples) In 1972, Title IX barred sex discrimination in any federally funded education programme, opening universities, professional schools and athletics to women on a far larger scale. ($\checkmark$ knowledge) The following year, the Supreme Court's decision in Roe v. Wade (1973) recognised a constitutional right to abortion, granting women unprecedented control over reproduction. ($\checkmark$ examples)Taken together, these measures dismantled much of the formal legal architecture of discrimination, and their impact was direct and durable. ($\checkmark$ analysis)

These legal changes also translated into profound educational and economic impact. ($\checkmark$ focus) In the decades after Title IX, women entered higher education, law, medicine and business in rapidly rising numbers, eroding the assumption that the professions were male preserves. ($\checkmark$ knowledge) Access to reliable contraception and, after 1973, to legal abortion allowed many women to delay or plan childbearing, making sustained careers more achievable and reshaping patterns of work and family life. ($\checkmark$ analysis) The result was a lasting increase in female workforce participation and a slow narrowing of the gap between men's and women's public roles. ($\checkmark$ perspectives)

Beyond legislation, feminist movements transformed political organisation and cultural attitudes. ($\checkmark$ focus) The founding of the National Organization for Women (NOW) in 1966, led by figures such as author and activist Betty Friedan created a durable lobbying vehicle for women's rights. ($\checkmark$ examples) Consciousness-raising groups, the launch of *Ms.* magazine in 1972 and the public advocacy of activists such as Gloria Steinem carried feminist ideas well beyond activist circles. ($\checkmark$ knowledge) The effect was to change everyday expectations about marriage, motherhood, work and sexuality, so that assumptions taken for granted in 1950 had become widely questioned by 1980. ($\checkmark$ analysis) In this sense the movement's cultural impact arguably outlasted any single statute, because it altered how a generation of women understood their own possibilities. ($\checkmark$ perspectives)

The significance of these changes, however, must be weighed against clear limitations. ($\checkmark$ focus) The campaign for the Equal Rights Amendment, passed by Congress in 1972, ultimately failed, falling short of ratification by the necessary thirty-eight states before the 1982 deadline, defeated in part by Phyllis Schlafly's STOP ERA campaign, which mobilised conservative women who feared feminism threatened their idea of the traditional family. ($\checkmark$ examples) This defeat revealed both the strength of the opposition and the limits of feminist consensus. ($\checkmark$ analysis) Critics also argued that mainstream feminism spoke primarily for white, middle-class women and neglected working-class women and women of colour, a critique that gave rise to Black feminist and, later, intersectional analyses. ($\checkmark$ perspectives) From this angle the movement's impact was real but uneven, benefiting some women far more than others and leaving structural inequalities like the persistent wage gap only partly addressed. ($\checkmark$ analysis)

On balance, the feminist movements of the United States had a transformative impact on the legal status, education and employment of women, and their deeper significance lies in the permanent shift they produced in cultural expectations about gender. ($\checkmark$ focus) The failure of the ERA and the exclusion of many women from the movement's early gains show that this transformation was neither complete nor evenly shared. ($\checkmark$ perspectives) Yet even allowing for these limits, American feminism fundamentally redrew the boundaries of women's public and private lives, and its significance is best understood not as a single legislative moment but as a lasting reorientation of society that continues to shape debates over gender to the present day. ($\checkmark$ conclusion)
