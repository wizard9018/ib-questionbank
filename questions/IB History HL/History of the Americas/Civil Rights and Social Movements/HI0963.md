# HI0963

Identify **two** historical references in Source C and explain their significance to Wallace's message.
[6 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:
\
Any **two** of the following with a conclusion. Each point requires an explanation and discussion of significance.

Historical reference 1: Jefferson Davis
- President of the Confederate States of America during the Civil War (1861-1865).
- Why Wallace references him:
    - To align himself with the Confederacy's legacy of Southern resistance to federal authority.
    - To evoke Southern pride and reinforce the image of segregation as a heritage issue, not a moral failing.
    - To legitimise defiance of federal civil rights laws by invoking a well-known figure who also rejected federal control.
- Significance:
    - Sets a tone of rebellion and continuity; Wallace presents himself as a modern heir to Davis's cause.
    - Suggests that resisting civil rights is a defence of tradition rather than extremism.

Historical Reference 2: “Cradle of the Confederacy”
- What it refers to: Montgomery, Alabama, where the Confederacy was founded and its first capital in 1861.
- Why Wallace uses it:
    - To anchor his speech in a location symbolic of Southern defiance and white supremacy.
    - To invoke nostalgia for a romanticised version of the Old South.
- Significance:
    - Embeds his political position in a place deeply associated with slaveholding and segregationist values.
    - Strengthens the idea that Alabama, and the South in general, has a special mission to resist federal changes.

Historical Reference 3: “The Great Anglo-Saxon Southland”
- What it refers to: A racialized phrase used by Wallace to describe the cultural identity of the South.
- Why Wallace uses it:
    - To appeal to a white, Protestant conception of Southern heritage and cultural superiority.
    - Reinforces the idea that segregation is not just political, but deeply rooted in racial and cultural identity.
- Significance:
    - Frames the civil rights movement as a threat to the racial and cultural order of the South.
    - Highlights how white identity was embedded in political resistance to integration.

Historical Reference 4: “Tyranny that clanks its chains upon the South”
- What it refers to: An allusion to Revolutionary War-era language about British oppression.
- Why Wallace uses it:
    - To liken the federal government's push for desegregation to a foreign tyranny infringing on Southern “freedom.”
    - Portrays the South as a victim of oppressive outside control, echoing the Confederate and Revolutionary tradition of resistance.
- Significance:
    - Helps cast federal civil rights enforcement as morally illegitimate and reminiscent of colonial oppression.
    - Adds emotional and historical weight to the argument against federal intervention, appealing to patriotic Southern sentiment.
\
[6 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
One reference is to Jefferson Davis, the President of the Confederacy ($\checkmark$). Wallace states that he is standing “where once Jefferson Davis stood,” signalling to his audience that he sees himself as the successor to Davis's legacy of resisting federal authority ($\checkmark$).  By invoking Davis, Wallace attempts to legitimise his opposition to civil rights as part of a proud Southern tradition rather than as political extremism ($\checkmark$).

A second reference is to Montgomery, Alabama, described as the “Cradle of the Confederacy”($\checkmark$).  This phrase evokes the founding of the Confederacy and reinforces the idea that Alabama has a special historical role in defending states' rights and white supremacy ($\checkmark$).  By anchoring his speech in this symbolic location, Wallace aligns the modern civil rights struggle with the Civil War and frames federal desegregation efforts as a continuation of historical tyranny ($\checkmark$).
