# HI1007

Evaluate the contribution of the US Supreme Court in advancing the civil rights of African Americans between 1945 and 1980.

This question requires candidates to make an appraisal of the contribution of the Supreme Court in advancing the civil rights of African Americans up to 1980. They should demonstrate a clear understanding of the impact

of specific Supreme Court rulings. Candidates could argue some rulings advanced the movement, while others hindered it. 

For positive contributions to civil rights, candidates may refer to:

 - *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948): Stated that restrictive agreements for purchasing property were unconstitutional.

 - *Brown v. Topeka* Board of Education (1954): Overturned Plessy v Ferguson and can be seen as the most important contribution in pushing the Civil Rights Movement forward.

 - *Browder v. Gayle* (1956): Ruled that bus segregation was unconstitutional and ended the Montgomery Bus Boycott.

 - *Cooper v. Aaron* (1958): Reaffirmed Brown and declared that states could not resist desegregation orders. A response to $\text{\textquotedblleft}$massive resistance” campaigns.

 - *Boynton v. Virginia* (1960): Declared segregation in interstate bus terminals was unconstitutional, helping spur the Freedom Rides (1961).

 - *Griffin v. County School Board of Prince Edward County* (1964): Held that Virginia's closing of schools to resist desegregation was unconstitutional, helping maintain public education opportunities.

 - *The Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States* (1964): Established the constitutionality of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and ruled that Congress could prohibit racial discrimination in public accommodations via the Commerce Clause.

 - *South Carolina v. Katzenbach (1966)*: Upheld the Voting Rights Act of 1965 as a valid use of Congress's Fifteenth Amendment powers, allowing federal preclearance and examiners to stop states from disenfranchising Black voters. 

 - *Loving v. Virginia (1967)*: Struck down state bans on interracial marriage.

 - *Green v. New Kent County* (1968): Struck down ineffective $\text{\textquotedblleft}$freedom of choice” plans and required schools to dismantle segregation $\text{\textquotedblleft}$root and branch.”

 - *Alexander v. Holmes County Board of Education* (1969): Ended the $\text{\textquotedblleft}$all deliberate speed” standard and ordered the immediate desegregation of dual school systems.

 - *Swann v. Charlotte-Becklenburg Board of Education* (1971): Upheld busing and other $\text{\textquotedblleft}$remedies” as a method to integrate schools, helping legitimize more aggressive desegregation measures.

For negative contributions to civil rights, candidates may refer to:

 - *Brown v. Board of Education II* (1955): Ordered desegregation at $\text{\textquotedblleft}$all deliberate speed.” Vague language allowed states to delay compliance for years.

 - *Milliken v Bradley* (1974): Prohibited cross-district busing unless it was proven that districts had deliberately segregated, limiting desegregation to within single districts and advancing school segregation in cities where cities and suburbs were racially divided.

 - *California v Bakke* (1978) prohibited affirmative action programs if these led to reverse discrimination. The Bakke decision could be seen as slowing down the Civil Rights Movement. 

Candidates' opinions or conclusions will be presented clearly and supported by appropriate evidence.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:

\

US Supreme Court rulings between 1945 and 1980 are often associated with advancing the civil rights of African Americans ($\checkmark\, \text{ focus}$). While some historians regard these rulings as decisive turning points, others adopt a more measured view, emphasizing the decisive role of congressional legislation as the real driver of civil rights progress. These historians tend to argue that the Supreme Court operates within a broader framework of constitutional development and rarely initiates social change independently ($\checkmark\, \text{ perspectives)}$. This essay evaluates the Court's contribution to advancing African American civil rights through two case studies focusing on education and voting rights ($\checkmark\, \text{ organization}$). It argues that the Court rarely achieved significant civil rights advances in isolation. Only when its rulings were aligned with federal legislation did they become transformative ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$). 

The Supreme Court was able to make significant contributions in advancing civil rights for African Americans ($\checkmark\, \text{ focus}$) when its decisions were aligned with a broad framework of congressional and judicial action ($\checkmark\, \text{ organization}$). The 1954 Brown v Topeka's Board of Education ($\checkmark\, \text{ examples}$) ruling became transformative once Congress, the Executive, and courts aligned behind its decision. Although the original ruling was won unanimously 9-0 and did much to weaken the $\text{\textquotedblleft}$Separate but Equal" principle outlined in Plessy v Ferguson (1896) ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$), it was bitterly resisted for almost a decade. But Brown I failed to give precise instructions on how and when to desegregate schools. Brown II, in 1955, ordered federal district courts to oversee desegregation $\text{\textquotedblleft}$with all deliberate speed" ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$), but it set no deadlines and provided no enforcement mechanism. Brown II essentially left implementation to local school authorities, many of whom were hostile to integration. This lack of direction empowered resistance movements such as the Southern Manifesto (1956), and the events at Little Rock (1957) to take hold, slowing progress ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$) to the extent that by 1963, an estimated 98% of Black students in parts of the Deep South were still attending segregated schools ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$).

Once Congress and federal agencies aligned behind Brown, integration accelerated sharply and produced significant gains ($\checkmark\, \text{ organization}$). The Civil Rights Act (1964) granted Title IV, allowing the US Attorney General to file lawsuits to enforce school desegregation. Title IV also offered federal funding to help schools meet desegregation requirements. Title VI provided enforcement measures by withdrawing federal funding from schools that resisted desegregation. Moreover, the Department of Health, Education and Welfare could withhold funding from non-compliant districts ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). This provided the necessary financial incentive for districts to follow the Brown ruling. 

Moreover ($\checkmark\, \text{ organization}$), when momentum picked up for Brown, the Supreme Court became more confident enforcing remedies to overturn $\text{\textquotedblleft}$freedom of choice" plans in Green v New Kent County (1968), to abolish $\text{\textquotedblleft}$all deliberate speed" delaying tactics in Alexander v Holmes (1969), and to create busing, school zoning and racial ratios in Swann (1971). By 1970, 33% of Black students in the South were attending majority white southern schools, a figure that approached 43$\%$ in the 1980s ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). This demonstrates that the Supreme Court's breakthrough in advancing African American civil rights achieved real force only when Congress and federal agencies provided the political will and enforcement mechanisms to implement it. The 1954 landmark ruling stood as a principle without practical effect for over a decade. It lay dormant, waiting for legislative and administrative action to give it life ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$).

In addition ($\checkmark\, \text{ organization)}$, the Supreme Court's 1966 ruling against Black voting suppression in South Carolina underscores that only when paired with existing statutory framework could judicial decisions overturn disenfranchisement. The Voting Rights Act (1965) banned all discriminatory voting practices and provided preclearance requirements for changing voting procedures, including literacy tests to restrict voting. Even without Supreme Court involvement, the Act accelerated Black voter registration in Mississippi from 6.7$\%$ in 1964 to 59.8$\%$ in 1967 ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). However, when states challenged the Act, arguing that it infringed on Tenth Amendment rights, the Supreme Court could rule with the backing of legislation against the states. In South Carolina v Katzenbach (1966) ($\checkmark\, \text{ examples}$), the Court upheld the Voting Rights Act under the 15th Amendment, which prevented racial discrimination in voting ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). The ruling immediately validated the Act's enforcement mechanisms and set a precedent for removing legal obstacles that states could use to delay compliance. Katzenbach demonstrates that the Supreme Court was effective in advancing civil rights when it backed existing legislation. Without the Act's preclearance and enforcement powers, the ruling would have been symbolic rather than transformative ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$). 

However ($\checkmark\, \text{ organization}$), while the power of Congress and the Supreme Court did advance African American Civil Rights ($\checkmark\, \text{ focus}$), states adapted to the new legislation by utilizing gerrymandering and annexation that slowed the Black enfranchisement. Moreover, voting legislation required a renewal in 1970, which abolished the remaining literacy tests.  By the late 1970s, critics argued that judicial resources were too thinly stretched to curb states' manipulation of the system to stall electoral reform.

In conclusion ($\checkmark\, \text{ conclusion}$), the  Supreme Court contributed greatly to advancing African American Civil Rights, but its rulings were much more effective when aligned with legislation enacted by Congress. This alignment produced tangible results in education and voting rights ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$). However, progress was maximized when Court rulings followed federal legislation. Advancement was delayed by a decade when the Brown ruling preceded the Civil Rights Act, yet brought immediate results when the Katzenbach ruling followed the Voting Rights Act. This suggests that the Court could open the door to equality, but only when Congress chose to walk through it did civil rights advance in a meaningful and lasting way ($\checkmark\, \text{ perspectives}$).
