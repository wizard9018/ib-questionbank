$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:
\
For comparison, any of the following similarities:
- Themes of freedom and American identity: Both reference freedom, heritage, and what it means to be American, but they define these ideals in very different ways.
- Strong emotional appeals: Both use rhetoric and emotive language to persuade their audiences, Wallace appeals to Southern pride, Kennedy to moral responsibility and national unity.

For contrast, any of the following differences:
- View of Segregation
    - Wallace defends it as a permanent, positive good (“segregation now, tomorrow, forever”).
    - Kennedy condemns segregation as morally wrong and contrary to American values, calling for legislation to end it.
- Role of Government
    - Wallace positions the federal government as a “tyranny” imposing its will on the South.
     -Kennedy presents the federal government as a protector of rights, upholding the Constitution for all citizens.
- Tone and Vision of National Unity
    - Wallace invokes division, portraying the South as culturally and politically distinct.
    - Kennedy seeks unity, arguing that all Americans, regardless of race, should enjoy the same rights and opportunities.
- Intended Audience and Purpose
    - Wallace addresses a Southern, white segregationist base to solidify political power.
    - Kennedy speaks to the entire nation, urging moral progress and legislative reform.
\
[5 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
A point of similarity is that both leaders refer to core American ideals, such as freedom and heritage, to support their arguments ($\checkmark\, \text{similarity}$). Wallace invokes the “Cradle of the Confederacy” and the “Anglo-Saxon Southland” to stir up Southern pride, while Kennedy appeals to the Declaration of Independence and American moral values, stating that the nation was founded on the principle that “all men are created equal” ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$). Additionally, both use emotionally charged language to rally their audiences ($\checkmark\, \text{similarity}$). Wallace's dramatic phrasing, “draw the line in the dust”, mirrors the passionate tone of Kennedy's call for equal rights and national conscience ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$).

However, the two speeches fundamentally differ in their messages ($\checkmark\, \text{difference}$). Wallace passionately defends segregation, calling for its continuation “forever” as a symbol of Southern resistance to federal authority ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$). In contrast, Kennedy opposes segregation, declaring it unjust and emphasising the need for civil rights legislation to ensure equality. Furthermore, Wallace depicts the federal government as an oppressive force threatening Southern traditions. In contrast, Kennedy argues that it is the duty of the federal government to enforce constitutional rights for all citizens ($\checkmark\, \text{difference}$).
