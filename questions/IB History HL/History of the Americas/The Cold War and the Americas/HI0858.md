# HI0858

With reference to its origin, purpose and content, analyse the value and limitations of Source B for a historian studying the aims and priorities of U.S. Cold War strategy during the Eisenhower administration.
[4 marks]

$\\$

| |  | |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
|**Source B** $\hspace{3em}$ | $\hspace{3em}$ |  Excerpts from NSC 162/2 on 30 October 1953. This policy is the basis for what is known as Eisenhower's $\lq$New Look' Policy. |

[Source: National Security Council (October 30, 1953) *NSC 162/2 Note by the Executive Secretary to the National Security Council on Basic National Security Policy.* Retrieved July 16, 2025 from https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v02p1/d101. Public domain.]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:
\
A combination of the below that meets the following guidelines:
- Both values and limitations must be addressed. If only one is addressed, award a maximum of **two** marks.
- Origin, purpose and content must all be referenced at some point in the response (either as a value, limitation or both) to receive the full **four** marks.

Values
- Created by the U.S. National Security Council and approved by President Eisenhower in 1953—highly authoritative. Reflects official U.S. government thinking at a pivotal moment of Cold War policy development. Its timing offers insight into the early Cold War context and the transition from Truman to Eisenhower.
- Designed to guide national security decisions—shows formal articulation of the New Look strategy. Reveals core aims such as reliance on nuclear weapons, deterrence, strategic bases, and cost control.
- Clearly outlines the central tenets of the New Look strategy, including massive retaliation and economic sustainability. Useful for identifying key U.S. priorities during early Cold War tensions.

Limitations
- Reflects only elite, internal U.S. government perspectives—excludes dissent, public opinion, and international responses. May downplay risks or controversial elements for the sake of bureaucratic clarity.
- Created at the start of the policy, so it doesn't reveal how strategies were implemented or whether they succeeded.
- As a prescriptive planning document, it does not evaluate actual outcomes or acknowledge limitations in execution. May have been written to justify decisions rather than explore risks or failures.
- Focused on broad strategy—omits regional details (e.g., Latin America) or concrete case studies. Does not address responses from allies or adversaries or unintended consequences.

[4 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Source B is valuable for a historian studying U.S. Cold War strategy because it was produced by the National Security Council and approved by President Eisenhower in 1953, offering direct insight into the thinking of top U.S. officials during the early Cold War ($\checkmark\, \text{VO}$). Its purpose was to coordinate national security policy, making it useful for understanding the intended direction of the “New Look” strategy, especially its focus on nuclear deterrence, alliance-building, and economic sustainability ($\checkmark\, \text{VP}$). The content further supports this by clearly outlining the central pillars of the strategy, such as reliance on atomic weapons, strategic air power, and overseas bases, which were all defining features of Eisenhower's approach ($\checkmark\, \text{VC}$).

However, the source also has limitations. Since it was produced at the beginning of the New Look, it does not reflect how the strategy was implemented or whether it was successful, limiting its usefulness for evaluating actual outcomes ($\checkmark\, \text{LO}$). Its purpose as a planning document means it was not intended to analyze failures or dissenting views, which makes it one-sided and potentially idealized ($\checkmark\, \text{LP}$). The content, while clear on general strategic aims, lacks detail about how these policies played out in specific regions, such as Latin America, and provides no perspective on how allies or opponents reacted ($\checkmark\, \text{LC}$).
