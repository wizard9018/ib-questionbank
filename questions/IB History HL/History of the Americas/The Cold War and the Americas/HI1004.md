Most responses are likely to argue that the Cold War significantly impacted society and culture in the Americas, while some may also acknowledge areas of life that were less impacted. The strongest responses will provide examples of impacts in a variety of countries and/or regions, possibly including the US, Canada, and Latin America.

Cold War impact in the United States:

 - McCarthyism: Senator Joseph McCarthy led trials in an attempt to root out communism in the United States. Similar to the HUAC, these trials focused on influential people or groups. These included Hollywood actors, federal employees, the military, college professors, and labor unions, though accusations were made against many people without any evidence. This led to a culture of fear in the United States, where anyone could be accused and investigated.

 - Red Scare: The end of World War II and the beginning of tensions with the Soviet Union brought about a second Red Scare, or fear of the spread of communism. This fear was fueled by the actions of the HUAC and President Truman's foreign policies, as well as the looming threat of nuclear war. The threat of nuclear war, in particular, permeated the lives of all Americans as children practiced hiding under their desks in school in preparation for a nuclear attack. Many movies, books, and cartoons also included evidence of anti-communist rhetoric.

 - House Un-American Activities Committee: While the HUAC existed before World War II, after the war, it focused almost exclusively on rooting out communism in America. The HUAC inspired the Hollywood Blacklist, a refusal to hire people who may have had ties to subversive people and ideas. Hearings were highly publicized and instilled fear in Americans while encouraging conformity. People who were questioned often had their careers ruined.

 - Civil Liberties: Fear of communism and the Cold War led to restrictions on civil liberties. Because Senator McCarthy's and the HUAC's hearings were so detrimental to people, they were unlikely to question the methods being used to root out communism. Since people could accuse just about anybody of being a communist or knowing a communist, people kept quiet to stay out of it.

Areas of life with less of an impact

 - Consumer Culture: The United States prospered following World War II, leading to a consumer culture that had not previously existed. Some historians believe that this had a greater impact on American life. Some specific examples that can be connected to this argument are increased amounts of consumer goods, suburbanization, and increased forms of escapist entertainment. While music would become more political later in the Cold War, early rock $\text{\textquotedblleft}$n" roll was more influenced by emerging teen culture.

 - Civil Rights: The Civil Rights movement in the United States gained momentum during this time and was not influenced by the Cold War in many ways. In some ways, the Cold War may have helped move along the Civil Rights movement. For example, the army was desegregated before the Korean War. Also, the United States wanted to present itself as a united front without much internal strife. However, the Cold War also encouraged assimilation in the United States, something that worked against civil rights as it eliminated the cultures of groups such as African Americans and Indigenous Americans. Regardless, most people fighting for Civil Rights were focused on domestic life in the United States and did not focus as much on foreign affairs.

The Cold War Impact in Canada:

 - The Red Scare did spread to Canada, particularly after the Gouzenko Affair. This event proved that the Soviet Union had established an extensive spy network in Canada. This led to several arrests and widespread fear of communism.

 - Similar to the United States, Canada limited civil liberties in an effort to stop any spread of communism.

 - Canada grew closer to the United States during this time because of their similar views on stopping communism.

The Cold War in Latin America:

 - Anti-Communism and Social Polarization: impacts of containment leading to accusations and suspicion of countries pushing for progressive reforms (*e.g. Arévalo in Guatemala*). Elites pushed for anti-communism (pro-capitalism), while many workers supported socialist reforms.

 - Cultural and Intellectual Changes: US influence toward  democracy/consumerism through educational exchange and cultural diplomacy. Surge of leftist Latin American artists critical of US dominance and imperialism in Latin America.

 - Militarization and Social Identity: elevation of military elites and leaders to defend against the spread of Communism. Student movements tended to embrace reformism and anti-imperialism.

 - Religion and Conservatism: The Catholic Church became a powerful anti-communist tool in the highly religious region, reinforcing conservatism. The Church's stance later became a seed for social division in Latin America.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:

\

The conflict between the United States and the Soviet Union changed many facets of society and culture in the Americas, particularly in the United States and Canada. On the home front, American and Canadian citizens saw a second Red Scare, brought on by the fear of the spread of communism. While some aspects of life would not be affected by the Cold War, the fear inspired by Senator Joseph McCarthy and the House Un-American Activities Committee in the United States, and the Gouzenko Affair in Canada, would lead to a significant change in culture and society ($\checkmark\, \text{ focus}$).

The relationship between the Soviet Union and the United States was never firm, but deteriorated substantially at the end of World War II. Part of that deterioration came with the news that the United States had developed a nuclear weapon without the knowledge of its allies. Following the war, the Soviet Union began developing its own weapon, leading to an arms race for several decades ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). The knowledge that the Soviet Union was actively trying to develop a weapon of mass destruction led to a culture of fear in the United States ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$). This fear permeated many areas of life. Much like fire drills, children were expected to practice what to do in the event of a nuclear strike ($\checkmark\, \text{ examples}$). Many movies, books, and cartoons included mentions of the Cold War and played on the fears of the American public. However, people continued to live their lives. The United States experienced a surge in consumerism following the war. People did not stop spending money or buying homes and cars because of the culture of fear, suggesting that not every aspect of life changed. Also, many movies made during this time were westerns and musicals and showed little connection to the Cold War. Historian Alan Brinkley suggests that the Cold War impacted politics substantially, but American values guided society during this time. This could be contradicted by the idea that consumerism, music, and movies of the time might be seen as escapism, where people used them to distract from the fears of the Cold War ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$ $\checkmark\, \text{ perspectives}$).

The most significant changes to everyday life in the US happened due to the actions of the House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC) and Senator Joseph McCarthy's trials ($\checkmark\, \text{ focus}$). While HUAC existed before World War II, during the Cold War, it shifted to rooting out communism in the United States. The wave of patriotism after World War II and the new Red Scare led to more feverish anti-communist activities within HUAC. This group targeted Americans whom they believed held influence. For example, several Hollywood directors and Broadway actors were investigated for subversive behavior ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$ $\checkmark\, \text{ examples}$). Senator McCarthy built on the work of the HUAC. He accused State Department workers of being communist, inspiring Congress to investigate these accusations. Most of the accusations were unfounded, but could still disrupt people’s lives. Many lost their jobs or credibility. While the actions of the HUAC and Senator McCarthy were harsh, the American people were so afraid of communism that they let it happen. Most Americans in the 1950s thought that the United States had to do more to protect the people from communism, especially after the Rosenberg case and the expansion of the Soviet nuclear program ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$ $\checkmark\, \text{ examples}$). However, civil liberties were at risk during this time, and the freedom of speech and the right to due process were challenged directly by McCarthy and HUAC. It may be argued that these cases are extreme and that most people were not affected by the show trials; however, many Americans self-censored their ideas and their work to avoid any accusations of communist sympathy ($\checkmark\, \text{ organization}$). Federal employees were forced to sign loyalty oaths, and many people lost their jobs because of past political activity or association with people who may have been sympathetic to communism. Very few of these people were tied to actual acts of espionage ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$ $\checkmark\, \text{ examples}$). Revisionist historian Ellen Schrecker believed that the actions taken by the government during this time significantly undermined democracy by silencing any unpopular opinions. However, Orthodox historian Richard Powers thought these steps were necessary in the face of threats and Soviet espionage ($\checkmark\, \text{ perspectives}$). Regardless of the justification, it is clear that civil liberties were restricted during this time ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$).

Canada's Red Scare began when a Russian cipher clerk defected and revealed a Soviet spy ring in Ottawa. Known as the Gouzenko Affair, this led to arrests, fear, and anti-communist fervor in Canada. Civil liberties were challenged similarly to those in the United States. Many people were imprisoned without a trial: a significant breach of the right of habeas corpus. College professors were silenced, and members of the Communist Party of Canada were monitored and, in some cases, arrested ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$ $\checkmark\, \text{ examples}$). Because of the similar fears of the two countries, some Canadian historians, like Harold Innis, believed that the United States media attempted to dominate Canadian culture ($\checkmark\, \text{ perspectives}$). However, it can be argued that the United States and Canada had always maintained some similar cultural aspects and that Canada was committed to preserving national identity through public broadcasting and arts as a defense against the Soviet Union, leftists, and becoming too Americanized ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$).

The Cold War profoundly impacted many aspects of life and culture in the Americas. While the Cold War may not have obviously changed some forms of entertainment and consumer culture, the anxieties of the Cold War permeated every aspect of life. Ultimately, the Cold War’s domestic impact lay less in transforming every aspect of culture than in embedding a sense of vigilance and conformity into everyday life that allowed for extreme changes in the government and the workplace ($\checkmark\, \text{ conclusion}$).
