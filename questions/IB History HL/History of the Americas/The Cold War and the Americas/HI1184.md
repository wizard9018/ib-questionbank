$\textbf{\underline{From the Sources}}$:

Source A: Rusk, 1964 (Official justifications during the War)
- Rusk presents the U.S. as acting defensively, protecting “peaceful nations” from “pressures from the north.”
- Reflects the moral and ideological framing of U.S. policy: preserving freedom and order.
- Immediate justification for the Gulf of Tonkin strikes as deterrence against communist aggression.
- Emphasis on credibility and avoiding the perception of the U.S. as a “paper tiger.”
- Broader continuation of Cold War orthodoxy: containment, deterrence, and credibility maintenance.

Source B: Kerry, 1971 (Critical reassessment)
- Kerry's testimony marks a turning point in public discourse, challenging the justification of intervention.
- Calls war “the biggest nothing in history”, highlighting futility and moral decline (“destroying villages to save them”).
- Represents the post-1968 anti-war sentiment and loss of trust in U.S. foreign policy.
- Rejects the Cold War logic that Southeast Asia mattered strategically to U.S. security.
- Reflects shift from Cold War idealism to realism and the demand for policy restraint.

$\textbf{\underline{Own knowledge}}$:

Pre-Vietnam Cold War Policy context
- Post-WWII U.S. policy rooted in containment (Truman Doctrine, 1947).
Successive administrations viewed conflicts as part of a global struggle against communism.
The Domino Theory shaped belief that loss of Vietnam would endanger all of Southeast Asia.
U.S. credibility became a key Cold War tool after setbacks in China (1949) and Korea (1950-53).

Impact during the war: Policy continuity and escalation
- Under Kennedy and Johnson, Vietnam became the test case for containment in Asia.
- Massive escalation (1964-68): U.S. troop deployment peaked at over 500,000.
- Demonstrated shift toward militarised containment and the use of limited war strategy.
- Reinforced belief in U.S. global responsibility to resist communism (“credibility gap” if lost).
- The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964) effectively handed the president open-ended war powers.

Erosion of credibility and public confidence
- As the war dragged on, domestic opposition undermined political consensus on Cold War aims.
- Growing media coverage (e.g., Tet Offensive, My Lai Massacre) eroded moral legitimacy.
- The concept of the “credibility gap” emerged between government statements and battlefield realities.
- Disillusionment led to questioning the moral and strategic basis of U.S. global leadership.

Policy consequences and shifts post-Vietnam
- “Vietnamization” (1969-73): Nixon's policy to withdraw U.S. troops while strengthening South Vietnamese forces.
- Nixon Doctrine (1969): Reinterpreted containment. Allies expected to defend themselves with U.S. aid, not troops.
- Détente: A pragmatic shift toward negotiation (SALT I, improved relations with China) rather than direct military confrontation.
- Emergence of the “Vietnam Syndrome”, a public aversion to intervention abroad without clear objectives or exit strategy.
- Congressional limits on executive power:
    -  War Powers Act (1973) restricted presidents' ability to commit U.S. forces without congressional approval.
- Broader decline in moral authority and global prestige of the United States.

Long-Term Implications for U.S. Cold War Policy
- U.S. foreign policy became more cautious and pragmatic with the emphasis on stability, not ideology.
- Shift from military containment to diplomacy and proxy support (e.g., in Latin America, Africa, Afghanistan).
- Questioning of the domino theory and limits of U.S. power.
- Set the stage for Reagan's later reassertion of U.S. global confidence in the 1980s, but within awareness of Vietnam's lessons.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
The Vietnam War initially reinforced U.S. Cold War policy by deepening the commitment to containment and the need to maintain global credibility ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). From the beginning, American leaders viewed the conflict as a test of the containment doctrine and the United States' position as the leader of the free world. As Source A highlights, Secretary of State Dean Rusk framed U.S. involvement in Southeast Asia as part of a wider mission to build “a decent world community” and to ensure that nations “leave their neighbors alone” ($\checkmark\, \text{sources}$). This echoed the moral tone of U.S. foreign policy since 1947, shaped by the Truman Doctrine, and the belief that communist expansion had to be opposed wherever it appeared ($\checkmark\, \text{O-K}$). The Domino Theory added urgency to these concerns, fueling fears that a communist victory in Vietnam could trigger regional collapse across Asia. After the alleged Gulf of Tonkin attacks in 1964, Rusk defended immediate retaliation, reflecting the broader goal of safeguarding American credibility. Congress soon passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, granting President Johnson sweeping authority to escalate U.S. involvement. By 1968, more than 500,000 American troops were in Vietnam ($\checkmark\, \text{O-K}$). The U.S. strategy of militarised containment was meant to deter communism through strength. However, it also revealed contradictions: in fighting for “freedom,” the U.S. found itself backing authoritarian regimes and engaging in a brutal war that alienated allies and divided its own population. Although the intention was to bolster global deterrence, the war increasingly exposed the fragile foundations of America's moral authority and raised doubts about the long-term viability of its interventionist strategy ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$).

Over time, the war's failures and controversies transformed U.S. Cold War policy from idealistic interventionism to a more cautious, pragmatic approach ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). By 1971, as Kerry declared in Source B, many returning veterans saw the war as “the biggest nothing in history” and criticised the government for what they saw as “criminal hypocrisy”  ($\checkmark\, \text{sources}$). This reflected a growing sense of disillusionment with the principles behind U.S. containment policy. Events like the Tet Offensive and the My Lai Massacre shattered public confidence and undermined the idea that the U.S. was fighting a just war. Protest movements intensified, and the widening “credibility gap” between government statements and the war's reality forced a reevaluation of policy ($\checkmark\, \text{O-K}$). Under President Nixon, this shift became more formalised. The strategy of Vietnamization aimed to reduce American troop involvement while still supporting anti-communist governments. At the same time, the Nixon Doctrine signaled a significant change: instead of direct military intervention, the U.S. would focus on providing aid and diplomatic support to allies. This coincided with a broader move toward détente, including key diplomatic openings to the Soviet Union (through SALT I) and China. Domestically, the War Powers Act of 1973 sought to limit presidential war powers, ensuring greater Congressional oversight ($\checkmark\, \text{O-K}$). The psychological and political impact of the war, often referred to as the “Vietnam Syndrome”, left American policymakers wary of future large-scale interventions ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$).

Ultimately, the Vietnam War represented both a continuation and a turning point in U.S. Cold War policy. In its early years, it reflected the logic of global containment and the emphasis on credibility that had defined U.S. strategy since the late 1940s. But the prolonged stalemate, moral controversies, and backlash at home compelled a shift toward realism. Military power alone was no longer seen as a reliable tool for achieving ideological goals. By the mid-1970s, U.S. policy had evolved into a more selective, restrained form of engagement. One that emphasised diplomacy, alliances, and caution. This more measured approach would shape American foreign policy for the remainder of the Cold War and beyond ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$).
