# HI1005

$\text{\textquotedblleft}$The Cold War significantly influenced the policies of countries in the Americas." With reference to **two** countries (excluding the US), to what extent do you agree with this statement?

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:
\
General Guidance:
- This question asks students to assess the extent to which the Cold War influenced the internal and foreign policies of two countries in the region.
- To evaluate the Cold War's influence on the internal and foreign policies of two countries, students can discuss both Cold War-related changes and those caused by other factors, or they can concentrate their analysis on just one of these elements.
    - It is essential to note that many changes are driven by Cold War dynamics themselves. Still, others are rooted in internal situations beyond how foreign powers like the US perceive them, and it is crucial to emphasize this.
- Each student should clearly state their opinions or conclusions and support them with enough evidence.

Possible content and examples:
- $\underline{\textrm{Cuba}}$: alignment with the USSR resulted in the adoption of Marxist-Leninist policies (not during, but after the Cuban Revolution), including nationalization of industries, collectivization of agriculture, and the restructuring of the economy around Soviet aid and trade. Foreign policy was also influenced as Cuba supported revolutionary movements across Latin America and Africa.
- $\underline{\textrm{Chile}}$: Allende's socialist reforms, including land redistribution and nationalization, came before Soviet involvement and reflected its own internal political processes. However, these reforms increased hostility from the US, eventually leading it to back Allende's overthrow and install General Augusto Pinochet via military coup. Under Pinochet, Chile moved away from Allende's policies and toward neo-liberal, anti-communist policies to align with the US.
- $\underline{\textrm{Argentina and Brazil}}$: In both countries, military regimes claimed legitimacy by framing themselves as a bulwark against the threat of communism (in alignment with the US and its fears of a $\lq$domino effect' of communism). US support, including initiatives like Operation Condor, helped coordinate the repression of groups it considered socialist, communist, or generally leftist. Policies of censorship and domestic terror were justified using anti-communist Cold War rhetoric regardless of actual motivation.
- $\underline{\textrm{Guatemala}}$: The US backed the overthrow of democratically elected President Jacobo Arbenz after his portrayal as a communist threatened US business interests, leading to decades of authoritarian rule and civil war justified as anti-communist.
- $\underline{\textrm{Nicaragua}}$: The Somoza dictatorship was overthrown in 1979 by the Sandinistas, who implemented socialist-inspired reforms in alignment with Cuba and the USSR. In response, the US funded the Contra insurgency, which pushed its Sandinista policymaking toward wartime defense, limiting the progress of reforms.
- Examples of countries whose policies were generally less influenced by the Cold War (non-examples based on the phrasing of the quotation):
    - Costa Rica, Mexico, Uruguay, Canada (significantly influenced its foreign policy, but less influence domestically).
\
[15 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
The Cold War significantly influenced Latin America's development, shaping its internal and external affairs with the competition between the US and the USSR. Cuba and Chile are key examples of this situation ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). In both cases, the Cold War was the dominant factor influencing policy, but its influence interacted with domestic realities in varying ways and to different extents in each country ($\checkmark\, \text{organisation}$).

The Cuban Revolution's victory in 1959 began a new political era as it dismantled the old political and economic systems. US pressure pushed Cuba toward socialist radicalization and a one-party regime under the Communist Party, inspired by alignment with the USSR ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Cuba nationalized several major industries and sectors, including land and banks. Furthermore, many companies, most of them owned by US investors, like sugar plantations and the Cuban Telephone Company ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$), were also nationalized. Nationalization threatened US interests, leading to increasing pressure from the US, to which the fledgling revolutionary government responded with policy changes. In response to the threat of US-backed counterrevolution, the state established a surveillance system, motivated by the need to defend against “imperialism” ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Soviet alignment implied not just economic and military aid, but also centralized planning, collectivization, and nationalization of education, culture, and media ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$), such as the newspaper Prensa Libre ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$), which allowed Cuba to spread revolutionary ideas within Cuba and beyond as a part of state policy ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In foreign policy, the Cold War made Cuba the focus of hemispheric confrontation. This was built upon the domestic policy's focus on national security and revolutionary internationalism. For example, the CIA-backed attack on the Bay of Pigs was paramount to the consolidation of Cuba's security stance in the region. Although the attack was unsuccessful, it was critical in formalizing an alliance with the USSR. Although some historians such as Kronbluh and Petras suggest that the interplay of Cuban domestic and foreign policy actions was heavily influenced by the wider Cold War tensions and the Soviet Union's interests, Cuban intellectuals stress that the national revolutionary move was the main driving force of the policies.

For instance, the Missile Crisis of 1962 and Castro's aims of spreading revolutionary processes within Latin America and other regions allowed Cuba to become a key international player. For example, its growing participation in the Non-Aligned Movement, which it had joined in 1961, and its promotion of armed insurgency in Nicaragua and Angola ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$) ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$) further positioned Cuba to engage in geopolitics independently and reflected the convergence of foreign policy and internal revolutionary ambitions ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). However, the extent of these foreign policy actions was limited as Cuba received protection and aid from the Soviet Union, including sugar subsidies and preferential oil ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Ultimately, this dependence came at the expense of sacrificing national autonomy and accepting subordination to the Eastern Bloc's agenda ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

With reference to Chile, the election of Salvador Allende (1970) brought about the implementation of structural reforms within a legal and pluralistic framework ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). In acknowledging the significance of international anti-imperialist movements, Allende's government diversified its alliances by establishing diplomatic and commercial relations with socialist countries ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$), including China and nations from the Non-Aligned Movement ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Although Allende himself was not fond of communism, in the eyes of both national opposition groups and the USA, this was evidence of alignment with the Eastern bloc.

International pressure and domestic polarization regarding Allende's idea of “peaceful path to socialism” were intensified by the US fear of the “domino effect” in Latin America. Socialism, even when peaceful and elected democratically, put Chile in a precarious position in the Cold War context. In the short term, this resulted in the US funding of Allende's opposition in different anti-regime actions such as economic sabotage and targeted media campaigns supported by the CIA's control of El Mercurio ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$).  International boycotts exacerbated economic problems, heightened social fears, and legitimized the growing militarization of politics with the radicalization of discourses ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Although some historians argue that the Cold War significantly influenced Chile's political upheaval, others highlight that internal struggles among political groups were just as influential as external intervention. Nonetheless, these perspectives reveal how both foreign and domestic forces drove Chile's transformation during this era ($\checkmark\, \text{perspective}$).

Moreover, the successful 1973 military coup, which overthrew Allende, helped establish a dictatorship led by Pinochet. This regime, aligned with the doctrine of national security and anti-communist efforts, was heavily influenced by the Cold War tensions and US interests. For instance, policies of systematic repression, elimination of political parties, and introduction of neoliberal reforms were supported by the USA ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Chile's domestic policy shifted thus from pluralist inclusion to authoritarian control, influenced by Cold War dynamics ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Additionally, Chile rejoined the inter-American system, cut ties with Cuba and the USSR, and became an ally of Washington in the continental “dirty war” ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Within Operation Condor, Pinochet collaborated with other South American dictatorships and US agencies to suppress leftist movements. This international alliance strengthened the legitimacy of the new regime, securing financial and political support ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In conclusion, the Cold War significantly influenced both Cuba and Chile's domestic and foreign policies. In Cuba, radicalization, statism with major social reforms, and dependence on the USSR emerged alongside US intervention, polarization, and repressive militarization in Chile. Both nations experienced a decrease in national autonomy, but through different routes: Cuba aligned itself with the socialist bloc, while Chile, following the coup, joined the capitalist bloc. In Cuba, the revolution absorbed external pressure and turned it into unity and international revolutionary outreach; in Chile, institutional weakness and foreign intervention led to democratic collapse and dictatorship. In both cases, foreign policy reflected domestic policy and vice versa. The experiences of Cuba and Chile demonstrate how the Cold War not only set opposing models against each other but also reshaped societies, redefined the boundaries of sovereignty, and left lasting impacts on how nations govern themselves ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
