# HI1233 

Compare and contrast the application of Eisenhower's New Look policy in **two** countries in the Americas (not including the United States).

Guidance:
- The command term calls for both similarities and differences in the application of the policy.  A running comparison structure, rather than a structure organized by case study, allows for better analysis, but both can earn marks.
- There are many examples of the application of New Look in Latin America. Some possible examples include:
    - **The support of the Batista regime in Cuba:** With US support, Batista controlled “puppet” governments from 1933 until the elections of 1952. Knowing he would lose, he organized a bloodless coup in March 1952. Batista remained in power until the victory of the Cuban Revolution in 1959.
    - **US support of the Somoza regime in Nicaragua:** The United States provided military assistance and economic support, which helped strengthen the Somoza regime and its National Guard. This allowed the Somoza family to remain in power between 1936 and 1979, and the US to have a reliable anticommunist ally in the region.
    - **Vice President Nixon's tour of Latin America in 1958:** Vice President Nixon's 1958 goodwill tour of Latin America was intended to improve U.S. relations with the region. The initial motivation was to participate in the assumption ceremony of the newly democratically elected president of Argentina, but it also included visits to dictators like Stroessner of Paraguay. The visit was marked by widespread anti-American protests, particularly in Venezuela, where his motorcade was violently attacked. The tour highlighted deep-seated resentment over U.S. support for dictators and perceived economic exploitation.

**Indicative content for the Bolivian National Revolution (1952-1964) and the coup against President Arbenz in Guatemala (1954):**

$\underline{\textrm{Context and reasons for application of the New Look policy}}:$
- Bolivian National Revolution
    - Between 1956 and 1958, the US assisted the Revolutionary Nationalist Movement (MNR)  with 78.3 million USD. In exchange, Bolivia was expected to adopt conservative economic policies that favored US interests. With these policies, the BNR abandoned its original nationalist programme and distanced itself from the miners' movement that had originally supported the 1952 coup.
    - US influence in the way the BNR restructured the Armed Forces determined the Bolivian government's way of dealing with “domestic insurgents”.

- Guatemala
    - The U.S. successfully secured near-unanimous support for a resolution denouncing communist influence in Guatemala at the Tenth Inter-American Conference of the Organization of American States (OAS) in March 1954.
    - Telecommunications infrastructure owned by the United Fruit Company was used to broadcast the “Voice of Liberation” radio station run by CIA agents to portray Arbenz as a Soviet agent.
    - The CIA implemented Operation PBSUCCESS to stage a coup
    - Around 200 exiled Guatemalans, led by Carlos Castrillo Armas, a member of the far-right National Liberation Movement (MLN) who had also worked with the CIA during the Truman administration, were trained and equipped by the CIA in Honduras and Nicaragua to overthrow the government.
    - CIA operatives bombed the main cities of Guatemala while forces invaded from both Honduras and El Salvador. Castillo was then installed as an authoritarian leader with the backing of the US and would rule Guatemala until his assassination in 1957.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
When Eisenhower became president in 1953, he was worried that if the US continued the policy of Containment, it would damage the US economy and hurt the “American Way of Life”. Therefore, he sought to implement a policy that would be more cost-effective in responding to perceived communist aggression. Elaborated by Secretary of State John Foster Dulles, this “New Look” policy relied on diplomatic maneuvering, economic pressure, and covert operations to eliminate perceived threats to national security ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). This policy was applied in different ways to the governments of the Bolivian National Revolution (BNR), which had taken power in 1952, and of Jacobo Árbenz in Guatemala ($\checkmark\, \text{organisation}$). On paper, it would seem that in both cases, the presence of a nationalist government that had the people's support, as well as a rhetoric of reform against the oligarchical powers of the region, would command the same US response. However, the US treatment of the two countries was distinct ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$).

Ideological differences determined how the US administration dealt with both governments. While the government of Árbenz in Guatemala viewed the Communist Party as another reformist group, the government of the Bolivian National Revolution (BNR) viewed it as an enemy that should be destroyed ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). This in part explains US involvement in the overthrow of Àrbenz and US support for the BNR.

However, the difference in the importance of US economic interests in each country better explains the different ways in which the New Look policy was applied ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). While American companies did operate in Bolivia, their actions were minimal. The only notable US-Bolivia commercial relation at the time was the extraction of tin, a strategic resource during World War II. However, by 1952, the US already had a significant stockpile of it. Moreover, the BNR promised to nationalize tin mines but at the same time to compensate American companies ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). This was not the case in Guatemala, where the United Fruit Company (UFC) owned 40% of Guatemalan land, and much of it was left fallow as a way to limit the supply of bananas and thus control their international price. Árbenz nationalized fallow land to distribute to landless peasants and promised to pay the Company what it calculated to be the land value based on the amount declared by United Fruit Company for tax purposes, which came out to be $\text{\textdollar}$1,185,000. The Company claimed that the land was actually worth $\text{\textdollar}$19,355,000 ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). When Árbenz's administration replied that it would have received the amount it was demanding if it had not cheated on its tax forms, the UFC started a publicity campaign to convince the American public that he was a communist ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$).  In a 1953 article by the New York Times, Guatemala was described as “operating under increasingly severe Communist-inspired pressure to rid the country of United States companies” ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$).

Another significant difference that motivated the varied application of the New Look policy was the personal connections between the UFC and the US administration  ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$), which did not exist in Bolivia. For example, CIA director, Allen Dulles, had previously served on the UFC's board ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Aside from the nationalization of US interests, these two countries had few things in common for the US in the 1950s ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$), which helps explain the distinct treatment by the US.

The US also applied the New Look policy inconsistently in other ways. Since the BNR came to power through a coup in 1952, the US could have chosen to view it as illegitimate. However, the Truman administration recognized this new, but illegal government. Arbenz, on the other hand, was elected president through a democratic process in 1951, so his administration automatically received US recognition. Initially, although the nature of the government was different in both countries, they received the same treatment by the US ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$).  However, the US initiated a diplomatic attack on Arbenz at the  1954 conference of the Organization of American States (OAS), where it secured a resolution denouncing communist influence in Guatemala ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). A CIA operation followed which took advantage of the UFC's near-complete control of the communications infrastructure in Guatemala to undermine the government. The CIA coordinated propaganda campaigns, including fictitious radio broadcasts, to demonize Árbenz's government. Spending between 5 and 7 million USD, the US trained Guatemalans led by Colonel Castillo Armas to invade ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). To help, planes piloted by CIA operatives bombed key towns. As a result, Arbenz left the country, and Castillo Armas became dictator. This US-orchestrated coup against a democratic government contrasts with the treatment given to the de facto government of Bolivia. In this case, but with similar results for US interests, the BNR received financial aid to help the new government remain in power. For example, in 1957, 32% of the central government`s budget came from US assistance ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). In exchange, Bolivia had to adopt conservative monetary policies like spending cuts and the opening of oil fields to foreign companies ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). For example, US advisors were involved in the drafting of the 1955 Petroleum Code ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). The US also helped the BNR maintain its control over the country by training and equipping the Bolivian armed forces, enabling them to control civil unrest. By 1964, 20 out of 23 senior officers of the Bolivian army had either attended the U.S. School of the Americas or had visited the United States for training ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$).

In both cases, the application of the New Look policy achieved the goals of obtaining friendly governments in power with little cost to the US public. While in Guatemala, this came about through a covert operation, in Bolivia, it was achieved with economic influence ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). In Bolivia, the combination of limited economic stakes and the government's anti-communist declarations led Washington to adopt a cooperative approach. In Guatemala, strong corporate interests, the Dulles brothers' connections to UFC, and a campaign designed to convince the American public of communist infiltration prompted a  CIA intervention. The New Look policy and its flexibility allowed the Eisenhower administration to adjust its responses based on its interests and not on the nature of each government ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
