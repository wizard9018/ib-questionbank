# HI1481

Examine the reasons for, and effects of, Nixon's covert operations in Chile.
[15 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:

**General Guidance:**

The command term $\text{\textquotedblleft}$examine" requires candidates to investigate both the reasons for and effects of Nixon's covert operations in Chile, explaining why the United States intervened and analysing the consequences of this intervention for Chile, the United States and the wider Cold War. Responses should move beyond a chronological narrative by examining the interaction of ideological, strategic, political and economic motivations before assessing both the immediate and longer-term effects. The strongest responses will distinguish between short-term and long-term consequences while recognising that historians continue to debate the extent to which US covert operations directly caused the 1973 military coup.

**Possible suitable examples include:**
- **Containment and the $\text{\textquotedblleft}$demonstration effect"**
    - Nixon and National Security Adviser Henry Kissinger viewed Salvador Allende's election (1970) as a threat to US containment policy in Latin America.
    - The administration feared that a successful democratically elected Marxist government would encourage similar movements elsewhere in the region (the $\text{\textquotedblleft}$demonstration effect").
    - US policymakers believed Chile could become another Cuba if Allende consolidated power.
    - $\underline{\textrm{Counterpoint:}}$ Chile remained a constitutional democracy under Allende, and there is debate over whether his government posed an immediate strategic threat to the United States.

- **Cold War strategic considerations**
    - The United States sought to prevent increased Soviet and Cuban influence in South America.
    - Nixon authorised covert action, including Track II, to prevent Allende from taking office and later to destabilise his government.
    - The failed kidnapping of General René Schneider (1970), who opposed military intervention, illustrated the risks of covert intervention.
    - $\underline{\textrm{Counterpoint:}}$ Soviet support for Allende remained cautious and limited, suggesting that US policymakers may have exaggerated the communist threat.

- **Economic motivations**
    - The nationalisation of Chile's copper industry threatened major American investments, including those of Anaconda Copper and Kennecott.
    - The United States reduced economic assistance and encouraged international financial institutions to restrict loans to Chile.
    - Nixon reportedly instructed officials to $\text{\textquotedblleft}$make the economy scream" in order to increase pressure on Allende's government.
    - $\underline{\textrm{Counterpoint:}}$ while revisionist historians emphasise the protection of American economic interests, most historians argue these concerns reinforced rather than replaced broader Cold War objectives.

- **Effects on Chile**
    - CIA funding supported opposition parties, newspapers such as El Mercurio, and anti-government organisations, contributing to growing political polarisation.
    - General Augusto Pinochet overthrew Allende on 11 September 1973.
    - Military rule suspended democratic institutions, banned political parties and resulted in approximately 3,000 deaths or disappearances, with tens of thousands imprisoned or tortured.
    - Market-oriented reforms implemented by the $\text{\textquotedblleft}$Chicago Boys" transformed Chile's economy but also increased social inequality.
    - $\underline{\textrm{Counterpoint:}}$ Chile's political and economic divisions had significant domestic origins, meaning US intervention accelerated rather than solely caused democratic collapse.

- **Effects on the United States and the Cold War**
    - The Nixon administration initially viewed Allende's removal as a successful example of containment.
    - Congressional investigations, particularly the Church Committee (1975), exposed CIA activities and increased oversight of US intelligence agencies.
    - US involvement damaged America's international reputation and fuelled criticism that it had undermined democracy while claiming to defend it.
    - Chile became one of the defining examples of Cold War interventionism in Latin America.
    - $\underline{\textrm{Counterpoint:}}$ although Chile demonstrated the global reach of the Cold War, superpower rivalry in Latin America reflected broader ideological and geopolitical competition.

[15 marks]

$\\$

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:

Nixon's covert operations in Chile remain one of the clearest examples of Cold War intervention because they combined ideological rivalry, strategic calculation and covert action to influence the internal politics of another sovereign state. ($\checkmark$ focus) Although economic interests contributed to American policy, the overriding objective was to prevent a successful democratic socialist government from becoming a model for the rest of Latin America. The consequences extended far beyond Chile itself, reshaping Chilean politics, damaging America's international reputation and influencing later debates over the limits of covert intervention. ($\checkmark$ organization)

The principal reason for Nixon's covert operations was the belief that Salvador Allende's presidency threatened the credibility of American containment in the Western Hemisphere. Allende's election in September 1970 represented the first time a Marxist had gained power through democratic elections in Latin America. ($\checkmark$ knowledge) Nixon and Henry Kissinger feared not simply that Chile would align more closely with the Soviet Union, but that a successful constitutional path to socialism would encourage similar political movements elsewhere in the region. This so-called $\text{\textquotedblleft}$demonstration effect" challenged the United States' long-standing objective of limiting communist influence in its traditional sphere of influence. ($\checkmark$ analysis) Consequently, Washington viewed Chile less as an isolated national issue than as a test of whether democratic socialism could succeed during the Cold War.

Strategic concerns reinforced these ideological fears. Although Soviet economic assistance remained relatively limited and Moscow was cautious about committing major resources to Chile, American policymakers believed that Allende's government could eventually strengthen Soviet and Cuban influence in South America. Nixon therefore authorised covert CIA operations, including Track II, to prevent Allende from assuming office and later to undermine his government. ($\checkmark$ examples) The failed kidnapping of General René Schneider, who opposed unconstitutional military intervention, demonstrated both the determination of American policymakers and the dangers of covert action. However, historians generally agree that the United States did not directly organise or control the military coup of September 1973. ($\checkmark$ perspectives) As Paul Sigmund argues, American intervention significantly influenced events without completely determining their outcome. ($\checkmark$ analysis)

Economic considerations also shaped Nixon's policy, although they are best understood as reinforcing Cold War priorities rather than replacing them. Allende's nationalisation of Chile's copper industry threatened substantial American investments held by companies such as Anaconda Copper and Kennecott. The United States responded by reducing economic assistance, discouraging international lending and, according to Nixon's own instructions, attempting to $\text{\textquotedblleft}$make the economy scream." ($\checkmark$ examples) Revisionist historians such as Peter Kornbluh argue that protecting American economic interests formed an important motivation for intervention. Nevertheless, the broader evidence suggests that economic pressure was primarily intended to weaken a government perceived as strategically dangerous rather than simply protect corporate profits. ($\checkmark$ perspectives)

The effects of Nixon's covert operations were most profound within Chile itself. ($\checkmark$ focus) CIA support for opposition political parties, business groups and newspapers such as El Mercurio contributed to increasing political polarisation and undermined confidence in Allende's government. Combined with inflation, shortages, strikes and growing political violence, these pressures created conditions in which military intervention became increasingly likely. On 11 September 1973, General Augusto Pinochet overthrew Allende and established a military dictatorship that lasted until 1990. Democratic institutions were suspended, political parties were banned, and approximately 3,000 people were killed or disappeared while tens of thousands more were imprisoned or tortured. ($\checkmark$ knowledge) Pinochet also introduced market-oriented reforms developed by the $\text{\textquotedblleft}$Chicago Boys", which contributed to long-term economic growth but also increased social inequality. However, as Chilean historian Joaquín Fermandois argues, Chile's political crisis cannot be explained solely through American intervention because deep domestic divisions had already weakened the country's democratic system. ($\checkmark$ perspectives) US covert operations therefore acted as an important accelerant rather than the sole cause of democratic collapse. ($\checkmark$ analysis)

The consequences also extended well beyond Chile. Initially, Nixon regarded Allende's removal as a successful demonstration of containment in Latin America. However, revelations during the Church Committee investigations in 1975 exposed the extent of CIA involvement and damaged America's moral authority internationally. ($\checkmark$ examples) The Chilean case became one of the most prominent examples of Cold War interventionism, reinforcing criticism that the United States had undermined democracy while claiming to defend it against communism. Congress responded by increasing oversight of intelligence agencies, placing greater restrictions on future covert operations. More broadly, Chile illustrated that Cold War competition increasingly relied upon covert intervention, economic pressure and political influence rather than direct military confrontation between the superpowers. ($\checkmark$ analysis)

In conclusion, Nixon's covert operations in Chile were driven primarily by the desire to contain communism and prevent Chile from becoming a successful democratic socialist model for Latin America, while economic interests reinforced these broader strategic objectives. (✓ conclusion) The operations contributed significantly to the destabilisation of Allende's government, but they interacted with profound domestic political and economic tensions rather than independently causing the 1973 coup. Their effects proved far-reaching: Chile experienced nearly two decades of authoritarian rule and widespread human rights abuses, while the United States faced lasting criticism for its willingness to undermine democratic governments in pursuit of Cold War objectives. Ultimately, the Chilean case demonstrates both the global reach of the Cold War and the complex, often unintended, consequences of covert intervention as an instrument of American foreign policy. ($\checkmark$ conclusion)
