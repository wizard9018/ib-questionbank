# HI1182

With reference to its origin, purpose and content, analyse the value and limitations of Source A for a historian studying U.S. foreign policy during the Cold War.
[4 marks]

$\\$

| |  | |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
|**Source A** $\hspace{3em}$ | $\hspace{3em}$ |  Excerpt from a transcript of an interview of US Secretary of State Dean Rusk by NBC correspondent Elie Abel, broadcast over nationwide television on August 5, 1964. |

[Source: Office of the Secretary of Defense Vietnam Task Force. ## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:
\
A combination of the below that meets the following guidelines:
- Both values and limitations must be addressed. If only one is addressed, award a maximum of two marks.
- Origin, purpose and content must all be referenced at some point in the response (either as a value, limitation or both) to receive the full four marks.

$\textbf{\underline{\textrm{Value}}}$:

Origin
- Senior official directly involved in Cold War decision-making; reflects the official mindset and rationale of U.S. leadership. Rusk's long tenure (Kennedy and Johnson administrations) offers continuity across the broader Cold War containment strategy.
- Contemporary source that provides immediate insight into policy justification at a critical turning point (the Gulf of Tonkin incident/crisis).
- Public broadcast makes it useful for studying how the government communicated policy to domestic and international audiences.

Purpose
- Intended to reassure the public and allies that U.S. actions were defensive and morally justified.
- Aims to defend the credibility of U.S. commitments in Southeast Asia and to deter perceived communist aggression.

Content
- Explicitly links Vietnam to a global U.S. mission of building “a decent world community” illustrating idealistic self-perception in U.S. foreign policy.
- Frames the problem as “pressures from the North,” showing the Cold War binary worldview (aggressor vs defender).
- Mentions the need for immediate military action to prevent being seen as a “paper tiger,” providing evidence of credibility concerns central to U.S. containment thinking.

$\textbf{{\underline{\textrm{Limitations}}}}$:

Origin
- Produced by a highly positioned government official likely reflecting official policy line rather than personal doubt or internal debate.
- Occurred in a media setting, not a confidential document, limiting candour and depth.

Purpose
- Designed to justify and defend policy decisions, not to evaluate them critically.
- Propagandistic tone: aims to shape public opinion and win support for potential escalation.

Content
- Presents a simplified narrative: North Vietnam as sole aggressor, South Vietnam as passive victim, reflecting ideological bias.
- Fails to acknowledge U.S. involvement in South Vietnamese politics or covert operations.
- Offers no discussion of alternative diplomatic options or internal disagreements within the administration.
- Lacks reference to wider global Cold War complexities (Sino-Soviet split, French colonial legacy).
- Statements are predictably defensive and moralistic, offering limited empirical detail about actual policy processes.
- Omits controversial details such as the ambiguity over the Gulf of Tonkin attacks, thus limiting its factual reliability.
- Purposeful rhetoric of morality (“decent world community”) may mask strategic motives, such as containment and prestige.
\
[4 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
The origin is of value as it comes from a senior U.S. official directly involved in Cold War decision-making. As Secretary of State, Rusk's perspective reflects the official mindset and strategic rationale of American leadership during this period ($\checkmark\, \text{VO}$).   The source is valuable in its purpose because it was intended to justify U.S. actions in Southeast Asia and explain foreign policy aims to the public, providing insight into how the government sought to frame its involvement ($\checkmark\, \text{VP}$).

However, the origin also limits its value, as it represents the viewpoint of those responsible for shaping and defending policy, rather than offering independent or critical reflection ($\checkmark\, \text{LO}$).  Furthermore, the content presents a simplified, one-sided account that identifies North Vietnam as the sole aggressor and ignores U.S. interference in South Vietnamese politics, revealing strong ideological bias ($\checkmark\, \text{LC}$).
