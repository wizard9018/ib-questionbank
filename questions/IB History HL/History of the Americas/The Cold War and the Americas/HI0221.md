## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:

|Top Markband Descriptors (5-6 marks)|Labels |
| :-- | :-- |
|Includes clear points of comparison| ($\checkmark\, \text{similarity}$) |
|Includes clear points of contrast| ($\checkmark\, \text{difference}$) |
|Provides evidence from the text (SBQ) or own knowledge (SAQ) to validate arguments| ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$) |

For full marks, both similarities and differences must be addressed.

For comparison, any of the following similarities:
- Both sources reflect the perception of a direct and immediate threat to the United States arising from the installation of Soviet missiles in Cuba.
- Both emphasize the importance of Cuba's geographical proximity as a key factor in increasing the perceived danger.

For contrast, any of the following differences:
- Source A focuses on a technical representation of the threat (the range of the missiles), whereas - Source B uses an emotional and political speech to emphasize the impact of the crisis on national security.
- Source A was created for an internal audience with strategic purposes, while Source B was designed for a broader audience, aiming to influence public opinion and justify political actions.
- Source A does not explicitly express moral judgments or ideological evaluations, whereas Source B directly criticizes the actions of the Soviet Union, labeling them as deceptive and provocative.
\
[6 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Both Source A and Source B reflect the U.S. perception of a direct and immediate threat posed by the installation of Soviet missiles in Cuba ($\checkmark\, \text{similarity}$). Source A visually highlights the range of the Soviet missiles, offering concrete evidence of their capability to reach U.S. territory, which validates the concern of a direct nuclear threat. Source B, however, provides more detailed reasoning, with Kennedy framing the missile deployment as not only a physical threat but a political one, calling it a "deliberately provocative and unjustified change in the status quo." ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$)

Both sources also highlight Cuba's geographical proximity as a critical factor that amplifies the perceived danger ($\checkmark\, \text{similarity}$). While Source A depicts this proximity visually with the theoretical missile ranges from Cuba, Source B mentions the “buildup of Communist missiles--in an area well known to have a special and historical relationship to the United States and the nations of the Western Hemisphere” ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$).

However, the sources present contrasts in the US perception of threat as well. Source A provides a technical depiction of the threat, focusing on the missile range, while Source B employs an emotional and politically charged speech to underscore the crisis's impact on national security ($\checkmark\, \text{difference}$) such as Kennedy’s use of the phrase “clear and present danger” ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$).

Additionally, Source A avoids moral or ideological commentary, whereas Source B explicitly criticizes the Soviet Union's actions ($\checkmark\, \text{difference}$), portraying them as the aggressor by saying their actions are “deliberately provocative and unjustified” and in “violation” of their promises ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$).
