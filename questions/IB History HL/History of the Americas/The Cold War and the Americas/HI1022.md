# HI1022

Compare and contrast the foreign policies of Presidents Kennedy and Johnson.
[15 marks]

**General Guidance:**
- The question requires students to provide an account of the similarities and differences in the foreign policies of Kennedy and Johnson.
    - When using a comparative approach, it is advisable to organize the content in a specific order: first, the similarities, then the differences. Additionally, for each case, identify topics such as similarities in views of communism or perceptions of the “domino theory,” and then discuss differences related to Cuba, intervention in Southeast Asia, and so on.
- There does not need to be a balance of similarity and difference, but both must be features of the response.
- Students' opinions or conclusions will be presented clearly and supported by appropriate evidence.

**Possible content and examples:**

**Similarities**
- Both presidents reaffirmed their commitment to stopping communism in Southeast Asia, especially in Vietnam.
- The intervention strategy was justified by the “domino effect” doctrine, which argued that the fall of Vietnam would lead to communism spreading across the region.
- Kennedy and Johnson supported the South Vietnamese government with military and economic aid, including assistance, advisors, and equipment.
- Both administrations viewed the Vietnam War as a key part of the global effort to oppose Soviet and Chinese influence during the Cold War.
- Both presidents focused on stopping communism worldwide, not just in Asia, but also in regions such as Latin America and Africa.
- Each administration emphasized nuclear deterrence and bolstered arms control efforts, including dialogue with the Soviet Union (e.g., the signing of the Nuclear Test Ban Treaty under Kennedy, which Johnson continued to uphold).
- Both engaged in crisis management during Cold War standoffs—Kennedy with the Cuban Missile Crisis and Johnson with the Dominican Republic intervention—showing a willingness to use force or negotiate to protect U.S. interests in the Americas.

**Differences**
- Kennedy initially focused on limited intervention by sending military advisors and technical assistance, avoiding, as much as possible, the large-scale deployment of combat troops.
- During Johnson's presidency, the conflict significantly escalated, including deploying large numbers of U.S. troops and increasing bombings over North Vietnam (Operation Rolling Thunder).
- Kennedy was more cautious about direct intervention, aiming to avoid getting involved in a prolonged war, while Johnson, after the Gulf of Tonkin incident, obtained Congressional approval to escalate military involvement without a formal declaration of war.
- Johnson faced growing domestic and international opposition as the conflict escalated, which in turn influenced his foreign policy and the subsequent peace negotiations. In contrast, Kennedy did not encounter the same level of social unrest over the war.
- Kennedy's administration dealt with the Bay of Pigs invasion and the Cuban Missile Crisis, focusing on isolating and pressuring Cuba, while Johnson paid less attention to Cuba, prioritizing Southeast Asia.
- Kennedy launched the Alliance for Progress to encourage economic growth and political reform in Latin America; Johnson's approach was less ambitious, relying more on military intervention when needed.
- Kennedy's policies focused on high-stakes brinkmanship and efforts to establish direct communication with the Soviets, such as creating the Washington-Moscow "hotline" after the Missile Crisis. Johnson's administration paid more attention to arms control, including setting the stage for Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT).
- Kennedy took a balanced approach toward Israel and the Arab nations, while Johnson shifted toward stronger support for Israel, especially after the Six-Day War in 1967.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
During the turbulent years of the early Cold War, the foreign policies of Presidents Kennedy (1961-1963) and Johnson (1963-1969) reflected both continuity and change in America's approach to global affairs. Both leaders were committed to containing communism and protecting U.S. interests, especially in Southeast Asia and the Western Hemisphere, but their methods varied significantly ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). While Kennedy and Johnson shared the goal of stopping the spread of communism, Kennedy's foreign policy was characterized by cautious, limited intervention and diplomatic engagement. In contrast, Johnson's presidency saw a dramatic escalation of military involvement, especially in Vietnam, and a greater reliance on force, highlighting key differences in the balance between diplomacy and military action in their administrations ($\checkmark\, \text{organisation}$).

Both Kennedy and Johnson pursued a policy in Vietnam and Southeast Asia centered on containing communism through a combination of military and economic support for anti-communist governments ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Their administration's involvement in the region was based on the “domino effect” doctrine. To address this perceived threat, both presidents adopted a strategy of supporting and reinforcing the South Vietnamese regime, not only with military hardware and personnel but also by providing economic aid, in hopes of strengthening its stability and legitimacy ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). The threat of regional instability and the potential for a domino effect toward communism played a decisive role in shaping U.S. foreign policy throughout the 1960s ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

Beyond Vietnam, Kennedy and Johnson consistently pursued a global strategy of containing communism, using both diplomatic and military measures to safeguard U.S. interests wherever threats emerged ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Their administrations adopted a global containment strategy, aiming to stop the spread of communism wherever it posed a threat, whether in Latin America, Africa, or elsewhere ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Moreover, Kennedy, who was consistently cautious in his approach to nuclear escalation, handled the Cuban Missile Crisis ($\checkmark\, \text{example}$), ultimately securing a peaceful resolution while showing military strength. Johnson, on the other hand, used similar crisis management strategies during the intervention in the Dominican Republic ($\checkmark\, \text{example}$), justifying U.S. army involvement as necessary to prevent a communist takeover in the Western Hemisphere ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Both leaders, therefore, demonstrated a willingness to use force or negotiation as needed, always aiming to protect American interests and stop the spread of communism in the Americas ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

Although both Kennedy and Johnson were committed to containing communism, their contrasting strategies in Vietnam reveal key differences in how they balanced military action and diplomatic engagement during the Cold War ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Kennedy's approach to Vietnam emphasized caution and limited intervention. He focused on dispatching military advisors and offering technical assistance to the South Vietnamese government, deliberately avoiding the commitment of large-scale U.S. combat forces ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Kennedy's administration aimed to contain communism through strategic support and capacity-building, wary of the dangers of deep military engagement. His goal was to strengthen South Vietnam without dragging the United States into a prolonged, costly war ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). In contrast, Johnson's presidency marked a significant escalation of the conflict. After the Gulf of Tonkin incident in 1964, Johnson gained Congressional approval, which authorized expanding U.S. involvement without a formal declaration of war. So, the United States sent hundreds of thousands of combat troops and carried out continuous bombing campaigns over North Vietnam—most notably Operation Rolling Thunder ($\checkmark\, \text{example}$). This change reflected Johnson's belief that a much stronger response was necessary to prevent South Vietnam from collapsing and, by extension, to uphold the “domino theory” of communist containment. It was also partially motivated by his desire to end the conflict quickly and focus more on his domestic “Great Society” reforms ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Kennedy remained more restrained, preferring to exert influence through advisory missions and economic aid rather than direct military intervention. He was careful about the risks of escalation and aimed to limit American exposure in Vietnam. Johnson, however, showed a greater willingness to use overwhelming force, believing that military superiority could break the will of North Vietnam and its allies ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Regarding Johnson, historian Arthur Schlesinger sees pragmatic adaptation but misses Kennedy's vision. In contrast, C. Dale Walton is more critical, emphasizing increased military interventions and neglected reforms. Together, they depict a pragmatic yet controversial legacy, defined by shifting approaches to containment and global transformation ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$).

In addressing Cold War challenges in the Western Hemisphere, Kennedy and Johnson adopted distinctly different approaches to Cuba and Latin America, reflecting their broader philosophies on containing communism ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Kennedy's presidency focused heavily on Cuba and hemispheric issues ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). He dealt with the implementation and failure of the Bay of Pigs invasion and successfully handled the Cuban Missile Crisis, creating direct channels like the Washington-Moscow hotline to avoid nuclear conflict ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Kennedy also launched the Alliance for Progress, a broad initiative aimed at promoting economic growth and political change in Latin America as a means of combating communist influence peacefully. Johnson, however, paid less attention to Cuba, shifting U.S. foreign policy focus toward Southeast Asia and the escalating Vietnam conflict. In Latin America, Johnson's strategy was less ambitious and relied more on military interventions, when necessary ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$), such as in the Dominican Republic ($\checkmark\, \text{example}$), rather than on comprehensive development initiatives. Schlesinger and Walton's contrasting views highlight the complexity of Kennedy and Johnson's foreign policies during the Cold War. Schlesinger favors Kennedy's balanced, development-oriented diplomacy, while Walton criticizes its inconsistency and overlooked inequalities ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$).

In summary, Kennedy and Johnson's Cold War policies were characterized by a strong commitment to containing communism, the practical use of the “domino theory” in Southeast Asia, extensive military and economic support for anti-communist governments, and a willingness to handle crises through a combination of negotiation and, when necessary, military intervention. Kennedy preferred limited engagement and economic efforts, staying cautious of deep military involvement. Johnson, on the other hand, was ready to escalate militarily, dedicating significant American resources to Vietnam and taking a more interventionist approach in different parts of the hemisphere. These differences highlight distinct philosophies regarding the balance between military force and diplomatic and economic solutions for containing communism during the Cold War ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
