# HI0300

Examine the extent to which the Second World War improved the position of racial and ethnic minorities in the Americas.
[15 marks]

Guidance for responses
- Candidates should assess the degree to which the war led to improvements (social, economic, political, or cultural) for racial and ethnic minorities across the Americas, and balance this against evidence of continuity or worsening discrimination.
- Improvements might include new employment opportunities, migration to cities, military service and recognition, political mobilisation, and the beginnings of civil rights activism.
- Limitations might include: persistent segregation, violence and discrimination, treatment of Japanese Americans and Japanese Canadians, and limited long-term change.
- Candidates should choose more than one country (United States, Canada, Brazil, Mexico, etc.), and must evaluate both positive changes and enduring inequalities.
- Strong responses will include comparative analysis across regions.
- A substantiated conclusion should weigh whether the war represented a turning point or more of a temporary shift.

Indicative Content

$\underline{\textrm{United States}}$

African Americans:
- Over 1 million served in segregated units (e.g., Tuskegee Airmen). Military service fostered pride but highlighted inequality.
- “Double V Campaign” called for victory abroad and at home against racism.
- Migration from rural South to urban centres for war industries (Second Great Migration).
- FEPC (Fair Employment Practices Committee, 1941) banned discrimination in defense industries after A. Philip Randolph's threatened march.
- Gains were limited: segregation persisted, lynching continued, race riots (Detroit 1943).

Japanese Americans:
- 120,000 forcibly relocated under Executive Order 9066 (U.S. West Coast).
- Loss of property, harsh conditions in camps → dramatic worsening of position.
- Military service (442nd Regimental Combat Team) showed loyalty and won recognition, but discrimination remained.

Mexican Americans:
- Bracero Program (1942): provided farm labourers to the U.S. → economic opportunities, but exploitative conditions.
- Zoot Suit Riots (1943): U.S. servicemen attacked Mexican American youth in Los Angeles, highlighting prejudice.
- Mixed legacy: some integration into the war economy, but discrimination persisted.

Native Americans:
- 25,000 served in the armed forces; Navajo Code Talkers became famous.
- Many migrated to cities for war industries.
- Limited long-term gains; faced difficulty readjusting postwar.

$\underline{\textrm{Canada}}$

- About 3,000 Japanese Canadians were removed from coastal British Columbia, their property confiscated, and they were not allowed to return until 1949. → position worsened.
- Indigenous peoples enlisted and gained skills, but rights were not significantly improved postwar; some lost their Indian status.
- Black Canadians served in the forces and gained some visibility, but faced employment discrimination at home.

Latin America

$\underline{\textrm{Brazil}}$:
- Afro-Brazilians served in the Força Expedicionária Brasileira (FEB) in Italy, challenging racial stereotypes.
- Urban migration to wartime industries created limited opportunities.
- Still faced structural racism and limited political inclusion.

$\underline{\textrm{Mexico}}$:
- The Bracero Program (1942-1964) created economic links, but many workers were exploited.
- Indigenous and rural Mexicans gained some wartime industrial employment, but inequalities persisted.

$\underline{\textrm{Other Latin American countries}}$:
- Some restrictions were placed on Japanese immigrants in Peru, Brazil, and Mexico; deportations from Peru to U.S. camps → worsened the position of Japanese Latin Americans.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
The Second World War significantly affected racial and ethnic minorities across the Americas, but impacts varied widely ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). For some, such as African Americans and Mexican Americans in the United States, the war opened new opportunities in employment, migration, and military service. Others, such as Japanese Americans and Japanese Canadians, saw their position dramatically worsen through forced relocation and property loss. In Latin America, opportunities were limited, with Afro-Brazilians and Indigenous peoples experiencing only modest changes. This essay will argue that while the war created short-term economic and political openings for some minorities, overall progress was uneven and often overshadowed by persistent discrimination ($\checkmark\, \text{organisation}$).

In the United States, African Americans experienced both crucial gains and continued systemic discrimination ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). More than one million African Americans served in the armed forces, often in segregated units. The Tuskegee Airmen, an all-Black fighter group, became a symbol of African American skill and patriotism ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). On the home front, the “Double V” campaign called for victory against fascism abroad and racism at home, reflecting growing political awareness ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). The war economy also facilitated the migration of hundreds of thousands of African Americans from the rural South to urban centres in the North and West, sometimes called the Second Great Migration ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Employment in war industries offered higher wages and opportunities previously unavailable. In 1941, after A. Philip Randolph threatened a March on Washington, Roosevelt issued Executive Order 8802, banning discrimination in defense industries and creating the Fair Employment Practices Committee (FEPC)($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). While these were genuine improvements, segregation persisted in the military and civilian life, while race riots in Detroit in 1943 and continued lynching revealed deep-seated racism ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Thus, while the war helped lay the groundwork for the postwar civil rights movement, it did not transform African Americans' position in the short term.

Other U.S. minorities faced mixed experiences ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Japanese Americans endured some of the harshest treatment. After Pearl Harbor, 120,000 were forcibly removed from the West Coast to inland camps under Executive Order 9066 ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Two-thirds were U.S. citizens, and most lost homes, farms, and businesses ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Although some Japanese Americans served heroically in the military, notably the 442nd Regimental Combat Team, their loyalty did not prevent widespread suspicion and discrimination ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). For Mexican Americans, the war years brought new opportunities and new challenges($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). The Bracero Program, launched in 1942, brought hundreds of thousands of Mexican workers into U.S. agriculture ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). While this provided employment, conditions were often exploitative, with poor wages and little legal protection ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). At the same time, Mexican Americans participated in the armed forces and found new industrial employment in U.S. cities. However, events like the Zoot Suit Riots of 1943 in Los Angeles, when U.S. servicemen attacked Mexican American youth, revealed the persistence of discrimination ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Native Americans also contributed: around 25,000 served in the armed forces, including the famous Navajo Code Talkers, while many others migrated to cities for wartime employment ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Yet after the war, many Native veterans returned to reservations facing continued poverty and marginalisation, showing limited long-term improvement ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In Canada, the experience of minorities similarly showed both continuity and regression ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). The most dramatic case was the treatment of Japanese Canadians. In 1942, 22,000 were forcibly removed from the coast of British Columbia, their homes and fishing boats confiscated and sold by the Custodian of Enemy Property ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Families were relocated inland, and many were not permitted to return to the coast until 1949, long after the war had ended ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). This represented a clear deterioration in their social and economic position. Indigenous Canadians contributed significantly to the armed forces and gained new skills and experiences, but returned to communities where systemic discrimination remained in place ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Black Canadians served in the military and gained some visibility, yet faced ongoing barriers to equal employment and housing ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Thus, for Canada, the war years offered few lasting improvements for minorities, and in some cases, such as Japanese Canadians, conditions worsened ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In Latin America, the impact of the war on minorities was more indirect, yet still notable ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). In Brazil, Afro-Brazilians enlisted in the Força Expedicionária Brasileira (FEB) and fought in Italy, challenging long-held stereotypes about their role in society ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Some also benefited from urban migration as war industries expanded, particularly in Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). However, these opportunities did not significantly alter Brazil's entrenched racial hierarchy, and social inequality remained stark. In Mexico, the war spurred industrial growth and urbanisation, creating opportunities for Indigenous and rural workers to move into cities ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Yet, discrimination persisted, and most rural communities saw little change in living standards. In Peru, Japanese Peruvians faced hostility; under U.S. pressure, around 1,800 were deported to camps in the United States, where they were held for the duration of the war ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Others had property confiscated or were subject to surveillance and restrictions ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). This reflected continuity of prejudice rather than improvement ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

Overall, the impact of the Second World War on racial and ethnic minorities in the Americas was mixed, with more continuity than change ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). For African Americans and Mexican Americans in the United States, the war created new employment opportunities, fostered migration, and expanded political activism, though discrimination and segregation persisted ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Japanese Americans and Japanese Canadians saw their position deteriorate dramatically through forced relocation and property loss, while in Latin America, Afro-Brazilians, Indigenous peoples, and Japanese communities experienced only limited or negative change ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). In conclusion, the war produced some temporary and uneven improvements, particularly in economic and military participation, but it did not fundamentally alter social status; rather, it exposed inequalities and set the stage for postwar struggles for rights and recognition ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
