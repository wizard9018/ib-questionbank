# HI0587

Contrast the impact of conscription in the United States and Canada during World War II.

[4 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:

\

Any **two** of the following with its corresponding evidence:

 - In Canada, the issue of conscription generated a division in public opinion between the English speakers and the Quebecois, while in the US, it was generally less debated by the public, especially after Pearl Harbor. 

      - Although 70$\%$ of Canadians agreed to conscription in the plebiscite organized by the government, in Quebec only 20$\%$ of voters agreed. 

     - While there was some debate about the need for the draft in the US before Pearl Harbor, after it, it largely disappeared. Voluntary enlistment increased such that President Roosevelt had to issue an Executive Order (9279) to slow down enlistments in order to protect the US workforce.

 - The impact of conscription in the Canadian war effort was negligible, while in the US, it resulted in most of the combatants. 

     - Most Canadian combatants were volunteers. Only after 1944 did the Canadian government have to resort to conscription. Until the surrender of Germany only 2500 conscripts saw battle. In the US only about 40$\%$ of servicemen were volunteers. The majority of the 10 million strong Armed Forces were draftees. 

 - The governments dealt differently with those who were conscripted and refused to fight.

     - In Canada, there was a mutiny of soldiers who refused to be sent overseas in Terrace, British Columbia. The mutiny was quickly put down, and those involved were sent to other military bases. In the US, $\text{\textquotedblleft}$conscientious objectors” were allowed to serve as medics or work in the Civilian Public Service. The idea was to keep them out of public view. Members of the African American Nation of Islam refused to serve due to political reasons. They, including their leader Elijah Mohammad, were convicted on charges of draft dodging. 

\

[4 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:

\

In the war itself, the impact of conscription was quite different ($\checkmark$). One of the reasons that Prime Minister King was able to order conscription so late in the war was the large number of volunteers that made conscription unnecessary. This changed only after 1944 so, in total, only 2500 Canadian conscripts saw any action before Germany's surrender. In the US, the majority of servicemen, about 60$\%$, were actually draftees ($\checkmark$).

Both countries dealt differently with those who were conscripted but refused to fight ($\checkmark$). When in Canada there was a mutiny in Terrace, British Columbia ,of soldiers who refused to be sent overseas, it was quickly put dow,n and those involved were sent to other military bases suffering no long-term sanctions. However, in the US, while $\text{\textquotedblleft}$conscientious objectors” were allowed to serve as medics or work in the Civilian Public Service, this was not so for those who had political reasons to oppose conscription ($\checkmark$). The most notable case is that of members of the African American Nation of Islam who refused to serve and fight for a country that did not grant them equal rights. They, including their leader Elijah Mohammad, were convicted on charges of draft dodging and sent to prison ($\checkmark$).
