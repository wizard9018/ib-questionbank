# HI1479

Evaluate the significance of the US use of atomic weapons against Japan.
[15 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:

**General Guidance:**

The command term $\text{\textquotedblleft}$evaluate" requires candidates to make a judgement about the significance of the United States' use of atomic weapons against Japan, weighing its importance against other military, political and diplomatic factors associated with the end of the Second World War. Responses should move beyond describing the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki by assessing both their immediate and long-term significance. The strongest responses will distinguish between significance for Japan, the ending of the Second World War, and the post-war international order, while recognising that historians continue to debate whether the atomic bombings alone were decisive in securing Japan's surrender.

**Possible suitable examples include:**
- **Immediate military significance**
    - Hiroshima (6 August 1945) and Nagasaki (9 August 1945) caused unprecedented destruction, demonstrating the devastating power of nuclear weapons.
    - The bombings destroyed important military and industrial centres while inflicting enormous civilian casualties.
    - In his surrender broadcast, Emperor Hirohito referred to the "new and most cruel bomb", indicating that the atomic attacks influenced his decision to end the war.
    - Counterpoint: despite Hiroshima, Japan's Supreme War Council remained divided, with War Minister Korechika Anami and other military leaders continuing to favour resistance until after the Soviet declaration of war and Nagasaki.

- **Contribution to Japan's surrender**
    - The combination of Hiroshima, the Soviet declaration of war (8 August 1945) and Nagasaki rapidly transformed Japan's strategic position.
    - The atomic bombings strengthened Emperor Hirohito's ability to overrule military hardliners and accept surrender.
    - Historians such as Richard Frank argue that the bombings were decisive in convincing Japan that continued resistance was impossible.
    - Counterpoint: historians including Tsuyoshi Hasegawa argue that the Soviet invasion of Manchuria was equally, or even more, significant because it destroyed Japan's hopes of Soviet mediation.

- **Significance for the ending of the Second World War**
    - Japan surrendered on 15 August 1945 and formally signed the Instrument of Surrender on 2 September 1945.
    - The bombings helped avoid Operation Downfall, the planned Allied invasion of Japan.
    - Contemporary Allied planning anticipated hundreds of thousands of Allied casualties and potentially millions of Japanese military and civilian casualties if an invasion became necessary.
    - Counterpoint: Japan's military position had already become untenable because of conventional bombing, naval blockade, shortages of food and fuel, and the Soviet entry into the war.

- **Significance for international relations and the Cold War**
    - The bombings marked the beginning of the nuclear age.
    - They demonstrated overwhelming American military and technological superiority.
    - The Soviet Union accelerated its atomic programme, successfully testing its own nuclear weapon in 1949.
    - Nuclear deterrence, mutually assured destruction (MAD) and the arms race became defining features of the Cold War.
    - Revisionist historians such as Gar Alperovitz argue that the bombings were intended partly to strengthen the US diplomatic position towards the Soviet Union.
    - Counterpoint: although atomic weapons transformed Cold War diplomacy, ideological rivalry and competing security interests remained the fundamental causes of the Cold War.

- **Humanitarian and ethical significance**
    - Well over 100,000 people died by the end of 1945, with many more suffering long-term effects from radiation exposure.
    - The bombings raised enduring questions about civilian casualties, proportionality and the morality of nuclear warfare.
    - Historians remain divided over whether the bombings were militarily necessary or ethically justified.
    - Counterpoint: supporters argue that, despite the humanitarian cost, the bombings ultimately saved lives by preventing a prolonged invasion.

[15 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:

The United States' use of atomic weapons against Japan remains one of the most controversial decisions of the Second World War because of both its immediate military impact and its lasting consequences for international relations. ($\checkmark$ focus) Although the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki accelerated Japan's surrender, historians continue to debate whether they were decisive or whether the Soviet declaration of war played an equally important role. This essay argues that the atomic bombings were highly significant in bringing about Japan's surrender, but their greatest significance lay in inaugurating the nuclear age and fundamentally reshaping international politics. ($\checkmark$ organization)

The atomic bombings made a major contribution to Japan's surrender by demonstrating that continued resistance was increasingly hopeless. Hiroshima was devastated on 6 August 1945 and Nagasaki three days later, causing destruction on an unprecedented scale. ($\checkmark$ knowledge) Emperor Hirohito later referred to the "new and most cruel bomb" when explaining Japan's decision to surrender, suggesting that the attacks strengthened his determination to seek peace. However, Hiroshima alone did not immediately end Japanese resistance. Members of the Supreme War Council remained divided, and War Minister Korechika Anami continued to favour fighting despite the devastation. ($\checkmark$ examples) The bombings therefore strengthened the position of those seeking surrender, but they did not by themselves produce an immediate political consensus. ($\checkmark$ analysis)

The significance of the bombings becomes clearer when considered alongside the Soviet declaration of war on 8 August 1945. The rapid Soviet invasion of Manchuria destroyed Japan's final hope that Moscow might mediate a negotiated settlement with the Allies. Historian Tsuyoshi Hasegawa argues that Soviet intervention was at least as significant as the atomic bombings in forcing Japan to surrender, whereas Richard Frank maintains that the unprecedented destructive power of the bombs was the decisive factor. ($\checkmark$ perspectives) Rather than viewing these explanations as mutually exclusive, the evidence suggests that Hiroshima, the Soviet invasion and Nagasaki combined to convince both the Emperor and many civilian leaders that further resistance was futile. ($\checkmark$ analysis)

The atomic bombings were also significant because they brought the Pacific War to a rapid conclusion. American planners expected that Operation Downfall, the planned invasion of Japan, would result in extremely heavy casualties, with contemporary estimates ranging from hundreds of thousands of Allied casualties to several million Japanese military and civilian casualties. ($\checkmark$ examples) Japan's surrender on 15 August, formally signed on 2 September 1945, eliminated the need for such an invasion and brought the Second World War to an end. However, Japan's position had already deteriorated because of conventional bombing, naval blockade and severe shortages of resources. Consequently, the bombings accelerated the end of the war but should be viewed as one of several interconnected factors leading to Japan's defeat. ($\checkmark$ analysis)

Perhaps the greatest significance of the atomic bombings lay not in ending the war, but in transforming the post-war international order. ($\checkmark$ focus) The destruction of Hiroshima and Nagasaki marked the beginning of the nuclear age and demonstrated overwhelming American military and technological superiority. The Soviet Union accelerated its own nuclear programme, successfully testing an atomic bomb in 1949, while nuclear deterrence and the arms race became defining features of the Cold War. Revisionist historian Gar Alperovitz has argued that the bombings were intended partly to strengthen the United States' diplomatic position towards the Soviet Union, although most historians continue to emphasise Japan's defeat as the primary objective. ($\checkmark$ perspectives) Regardless of this debate, the existence of nuclear weapons fundamentally changed the way international crises were managed throughout the Cold War. ($\checkmark$ analysis)

The bombings also possessed profound humanitarian significance that continues to shape historical debate. Well over 100,000 people died by the end of 1945, while many survivors experienced long-term effects from burns and radiation. ($\checkmark$ knowledge) Critics argue that the attacks constituted an unnecessary use of force against civilian populations, particularly because Japan was already close to defeat. Others maintain that the bombings ultimately saved lives by preventing a prolonged invasion that would likely have produced even greater casualties. These competing interpretations illustrate why the use of atomic weapons remains one of the most contested decisions of the twentieth century. ($\checkmark$ perspectives)

In conclusion, the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki were highly significant in ending the Second World War, but their importance extended well beyond Japan's surrender. ($\checkmark$ conclusion) They strengthened those advocating peace within the Japanese government, hastened the end of the Pacific War and helped avoid an invasion, although they acted alongside the Soviet declaration of war and Japan's deteriorating military position. Their greatest significance, however, was the beginning of the nuclear age, which transformed international relations, reshaped Cold War diplomacy and permanently altered the nature of warfare. ($\checkmark$ conclusion)
