# HI1001

Evaluate the economic impact of the Second World War on **two** countries of the Americas.

[15 marks]

---

**General Guidance**:

 - The question requires that candidates consider the economic impact of the Second World War on two countries in the Americas. 

 - The economic effects can extend beyond the stated time period, but they must be clearly linked to the question. 

 - Candidates may offer equal coverage of the impact on both countries, or they may prioritize their discussion of either one. However, both aspects will be a feature of the response. A comparative approach may or may not be used. 

     - In the case of using a comparative approach, it is advisable to organize it by theme, exploring it first in one of the chosen countries and then in the other.

 - Each student should present their opinions or conclusions clearly and support them with appropriate evidence. 

**Possible content and examples**:

 - Candidates may refer to a wide range of effects of war on domestic economics, including increased GDP, higher employment rates, end of the Depression, higher inflation, industrialization and new industries, changes in trade patterns, changes in the economic role of government, economic integration of markets, and promotion of technology, especially new technologies.

 - Answers will vary according to the selected case study. Possible countries could be:

     - Brazil: World War II propelled rapid industrial growth and a substantial GDP increase. Government investment and Allied demand modernized key sectors like steel and infrastructure. U.S. support accelerated urbanization and established Brazil as a regional economic power.

     - Argentina: The wartime economy grew thanks to agricultural exports and foreign reserves. Industrial activity expanded, boosting prosperity in both rural and urban areas. Despite facing inflation and shortages, Argentina emerged from the conflict economically stronger and more diverse.

     - Mexico: Its economy expanded quickly because of rising demand for raw materials and exports. Government collaboration with the United States fueled industrial growth and infrastructure development. This modernization set the stage for prosperity after the war.

     - Canada: Depression ended, leading to quick industrial growth and more jobs. Massive government spending on war production made it a significant manufacturing power. Continued prosperity after the war and stronger economic ties with the U.S. followed.

     - The United States:  The Great Depression ended, leading to economic growth and full employment. Massive government spending and war production transformed the U.S. into a global industrial leader. Postwar prosperity was driven by technological innovation, expanded infrastructure, and the growth of consumer society.

 - While other relevant factors, for example, social effects, may be referred to, they would be relevant mainly as context for the issue raised in the question.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:

\

World War II transformed American economies, notably in Latin America, by spurring swift financial growth, industrialization, and social change. ($\checkmark\, \text{ focus}$). This essay examines Argentina and Brazil by highlighting GDP growth, employment, industrialization, and government intervention. ($\checkmark\, \text{ organization}$). The outbreak of war swiftly ended the economic stagnation of the 1930s caused by global conflict, and both countries saw GDP growth during the war years ($\checkmark\, \text{ focus}$).

Argentina and Brazil experienced notable economic growth and change due to the Second World War. Argentina's GDP grew as war-driven demand for agricultural exports, including meat, wheat, and corn, brought in foreign reserves and boosted domestic consumption ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). Industrial output rose with the export boom, spurring prosperity in rural and urban areas. New meat-packing plants and grain mills sprang up near ports like Buenos Aires and Rosario ($\checkmark\, \text{ examples}$). Inflation and shortages posed challenges, but Argentina emerged from the war as a more economically diverse country ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$). Brazil's GDP growth was even more pronounced. The Vargas government used the war for economic transformation, with Allied demand for raw materials fueling an export boom ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). Government investment led to rapid industrialization and high growth rates in the early 1940s. The National Steel Company (CSN) in Volta Redonda, funded by Brazil and the U.S., is an example of state-led industrial investment, like modern infrastructure, such as railroads and ports ($\checkmark\, \text{ examples}$).  The wartime boom also accelerated urbanization as rural populations moved to cities for new opportunities ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$).

The war also spurred rapid industrial growth in both countries ($\checkmark\, \text{ focus}$). Previously, Argentina relied on agricultural exports, but wartime import disruptions prompted a shift to Import-Substitution Industrialization (ISI). New industries such as chemicals, machinery, textiles, and processed foods emerged with tariff protection and government support ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). The government backed projects like the National Fertilizer Factory and Bagó Laboratories to boost domestic production of fertilizers and medicines ($\checkmark\, \text{ examples}$). Public-private partnerships and protectionist policies diversified Argentina's industrial sector ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). This period of import substitution and growth in the domestic market encouraged technological innovation and industrial expansion, supporting the emergence of new industries and helping Argentina reduce its dependence on agriculture ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$). In Brazil, the Vargas government promoted heavy industry ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$), founding the National Steel Company (CSN) in 1941 and laying the groundwork for Petrobras ($\checkmark\, \text{ examples}$). Production of durable goods, textiles, chemicals, and armaments grew. Import shortages from Europe and the US forced Brazil to develop its own national industrial sector. Brazil's education system began to support industrial needs with new technical institutes and training programs ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). This emphasis on technical education met immediate labor needs, setting the groundwork for Brazil's postwar scientific and technological progress. These steps reflected the Vargas administration;s goal for a modern, self-sufficient industrial economy that shaped Brazil's later growth ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$).

Both countries experienced a significant increase in government involvement in the economy, fueled by wartime demands ($\checkmark\, \text{ focus}$). The Argentine government increased its involvement through price and wage controls, export management, and investments in infrastructure and industry ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). The Argentine state became a business state, as Fernando Devoto argues ($\checkmark\, \text{ perspectives}$). The state also expanded its role in labor relations and social welfare. In the early 1940s, Argentina established the National Labor Department to mediate industrial disputes and implement minimum wage standards ($\checkmark\, \text{ example}$). This helped maintain stability and reinforced the government's commitment to balancing growth with social welfare. These policies accelerated diversification and established the government as an architect of national development ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$). In Brazil, the state led the economic planning and industrial growth. Strategic investments and the creation of state-owned companies, like National Motor Factory (FNM) in 1942, boosted domestic production ($\checkmark\, \text{ example}$). Government policies also promoted public-private collaboration, which set the stage for postwar growth and adaptability to global changes ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$).  A key development was the government's introduction of labor laws, minimum wage, and social security programs to protect workers in the industrializing economy ($\checkmark\, \text{ knowledge}$). These measures improved living conditions, reduced inequality, and helped stabilize the workforce, laying a foundation for social inclusion and shared prosperity ($\checkmark\, \text{ analysis}$).

In summary, World War II was a turning point for Argentina and Brazil, ending their economic stagnation and sparking growth. The war increased GDP and drove significant changes in employment, industry, and state involvement. Argentina's shift to import substitution created a more diverse economy. In Brazil, wartime opportunities fueled heavy industry, infrastructure upgrades, and technical education, making it a regional leader in industrialization. Greater government involvement promoted innovation, expanded social welfare, and developed new sectors. Despite challenges like inflation and shortages, both countries emerged from the war more modernized and prepared for ongoing development ($\checkmark\, \text{ conclusion)}$.
