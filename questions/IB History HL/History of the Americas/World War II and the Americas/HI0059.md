# HI0059

Examine the extent to which the treatment of people of Japanese descent in the Americas during the Second World War was motivated by security concerns rather than racial prejudice.

**Guidance for responses**

Candidates should evaluate whether the treatment of Japanese communities in the Americas (U.S., Canada, and Latin America) during the Second World War was primarily driven by wartime security concerns or by racial prejudice.
- Responses should assess U.S. relocation and confinement policies, Canadian removal and property confiscation, and Latin American measures such as deportations from Peru.
- They should consider wartime context (Pearl Harbor, Pacific theatre) as well as pre-existing discrimination, political pressure, and economic rivalry.
- Strong responses will explore comparisons across regions, highlighting similarities and differences in treatment.
- Conclusions should weigh whether security threats or racial prejudice better explain the policies, acknowledging the complexity of motivations.

**United States:**
- Pearl Harbor created panic over sabotage and invasion.
- Executive Order 9066 (Feb 1942) authorised exclusion zones → 120,000 Japanese Americans relocated.
- Two-thirds were U.S. citizens (Nisei).
- Roberts Commission alleged disloyalty; DeWitt declared “A Jap is a Jap.”
- Munson Report (1941) and later intelligence found no evidence of sabotage.
- Hawaii (with larger Japanese population) did not undergo mass removal → undermines security rationale.
- Racial prejudice and economic jealousy (land and farms seized) central factors.

**Canada:**
- 22,000 Japanese Canadians removed from British Columbia in 1942.
- Homes, boats, and businesses confiscated and sold.
- Mackenzie King justified as precaution, but little evidence of disloyalty.
- Military leaders often opposed mass removal; civilians pushed it.
- Policies reflected both wartime hysteria and entrenched racism in British Columbia.
- Japanese Canadians barred from returning to the West Coast until 1949.

**Latin America:**
- Under U.S. pressure, Peru deported ~1,800 Japanese Peruvians to U.S. camps.
- Others faced surveillance, property confiscation, restrictions on movement.
- Brazil and Mexico restricted Japanese schools, media, and movement.
- Measures motivated partly by hemispheric security coordination, but also by local prejudice and economic rivalry.
- Peru especially influenced by anti-Japanese resentment due to economic competition in trade and agriculture.

**Racial Prejudice and Long-Term Context:**
- Anti-Asian laws and sentiment predated WWII:
    - U.S. Chinese Exclusion Act (1882), Immigration Act (1924).
    - Canadian anti-Asian legislation, denial of suffrage, Alien Land Laws.
- Economic jealousy → white farmers and fishermen benefited from dispossession of Japanese property.
- Propaganda depicted Japanese as “unassimilable,” reinforcing prejudice.

**Legal Dimensions:**
- U.S. Supreme Court upheld relocation in Korematsu v. U.S. (1944), deferring to “military necessity.”
- Evidence of security irrelevance suppressed, later exposed by Peter Irons.
- Canadian government maintained restrictions until after 1945 despite end of war, showing prejudice outlasted security concerns.

**Historiographical Perspectives:**
- Roger Daniels (Prisoners Without Trial): Policies shaped by racial prejudice and economic opportunism, not evidence of threat.
- Greg Robinson (By Order of the President): Roosevelt influenced by political pressures and racial stereotypes.
- David M. Kennedy (Freedom from Fear): Acknowledges security fears but highlights hysteria and lack of evidence.
- Peter Irons (Justice at War): Legal system legitimised racial prejudice under the guise of security.
- Ann Gomer Sunahara (The Politics of Racism): Canadian policies reflected long-term racism in British Columbia rather than genuine wartime necessity.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
The treatment of people of Japanese descent across the Americas during the Second World War has been one of the most controversial aspects of the wartime home front ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). While governments defended their actions as security measures, the scale and nature of relocation, dispossession, and surveillance suggest that racial prejudice played the more decisive role ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). This essay argues that while security fears following Pearl Harbor created the immediate context for such policies, deep-seated racial discrimination in the United States, Canada, and parts of Latin America better explains the extent and severity of the measures ($\checkmark\, \text{organisation}$).

In the United States, the attack on Pearl Harbor in December 1941 triggered widespread fears of sabotage and invasion ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Military officials such as General John L. DeWitt insisted that Japanese Americans could not be trusted, claiming “a Jap is a Jap" ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$).Roosevelt's Executive Order 9066 (Feb. 1942) authorised the removal of around 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry from the West Coast to inland camps, two-thirds of whom were U.S. citizens ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). The justification was national security, reinforced by the Roberts Commission, which claimed—without evidence—that Japanese Americans had aided the Pearl Harbor attack ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). However, intelligence reports such as the Munson Report had already concluded Japanese Americans posed little threat. The lack of similar measures against German or Italian Americans strongly suggests that security concerns alone do not explain the U.S. response ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

Canada implemented similar policies, though on a smaller scale ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Following Pearl Harbor and Japan's entry into the war, Canadian authorities forcibly removed 22,000 Japanese Canadians from coastal British Columbia in 1942 ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Families were relocated inland, their homes, businesses, and fishing boats confiscated and sold by the Custodian of Enemy Property ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Prime Minister Mackenzie King justified this as a necessary wartime precaution, claiming the West Coast was vulnerable to Japanese attack. Yet, as historians such as Ann Sunahara note, there was no evidence of espionage, and many military officials opposed mass removal ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Much like the U.S., the Canadian government treated Japanese Canadians not on the basis of individual loyalty but ancestry, reflecting racial prejudice more than actual security concerns.

In Latin America, U.S. pressure influenced the treatment of Japanese communities ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). In Peru, which had the largest Japanese population in South America, around 1,800 Japanese Peruvians were deported to the United States, where they were placed in camps ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Many others were subject to surveillance, restrictions on movement, and property confiscation ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). These measures were justified as contributions to hemispheric security, as the U.S. sought to prevent Axis influence in Latin America. However, scholars such as Daniel Masterson emphasise that local hostility toward the Japanese community—resentment of their economic success and longstanding racial discrimination—was a critical factor in Peru's compliance ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). In Brazil and Mexico, restrictions were placed on Japanese-language schools, newspapers, and travel, again reflecting suspicion of disloyalty rooted more in prejudice than concrete threats.

The common thread across the Americas was the racialised perception of people of Japanese ancestry ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Anti-Asian discrimination had deep roots in North America: U.S. immigration laws barred Asian immigrants (Immigration Act 1924), while in Canada, Japanese immigrants had long faced exclusion from land ownership and fishing licenses ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$).Economic jealousy also played a role. On the U.S. West Coast, white farmers and businessmen benefited from the removal of Japanese Americans, acquiring land and businesses at cheap prices ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Similarly, in Canada, confiscated fishing boats were redistributed to white competitors ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Roger Daniels argues in Prisoners Without Trial that such economic opportunism, combined with racial prejudice, outweighed genuine security concerns ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

By contrast, where security threats were arguably greatest, governments did not resort to mass relocation ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Hawaii, which had a large Japanese population making up one-third of the territory's residents, experienced martial law and surveillance but no mass relocation ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). U.S. authorities concluded that removing the entire Japanese population would be both impractical and economically disastrous ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). This undermines the argument that security alone explains relocation on the mainland, as Hawaii faced the real threat of Japanese invasion yet avoided mass removal ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

The legal and political justifications for treatment further highlight the dominance of prejudice ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). In the United States, the Supreme Court upheld relocation in Korematsu v. United States (1944), accepting the government's claim of “military necessity” ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Peter Irons later exposed how evidence undermining the security rationale was suppressed, revealing how courts enabled racial prejudice under the cover of security ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). In Canada, Mackenzie King admitted privately in 1944 that Japanese Canadians were not a real threat but still resisted their return to the West Coast until 1949, long after the war had ended ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). These decisions show that prejudice and politics, not genuine military necessity, dictated policy.

In conclusion, the treatment of people of Japanese descent in the Americas during the Second World War was shaped by both security concerns and racial prejudice, but prejudice was the decisive factor ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$). In the United States and Canada, policies of mass removal, confiscation of property, and confinement were implemented without evidence of disloyalty, reflecting long histories of anti-Asian discrimination and economic opportunism ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). In Latin America, U.S. pressure combined with local prejudice to target Japanese communities, again without clear evidence of security threats ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). As Greg Robinson and Roger Daniels have shown, the internment policies across the hemisphere cannot be explained simply as wartime necessity—they reveal how racial prejudice was given legal and political cover under the guise of national security ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
