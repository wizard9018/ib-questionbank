# HI1232

Discuss the reasons for cooperation and/or neutrality in the Second World War in **two** countries of the Americas (excluding the USA).

Any **two** of the following (one example of Cooperation and one of Neutrality):

**Cooperation**
- Brazil: Axis attacks on Brazilian merchant vessels; strategic alliance with the U.S., providing vital naval and air bases; military contribution with the Brazilian Expeditionary Force; Vargas has economic and political motives, such as modernizing Brazil's economy and securing military aid.
- Mexico: German submarine attacks with the sinking of Mexican oil tankers; support for the U.S., closing ports to Axis ships, and supplying raw materials and labor to the Allied war effort; military role with the Escuadrón 201 (Mexican Air Force squadron); geopolitical alignment, strengthening Mexico's relationship with the U.S., and securing economic benefits.

**Neutrality**
- Chile: Governments faced pressure from the U.S. to join the Allies but resisted to maintain autonomy; Chile maintained significant trade with Axis powers, especially Germany and Japan; there was a gradual shift, as Chile broke relations with the Axis in 1943 under U.S. pressure, but only declared war on Japan in April 1945.
- Argentina: Economic interests related to exports were vital to both Britain and Germany; political divisions between nationalist and pro-Axis sympathies within the military and government slowed alignment with the Allies; sovereignty concerns led to a view of neutrality as a means of resisting U.S. dominance in Latin America; late entry due to declaration of war in March 1945, just before the conflict ended.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Mexico's entry into World War II was shaped by direct aggression, strategic necessity, and geopolitical opportunity, while Argentina's stance reflected a complex interplay of economic, political, and sovereignty concerns ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). The analysis will therefore examine, in Mexico's case, the impact of German submarine attacks, its subsequent support for the United States, its military role, and its broader geopolitical alignment. In contrast, Argentina's position will be explored through its economic interests, internal political divisions, and sovereignty issues, which together explain its delayed entry into the war. This structure highlights how differing national circumstances produced distinct responses to the global conflict ($\checkmark\, \text{organisation}$).

In 1942, Mexico was drawn directly into World War II as a result of Germany's aggression ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). German U-boats sank Mexican oil tankers, including Potrero del Llano and Faja de Oro ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). These attacks violated Mexico's sovereignty and threatened its vital oil exports, leaving the government with little choice but to declare war on the Axis ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). The incidents galvanized public opinion, shifting Mexico from neutrality to active cooperation with the Allies ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). After aligning with the Allies, Mexico closed its ports to Axis ships, severing potential supply routes. It also became a crucial supplier of raw materials, particularly oil, copper, and other strategic resources, to Allied industry ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Additionally, the Bracero Program allowed Mexican laborers to work in U.S. agriculture and on railroads ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$), easing wartime labor shortages and strengthening ties between the two countries ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Though modest in scale, Mexico's deployment of a fighter squadron to the Philippines symbolized its commitment to collective security and provided its military with international experience ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Sarah Pruitt argues that Mexico played a valuable role by supplying vital resources and labor, by deploying Escuadrón 201, by strengthening ties with the U.S., and by contributing to the Allied victory ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$). In contrast, Juan Carlos Álvarez contends that Mexico's involvement was shaped more by U.S. geopolitical security needs than by autonomous strategy, making its military role largely symbolic and dependent on external pressure ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$).

Cooperation strengthened Mexico's relationship with the U.S., its dominant neighbor. By siding with the Allies, Mexico secured economic benefits, including modernization of its armed forces and industrial infrastructure ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Politically, it positioned itself as a responsible actor in hemispheric defense, thereby gaining prestige and leverage in postwar negotiations ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). In sum, Mexico's cooperation was driven by Axis aggression, pragmatic support for the U.S., symbolic military participation, and the pursuit of long-term economic and diplomatic gains ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Although limited compared with that of major powers, its role marked a decisive shift in Latin America's involvement in global conflict ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In contrast to Mexico's cooperation, Argentina remained neutral until very late in the war ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Argentina's exports of beef and grain were essential to Britain and Germany ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Maintaining neutrality enabled Argentina to continue trading with multiple markets, ensuring economic stability during the war ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Historian Julio Mutti notes that the decision to remain neutral also had an economic dimension, as Argentina was a major supplier of meat and raw materials to countries such as the United Kingdom ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$). For example, Britain relied heavily on Argentine beef to feed its population, while Germany sought grain shipments via neutral intermediaries ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Within Argentina, nationalist factions and pro-Axis sympathizers in the military and government resisted aligning with the Allies ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). These groups admired aspects of German and Italian authoritarianism and saw cooperation with the U.S. as compromising national sovereignty. This internal division slowed any decisive move toward joining the Allied cause ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Some historians, such as David Rock, frame Argentina's neutrality as a defense of its independence from U.S. influence ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$). Washington urged Latin American nations to join the Allies, but Argentina resisted, portraying neutrality as a means to assert autonomy in regional politics. This stance reflected broader skepticism of U.S. dominance in hemispheric affairs ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Ultimately, Argentina declared war on Germany and Japan in March 1945, only weeks before the war ended ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). This late move was largely symbolic, intended to secure a place in postwar negotiations and avoid exclusion from the newly formed United Nations. By then, Argentina's neutrality had already marked it as one of the last major holdouts in Latin America ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). In summary, Argentina's neutrality was less about indifference than about striking a balance among economic interests, internal political divisions, and resistance to external pressure ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Its late entry underscored the pragmatic nature of its policy, which sought to protect sovereignty and trade while ultimately aligning with the victorious Allies to secure international legitimacy ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In summary, Mexico cooperated with the Allies primarily due to Axis attacks on its shipping and its strong ties to the United States, which prompted it towards a policy of cooperation with the Allies. In contrast, Argentina remained neutral for much of the war due to domestic politics, economic interests, and a reluctance to fully align with U.S. pressure ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
