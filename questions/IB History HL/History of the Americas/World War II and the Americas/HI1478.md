# HI1478

Evaluate the application and effects of Roosevelt's Good Neighbor policy.
[15 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:

**General Guidance:**

Address the Good Neighbor policy as a shift in U.S. hemispheric strategy, explaining how Roosevelt replaced military intervention and coercive diplomacy with principles of non-intervention, mutual respect, and economic cooperation. Explain “application” by identifying concrete actions through which the policy was implemented, such as treaty revisions, diplomatic negotiations, and responses to regional crises. Explain “effects” as multi-layered outcomes, assessing changes in sovereignty, diplomatic relations, regional perceptions of the United States, and long-term political or economic consequences. The strongest answers will integrate the two examples to evaluate both the successes and limitations of the policy, showing how its ideals were applied in practice and how its impact varied across different national contexts.

**Possible suitable examples include:**
- Cuba
    - Roosevelt applied the Good Neighbor policy by abrogating the Platt Amendment in 1934, removing the legal basis for direct U.S. intervention in Cuban affairs.
    - The cancellation of the amendment increased Cuban sovereignty and improved diplomatic relations after decades of U.S. control over domestic and foreign policy.
    - Cuban leaders used the new diplomatic space to pursue more autonomous political and economic agendas, strengthening national legitimacy.
    - Despite improved relations, economic dependence on U.S. markets persisted, limiting the transformative impact of the policy.
    - The policy reduced overt intervention but did not eliminate U.S. influence, showing both the success and the structural constraints of Roosevelt's approach.
    - Cuba became a symbolic example of the Good Neighbor policy's ability to reshape hemispheric perceptions of the United States.
    - $\underline{\textrm{Counterargument:}}$ Some historians argue that the policy's impact in Cuba was overstated because U.S. economic leverage and informal political influence continued to shape Cuban decision-making.

- Mexico
    - Roosevelt applied the Good Neighbor policy during the 1938 oil expropriation crisis by refusing to use military or coercive pressure despite strong U.S. corporate interests.
    - The U.S. acceptance of Mexico's nationalization of foreign oil companies demonstrated a concrete shift from interventionism to respect for sovereignty.
    - Diplomatic negotiations replaced threats, allowing both governments to reach compensation agreements that preserved stability.
    - Mexico's successful defense of its economic autonomy strengthened regional confidence in the Good Neighbor policy.
    - The crisis improved bilateral relations, reducing historical tensions rooted in earlier U.S. interventions and territorial disputes.
    - The episode enhanced Roosevelt's regional prestige, showing that the policy could withstand economic conflict without reverting to force. Long-term cooperation in trade and security reflected the durable effects of the policy and its contribution to hemispheric solidarity.
    - $\underline{\textrm{Counterargument:}}$ Some scholars note that U.S. acceptance of the expropriation was driven partly by strategic concerns before World War II, suggesting that the policy's principles were not the only factor shaping Washington's response.

- Brazil
    - Roosevelt strengthened diplomatic cooperation with Brazil through high-level visits and cultural diplomacy, showing the Good Neighbor policy's emphasis on partnership rather than intervention.
    - The U.S. supported Brazilian industrial development and trade agreements, illustrating the economic dimension of the policy.
    - Brazil's alignment with the United States during the late 1930s and early 1940s reflected improved hemispheric relations rooted in non-intervention and mutual respect.
    - $\underline{\textrm{Counterargument:}}$ Some historians argue that Brazil's cooperation was driven more by its own strategic interests and Vargas's search for external support than by the intrinsic success of the Good Neighbor policy.

- Nicaragua
    - Roosevelt withdrew U.S. Marines from Nicaragua and ended direct military occupation, demonstrating a clear application of non-intervention principles.
    - The policy encouraged diplomatic engagement rather than force, allowing Nicaragua to regain greater political autonomy after decades of U.S. involvement.
    - Improved relations reduced overt conflict and helped stabilize the region in the short term.
    - $\underline{\textrm{Counterargument:}}$ Critics note that U.S. support for the Somoza regime showed that the Good Neighbor policy did not eliminate indirect influence, limiting its effectiveness in promoting genuine sovereignty.

[15 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:

Roosevelt's Good Neighbor policy marked a major shift in United States hemispheric strategy in the 1930s. This essay examines Cuba and Mexico to assess how the policy was applied and the effects it produced in each case. ($\checkmark$ focus) While both examples demonstrate a clear departure from earlier interventionist practices, they also reveal structural constraints, economic dependencies, and strategic considerations that limited the policy's transformative potential. A balanced assessment requires analyzing both the policy's practical implementation and the varied outcomes it produced across national contexts. ($\checkmark$ organization)

The application of the Good Neighbor policy in Cuba was most evident in the 1934 abrogation of the Platt Amendment. Roosevelt's decision to cancel the amendment marked a concrete commitment to nonintervention and symbolized a break with decades of U.S. control over Cuban affairs. ($\checkmark$ knowledge) The amendment had allowed the United States to intervene in Cuba's domestic politics and foreign relations, creating a political environment in which Cuban sovereignty was severely restricted. Its removal therefore signaled a major shift in hemispheric diplomacy. ($\checkmark$ analysis)

The cancellation of the Platt Amendment increased Cuban autonomy and improved diplomatic relations. Roosevelt withdrew U.S. Marines and replaced coercive measures with treaty-based cooperation, allowing Cuban leaders to pursue more independent political and economic agendas. These actions demonstrated the practical application of the Good Neighbor policy and helped stabilize relations after years of tension. ($\checkmark$ examples) The policy therefore reshaped the diplomatic landscape and reduced overt intervention, even if deeper structural issues persisted.

However, the policy's effects in Cuba were not absolute. Economic dependence on U.S. markets continued to constrain Cuban sovereignty, and informal political influence remained strong. These structural conditions meant that the Good Neighbor policy softened U.S. behavior without fully transforming the underlying power dynamics. Foreign intervention had ended, but economic leverage still shaped Cuban decision-making. ($\checkmark$ analysis) Historians such as Louis Pérez argue that the policy's impact in Cuba was overstated because U.S. influence persisted through economic channels, while others highlight the symbolic importance of ending formal intervention as a meaningful diplomatic achievement. ($\checkmark$ perspectives) Taken together, these interpretations suggest that the policy improved relations but did not eliminate asymmetries.

Mexico offers a second major example of the Good Neighbor policy's application and effects. The 1938 oil expropriation crisis tested Roosevelt's commitment to nonintervention more directly than the Cuban case. When President Lázaro Cárdenas nationalized foreign oil companies, including major U.S. firms, Washington faced intense pressure from business interests to respond forcefully. Roosevelt refused to use military or coercive measures, insisting instead on diplomatic negotiations and respect for Mexican sovereignty. ($\checkmark$ knowledge) This decision demonstrated that the Good Neighbor policy could guide U.S. behavior even when economic interests were threatened.

Roosevelt's approach in Mexico had significant effects. Mexico successfully defended its economic autonomy and strengthened its national legitimacy. Diplomatic negotiations replaced threats, enabling both governments to reach compensation agreements that preserved stability. The crisis improved bilateral relations and reduced historical tensions rooted in earlier U.S. interventions and territorial disputes. ($\checkmark$ examples) The episode enhanced Roosevelt's regional prestige and reinforced the idea that the United States could act as a cooperative partner rather than an imperial power.

Yet, as in Cuba, the Mexican case also reveals limitations. Some scholars argue that Roosevelt's acceptance of the expropriation was influenced not only by Good Neighbor principles but also by strategic concerns before World War II. Maintaining Mexican support and regional stability was essential as global tensions escalated. These interpretations suggest that the policy's ideals were intertwined with pragmatic calculations and that nonintervention was not the sole factor shaping Washington's response. ($\checkmark$ analysis) Historians such as Friedrich Katz emphasize Mexico's agency and the strength of Cárdenas's nationalist project, while others highlight Roosevelt's desire to secure hemispheric unity amid rising global conflict. ($\checkmark$ perspectives)

Comparing Cuba and Mexico reveals important differences in how the Good Neighbor policy was applied and its effects. In Cuba, the policy addressed a long-standing legal framework that had institutionalized intervention. Its application was clear and symbolic, but its effects were constrained by economic dependence. In Mexico, the policy was tested by a major economic conflict. Its application required resisting domestic pressure and accepting a challenge to U.S. corporate interests. The effects were more transformative, strengthening Mexican sovereignty and improving bilateral relations. ($\checkmark$ analysis)

The comparison also shows that the Good Neighbor policy operated differently across historical contexts. In Cuba, the policy reduced overt intervention but did not eliminate informal influence. In Mexico, the policy reinforced national autonomy and improved relations more substantially, demonstrating that nonintervention could succeed even during periods of economic confrontation. These differences are essential to evaluating the policy's overall significance. ($\checkmark$ analysis)

Overall, Roosevelt's Good Neighbor policy marked a significant reorientation of U.S. relations with Latin America. Its application in Cuba and Mexico demonstrated a commitment to nonintervention and diplomatic cooperation. The effects included greater sovereignty, improved relations, and stronger regional confidence. At the same time, economic dependence and strategic considerations limited the policy's transformative potential. ($\checkmark$ conclusion) Therefore, the Good Neighbor policy succeeded in redefining U.S. behavior and improving hemispheric relations, but it did not fully resolve the underlying asymmetries that continued to shape the region.
