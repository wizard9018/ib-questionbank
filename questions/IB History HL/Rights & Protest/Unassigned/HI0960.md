# HI0960

To what extent does Source B support the argument that federal action was necessary to ensure civil rights progress?
[5 marks]

$\\$

| |  | |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
|**Source B** $\hspace{3em}$ | $\hspace{3em}$ | President John F. Kennedy's Radio and Television Report to the American People on Civil Rights, June 11, 1963. |

[Source: Kennedy, J. F. (1963, June 11). *Radio and Television Report to the American People on Civil Rights, June 11, 1963.* Retrieved July 12, 2025 from https://www.jfklibrary.org/archives/other-resources/john-f-kennedy-speeches/civil-rights-radio-and-television-report-19630611. Public domain.]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:
\
Any **two** of the following with supporting evidence and a conclusion. Both support for federal action and other important factors must be addressed in the response.

Points from the Source supporting federal action:
- Persistent inequality despite existing rights: The source details severe racial disparities in education, income, and life expectancy, suggesting local/state efforts had failed.
- Need for federal enforcement: References to military presence (e.g., “without having to be backed up by troops”) imply that only federal intervention could guarantee access to education.
- Civil rights tied to national values: JFK frames civil rights as central to American identity and international credibility (e.g., “we do not ask for whites only” in Vietnam), this suggests national responsibility.
- Failure of voluntary compliance: The fact that demonstrations were still necessary implies that federal law, rather than local goodwill, was essential.
- Moral imperative from the presidency: As the highest executive office, JFK's public stance signals the need for top-down intervention.

Points suggesting other important factors:
- Grassroots activism: The speech acknowledges public demonstrations, suggesting the impact of movements like sit-ins and Freedom Rides.
- Media coverage: While not directly mentioned, JFK's timing aligns with intense media attention on Birmingham and other flashpoints.
- Economic and social change: The rise of a Black middle class and urban migration shifted the political landscape.
- International pressure: Cold War context (referenced in speech) created pressure for the U.S. to address domestic racial injustice for its global image.
- Judicial progress: Earlier court cases like Brown v. Board of Education and Boynton v. Virginia were vital judicial steps outside of federal legislation.
- Local and state variation: Some areas (e.g., in the North) had made more progress through local reforms, suggesting federal action wasn't the sole pathway.
\
[5 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Kennedy cites stark disparities in education, income, and employment, framing these as evidence of enduring racial injustice. His remark that it “ought to be possible... to attend any public institution… without having to be backed up by troops” directly implies that local governments and institutions could not be relied on to uphold equal rights, therefore requiring federal intervention ($\checkmark$). Moreover, Kennedy connects civil rights to America's global reputation, referencing Vietnam and West Berlin to argue that the country cannot advocate freedom abroad while denying it at home. This alignment with national interests further justifies federal responsibility ($\checkmark$).

However, the speech also indirectly acknowledges the role of grassroots movements. The reference to demonstrations in the street reflects the pressure placed on the government by civil rights activists, especially those involved in the Birmingham Campaign and the upcoming Freedom Summer ($\checkmark$).  Additionally, judicial rulings such as Brown v. Board of Education and Boynton v. Virginia had already begun to erode legal segregation before federal civil rights legislation was passed ($\checkmark$).

In conclusion, while the source supports the idea that federal action was vital to achieving civil rights progress, it also implies that activism, court decisions, and international dynamics were important contributing factors. Federal action may have been decisive, but it was not the only force driving change ($\checkmark$).
