# HI0959

To what extent does Source A explain the reasons for the Civil Rights Act of 1964?
[5 marks]

$\\$

| |  | |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
|**Source A** $\hspace{3em}$ | $\hspace{3em}$ |  Radio Coverage of President Lyndon Johnson's Remarks upon Signing the Civil Rights Act of 1964. |

[Source: Johnson, L. B. (1964). *The Civil Rights Act of 1964: A Long Struggle for Freedom.* Retrieved July 13, 2025 from https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/civil-rights-act/multimedia/johnson-signing-remarks.html. Public domain.]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:
\
Any **two** of the following with supporting evidence and a conclusion. Both support from the Source and other factors must be addressed in the response.

Points from the source:
- Moral imperative: Johnson appeals to morality, stating that “morality forbids” the continuation of racial discrimination.
- Constitutional foundation: He cites the U.S. Constitution as a basis for equality; “Our Constitution… forbids it.”
- Legacy of American values: Reference to the Declaration of Independence and belief that “all men are created equal.”
- Historical injustice: Acknowledges that millions are denied rights “not because of their own failures, but because of the colour of their skin.”
- Bipartisan political support: Johnson references the overwhelming support in Congress, showing political will for change.
- Continuity with Kennedy's agenda: The bill was initially proposed by JFK, reinforcing a commitment to civil rights reform.

Points from Own Knowledge:
- Grassroots activism: Civil rights campaigns such as the Birmingham Campaign (1963) and Freedom Summer (1964) increased public support and pressure on politicians.
- Violent backlash and media exposure: Events like the Birmingham church bombing (1963) and police brutality against peaceful protesters drew national attention and sympathy.
- International Cold War pressure: The U.S. faced global criticism for racial inequality, which undermined its image as a defender of democracy.
- March on Washington (1963): Demonstrated mass support for civil rights and influenced legislative priorities.
- Southern resistance to desegregation: Highlighted the need for strong federal legislation to override state inaction or obstruction.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
One clear reason Johnson outlines in the speech is the moral obligation to end racial discrimination. He states, “Morality forbids it,” arguing that denying rights based on skin colour is fundamentally unjust. This moral framing positioned the legislation as not merely political, but a necessary ethical correction ($\checkmark$). He also references the U.S. Constitution as a legal and ideological foundation for the Act, stating that “our Constitution... forbids it.” This demonstrates an appeal to shared national values and legal equality, strengthening the legitimacy of the legislation ($\checkmark$).

However, the speech does not fully acknowledge the role of grassroots activism, which was instrumental in prompting federal action. Events like the Freedom Summer of 1964, which sought to register Black voters in Mississippi, exposed the violence and institutional racism still present in the South ($\checkmark$). The murder of three civil rights workers during that campaign shocked the nation and increased pressure for reform. Additionally, media coverage of brutality, such as that seen in Birmingham in 1963, where police attacked peaceful demonstrators, particularly children, drew national and international condemnation. This helped build public and political support for comprehensive legislation ($\checkmark$).

In conclusion, while Johnson's speech effectively highlights the moral and constitutional justifications for the Act, it underrepresents the critical role of civil rights activism and public outrage in driving the legislation forward. Therefore, the source provides a partial explanation of the reasons behind the Civil Rights Act of 1964 ($\checkmark$).
