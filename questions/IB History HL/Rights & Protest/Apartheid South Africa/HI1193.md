# HI1193

What does Source M suggest about the apartheid system in South Africa?
[2 marks]

$\\$

| |  | |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
|**Source M** $\hspace{3em}$ | $\hspace{3em}$ |  Map of the ten bantustans in South Africa at the end of the apartheid period (~1994), just before they were reincorporated into South Africa. By 1981, Transkei, Bophuthatswana, Venda and Ciskei all had nominal “independence.”|

*Note:* The Bantu Authorities Act of 1951 helped establish these “homelands,” but was built on earlier laws such as the Natives Land Act of 1913 and subsequently modified by later laws such as the Bantu Homelands Citizenship Act (1970) and Bantu Homelands Constitution Act (1971), as well as others which modified their borders and legal rights.

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:
\
Any **two** of the following:
- Large-scale segregation and forced removals, as well as how legislation disrupted the social and economic life of non-white communities.
- The creation of ten bantustans conveys apartheid's territorial strategy, transforming segregation into a system of geographic isolation and control.
- Fragmented and disconnected “homelands” suggest a reinforced marginalization, deliberately dividing Black South African communities to legitimize exclusionary policies.
- The organization of the homelands on the map suggested that smaller areas were reserved for a larger proportion of the population.
\
[2 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
The map suggests that the apartheid system was characterized by large-scale segregation imposed on non-white populations. This is shown by the relatively large reserves at the periphery of the national territory, which were reserved for black ethnic groups in South Africa, such as the Venda, Zulu, and Ndebele peoples ($\checkmark$). The map also suggests that the apartheid system created these “homelands” to be isolated and fragmented, as shown by their scattered and disconnected arrangement ($\checkmark$).
