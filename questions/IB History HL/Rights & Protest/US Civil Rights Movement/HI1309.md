## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:

|Top Markband Descriptors (5-6 marks)|Labels |
| :-- | :-- |
|Includes clear points of comparison| ($\checkmark\, \text{similarity}$) |
|Includes clear points of contrast| ($\checkmark\, \text{difference}$) |
|Provides evidence from the text (SBQ) or own knowledge (SAQ) to validate arguments| ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$) |

$\\$

A combination of the ideas below that meets the following guidelines:
- Both similarities and differences must be addressed. If only one is addressed, award a maximum of **four** marks.
Responses that only describe the content of the source can achieve a maximum of **two** marks.
The strongest answers will identify and develop at least **two** similarities and at least **two** differences, both supported by clear **evidence** from the texts.

For comparison, any of the following similarities:
- Both sources acknowledge violence as a major challenge facing non-violent protesters.
- Both recognise failures of state and local authorities to protect Freedom Riders.
- Both show that the Freedom Rides exposed profound resistance to desegregation in the South.
- Both indicate that non-violent action faced significant risk and opposition.

For contrast, any of the following differences:
- Farmer (Source A) stresses political inaction and federal reluctance; Kennedy (Source C) stresses federal concern and action within legal limits.
- Source A reveals a strategy to force federal enforcement; Source C urges restraint to avoid escalation.
- Source A presents challenges as rooted in systemic Southern segregation laws; Source C frames them as problems of public disorder and local responsibility.
- Source A emphasises non-violent protest as a deliberate confrontation to expose injustice; Source C views protest as something that may “provoke” unrest.
- Source A focuses on activist frustration with federal hesitation; Source C highlights federal efforts and appeals to state officials to act.
\
[6 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Both sources show that non-violent protest faced serious obstacles in the South ($\checkmark\, \text{similarity}$). Source A reveals that despite Supreme Court rulings, “state laws… and local ordinances” continued to mandate segregation ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$), creating a structural challenge for protest. Source C similarly shows conditions that hindered non-violent activism, describing a “situation… of deepest concern” marked by unrest and threats of violence ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$). Both also indicate failures by Southern authorities to protect the Riders ($\checkmark\, \text{similarity}$). Source A highlights that local officials upheld segregationist rules and activists expected “racists of the South to create a crisis” ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$), while Source C calls on state leaders to “prevent any further outbreaks of violence,” implying that they had not done so ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$).

The sources differ in how they characterise the role of the federal government ($\checkmark\, \text{difference}$). Source A stresses federal reluctance, suggesting that Washington's failure to enforce the law was “because of politics” and fear of Southern reprisals ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$), portraying federal inaction as a challenge activists had to overcome. Source C, by contrast, presents the government as engaged and responsible, with the President having “instructed the Justice Department to take all necessary steps” ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$). The sources also differ in interpreting the risks of protest ($\checkmark\, \text{difference}$). Source A frames non-violent action as a strategic necessity designed to compel federal intervention despite expected violence ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$), whereas Source C warns that protest actions might “provoke further outbreaks,” suggesting federal concern about escalation ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$).
