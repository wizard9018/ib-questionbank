# HI0648

To what extent had Mao consolidated his rule in China by 1976?

[15 marks]

---

Possible evidence/examples to argue that Mao **had** consolidated his rule:

 - Land Reforms 

     - The land reforms effectively transformed the rural areas by eliminating the landlords and putting the peasants into communes that were under the control of the CCP.

 - Three-anti and Five-anti campaigns

     - The campaigns were launched after a few years of the founding of the PRC. The campaign aimed to eliminate corruption, bureaucratism, and anyone that was considered to be a state enemy. 

     - The campaign also targeted capitalists by putting them on show trials to confess their crimes.

     - The campaigns weakened the Chinese bourgeois, especially business owners, professionals and intellectuals. 

     - The vacant government positions after the campaign were replaced by new younger party cadres that showed more devotion to Maoist ideology. 

 - Hundred Flowers Campaign and Anti-Rightist movement 

     - Although the Hundred Flowers Campaign encouraged people to criticise the government, the fact that Mao was able to launch the Anti-Rightist movement to purge those that criticized him.

 - Great Leap Forward

     - Officials often made exaggerated statistics on the food and steel production during the Great Leap Forward out of fear of being persecuted. Moreover, during the Lushan Conference in 1959 during the Great Leap Forward, only Peng Dehuai criticized Mao openly while other party officials kept silent or even defended Mao, this suggested that Mao had significant influence despite not being in direct control of the events.

 - Cultural Revolution

     - The Cultural Revolution marked the height of Mao's power. During this period, the cult of Mao reached a semi-divine level. The Red Guards would carry portraits of Mao around when doing parades and public trials. The Little Red Book containing Mao's quotes was also printed and distributed to everyone. Party officials and Red Guards often chanted slogans from the book. 

     - During the Cultural Revolution, political opponents that once challenged Mao such as Peng Dehuai, Li Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping were being attacked by Red Guards ordered by Mao. This left Mao unchallenged at this point.

     - Although Mao's health deteriorated drastically by the early 1970s, officials still feared being purged by Mao. In 1975 Mao was still able to issue orders to rehabilitate purged officials such as Deng Xiaoping back in power. 

Possible evidence/examples to argue that Mao **had not** consolidated his rule:

 - Although Mao was effectively the leader of the CCP and China, he was challenged numerous times by party officials or in campaigns such as the Hundred Flowers Campaign and the Lushan Conference. 

 - During the Great Leap Forward, Mao was not completely aware of and in control of the devastation in the countryside since most officials lied about the statistics. 

 - In the Lushan Conference, Mao was criticized by Peng Dehuai. After the Great Leap Forward, Mao retreated from politics and let Deng and Liu Shaoqi run the country.

 - During the later stages of the Cultural Revolution, Mao did not often involve himself in running the government and the movement; most of them were done by the Gang of Four. Moreover, despite the Red Guards showing loyalty to Mao, there was often infighting between different Red Guard factions to see which faction had stronger loyalty. This suggests that Mao was not in control during the Cultural Revolution. 

 - Mao was nearly assassinated by Lin Biao, which showed that Mao's rule was challenged even by those who had previously supported him.

Further guidance:

 - Some responses may divide their commentary about his consolidation into various themes or perspectives, such as political, ideological, military, social, and economic consolidation, arguing the degree to which Mao controlled one or more of them.

 - The strongest responses are likely to argue that Mao maintained a firm grip on power up until his death in 1976, but that it was far from fully consolidated in every aspect.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:

\

At the time of his death in 1976, Mao had been mostly successful in consolidating his rule, but there were still points of weakness in his control. He faced substantial internal dissent as the country experienced economic hardship and instability due to his policies. Mao's political power fluctuated throughout his rule, reaching its peak during the late 1950s following his ideological and political campaigns, and again during the late 1960s through the Cultural Revolution ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). However, the repercussions of the failures of the Great Leap Forward and internal opposition, particularly in the early 1960s and again in the 1970s as his health declined, indicated that his power was fragile by the end of his life ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$).

Mao was able to successfully consolidate and maintain a strong control over the country through political means and ideological control ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). These were achieved through Mao's political campaigns in the 1950s ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). For example, in the Three-anti and Five-anti movements ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$), Mao purged corrupt officials and bourgeois elements, such as intellectuals and business owners. Mao replaced the purged officials with younger party cadres who were more devoted to his ideology in government positions, which further consolidated Mao's support within the government ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Mao further consolidated his rule in the Hundred Flowers Campaign and Anti-Rightist movement ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Although the Hundred Flowers Campaign initially enabled people to voice their criticism of the government freely, Mao immediately reversed the campaign by launching the Anti-Rightist movement and purging those who once criticised him ($\checkmark knowledge$). These two events showed that even though Mao was initially willing to tolerate criticism, the sudden change suggested that Mao used this event to persecute his political opponents, consolidating his rule ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Moreover, many hesitated to challenge Mao's ideology and decision-making, further solidifying his control in the CCP ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). 

In the late 1950s and early 1960s, Mao's control was greatly tested by the failures of the Great Leap Forward, a campaign for rapid industrialization and collectivization of the Chinese economy ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). The widespread famine and mass deaths caused by the policies of the Great Leap Forward resulted in some temporary loss of power and control for Mao in favor of more practical leaders like Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping, who introduced reforms to help the country recover economically ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Though he was technically still in power, Mao retreated notably from leadership and lost some of his grip on the power he had previously held ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

However, by the early 1970s, Mao's control was increasingly symbolic. After 1968, the Red Guards were disbanded, and many were sent to the countryside as their chaos threatened Party stability ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Authority over the Cultural Revolution was transferred to the People's Liberation Army (PLA), and real power shifted to the Gang of Four, who claimed to act on Mao's behalf ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). As Mao's health declined after 1972, he became increasingly detached from day-to-day governance, relying on proxies who often pursued their own agendas ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Meanwhile, moderate leaders like Zhou Enlai and Deng Xiaoping began to re-emerge, especially after Zhou's efforts to stabilize the economy and Deng's brief rehabilitation in 1973 ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Though Mao remained the figurehead of Chinese Communism, the internal divisions within the Party, lack of coherent policy, and Mao's inability to fully control his successors suggest that his consolidation of power had weakened significantly by the time of his death ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$ $\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). 

In conclusion, Mao successfully consolidated his rule through purges, ideological campaigns, and personal cult-building from the 1950s into the 1960s. However, the failures of the Great Leap Forward and the chaotic legacy of the Cultural Revolution undermined the stability of his rule ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). By the 1970s, Mao remained a powerful symbol, but factionalism, declining health, and reliance on radicals like the Gang of Four meant that his direct control had weakened ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Thus, while Mao had effectively consolidated his rule in earlier years, by 1976 his grip on power was severely limited. ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
