# HI0826

Evaluate the consequences of the Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan in 1989.
[7 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:
\
Any **three** points with supporting explanation:
- Military: The withdrawal marked the end of a decade-long conflict that had cost the USSR over 15,000 troops and substantial military resources. It was widely regarded as a humiliating defeat for a superpower, having failed to subdue a guerrilla insurgency despite superior military technology. The Soviet military's poor performance exposed weaknesses in its logistics, morale, and adaptability, damaging the Red Army's reputation.
- Political (Soviet Union): The failure in Afghanistan undermined Soviet global credibility and confidence in the Communist system. It contributed to internal disillusionment with the leadership of the Communist Party and Mikhail Gorbachev's reforms (Perestroika and Glasnost). The strain of the war, combined with economic problems, helped accelerate the collapse of the USSR in 1991.
- Afghanistan (domestic impact): The withdrawal created a power vacuum, as the Soviet-backed Najibullah government collapsed by 1992. Rival Mujahideen factions turned on each other, leading to a brutal civil war (1989-1996), particularly in Kabul, where competing warlords vied for control.
- Taliban emergence: The chaos and instability enabled the rise of the Taliban, who promised order and Islamic governance and seized power by 1996. The Taliban's emergence was partly enabled by continued foreign support, especially from Pakistan's ISI, and disillusionment with factional infighting.
- Regional impact (South Asia): The war left millions of Afghan refugees in Pakistan and Iran, placing enormous social and economic strain on the region. The Pakistan-Afghanistan border became a hub for arms smuggling, drug trafficking, and radical Islamist activity, destabilising both countries.
- Global consequences (Islamist militancy): The war became a rallying point for global jihadist movements. Many foreign fighters, including Osama bin Laden, were radicalised and gained combat experience. Veterans later participated in conflicts in Bosnia and Chechnya, and eventually formed the core of al-Qaeda, leading to future global terrorism.
- U.S. perception and policy: The Soviet withdrawal was seen in Washington as a Cold War victory, confirming the effectiveness of U.S. covert intervention. However, the U.S. disengaged quickly, failing to invest in post-war reconstruction or governance, which allowed chaos to take root. This short-term success had long-term consequences, as abandoned fighters and arms stockpiles later empowered extremist groups.
\

- Should provide a clear summary judgment that reflects the overall significance and long-term impact of the Soviet withdrawal, linking the evaluated consequences to broader historical or global developments.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Firstly, the Soviet military withdrawal in 1989 marked a significant and costly failure for one of the world's superpowers ($\checkmark$). After nearly a decade of fighting, the USSR had lost over 15,000 soldiers and expended vast military resources, only to withdraw without securing a clear victory. The Soviet forces' inability to defeat a guerrilla resistance movement, despite possessing superior weaponry, exposed deep flaws in their military strategy, including poor troop morale, ineffective logistics, and a lack of adaptability. This withdrawal seriously damaged the credibility of the Red Army and eroded its prestige on the global stage ($\checkmark$).

Secondly, the political repercussions for the Soviet Union were equally severe. The defeat in Afghanistan undermined Soviet influence abroad and intensified domestic dissatisfaction with the Communist regime ($\checkmark$). Public support for continued military engagement eroded, and the war became symbolic of the broader failures of the Soviet system. The conflict also placed significant strain on the Soviet economy, compounding the difficulties facing Mikhail Gorbachev as he attempted to implement reforms such as Perestroika and Glasnost. These combined pressures played a key role in accelerating the decline and eventual collapse of the Soviet Union by 1991 ($\checkmark$).

Thirdly, in Afghanistan itself, the Soviet exit created a dangerous power vacuum. With no strong central government in place, various Mujahideen factions turned on each other, plunging the country into a brutal civil war ($\checkmark$). Amid the chaos, the Taliban emerged as a dominant force, promising to restore order through strict Islamic governance. Their rise to power by 1996 was aided by continued foreign backing, particularly from Pakistan, and widespread disillusionment with the corruption and infighting among rival warlords ($\checkmark$).

In conclusion, the Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan had far-reaching consequences, including military humiliation for the USSR, political instability at home, and long-term regional instability that paved the way for the Taliban's rise, consequences that would shape global geopolitics for decades to come ($\checkmark$).
