# HI0881

Explain how **three** Khmer Rouge ideologies shaped its policies from 1975 to 1979.
[6 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:
\
Any **three** points with supporting explanation:

Agrarian Utopianism:
- The Khmer Rouge believed that Cambodia should return to a mythical, pre-industrial golden age where all citizens were peasants working collectively on the land.
- This vision of a classless, agrarian society led to the forced evacuation of urban populations to the countryside shortly after they seized Phnom Penh in April 1975.
- Cities were considered corrupt and exploitative; people living in them were viewed as tainted by capitalism.
- The regime abolished private property, money, and markets in its effort to eliminate inequality and individualism.

Maoist Communism:
- Inspired by China's Cultural Revolution and Mao's Great Leap Forward, the Khmer Rouge emphasized self-reliance (autarky) and rural collectivism.
- The population was forced into agricultural communes, where food production was centrally planned and strictly monitored.
- Foreign aid and influence were rejected as imperialist and corrupting; Cambodia withdrew from international organizations and expelled most foreign diplomats.
- The push for rapid agricultural transformation led to overwork, starvation, and mass deaths, especially during failed attempts to meet unrealistic rice production quotas.

Anti-Intellectualism:
- The Khmer Rouge saw educated people as a threat to their revolutionary goals, associating knowledge with elitism, Western values, and counterrevolution.
- Teachers, doctors, engineers, and anyone with a university education were singled out for execution or harsh re-education.
- Even speaking a foreign language, wearing glasses, or appearing “bourgeois” could lead to arrest or death.
- This ideological hatred of intellectuals devastated Cambodia's professional class and crippled future development.

Extreme Nationalism and Xenophobia
- The Khmer Rouge promoted a radical form of Cambodian nationalism, claiming to protect the country from foreign contamination, especially from Vietnam.
- Ethnic minorities such as the Cham Muslims, ethnic Vietnamese, and ethnic Chinese were systematically targeted—many were massacred or forcibly assimilated.
- The regime sought to $\text{\textquotedblleft}$purify" Cambodia by eliminating non-Khmer cultural and religious influences, including Islam and Buddhism.
- Tensions with Vietnam, driven by both historical enmity and ideological differences, eventually led to border skirmishes and the Vietnamese invasion in 1979.

Year Zero Ideology
- The Khmer Rouge envisioned 1975 as “Year Zero,” the start of a completely new society unburdened by past traditions, values, or institutions.
- This led to the destruction of libraries, schools, religious buildings, and historical records, in an attempt to erase Cambodia's cultural memory.
- Religion was outlawed, and Buddhist monks were defrocked, forced into labor, or killed.
- Families were broken apart, with children placed in indoctrination centers and taught to report on their parents.

Totalitarian Control and Revolutionary Vigilance
- The Khmer Rouge promoted the idea that class enemies could be found anywhere, even among loyal citizens.
- People were encouraged to spy on neighbours, and dissent—even accidental—was met with immediate arrest or execution.
- The infamous Tuol Sleng (S-21) prison symbolised this paranoia; thousands were tortured and forced to confess to imaginary crimes before being executed.

Millenarianism and Revolutionary Purity
- The regime believed that violence was necessary to purify society and bring about revolutionary transformation.
- Anyone resisting the regime or failing to meet its expectations was labelled a traitor or enemy of the people, justifying mass killings.
- The scale of violence was partly driven by a belief that the old world had to be completely destroyed before a perfect society could be built.
\
[6 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
First, their belief in agrarian utopianism led to the forced evacuation of urban populations. Cities were viewed as corrupt, and the regime aimed to transform all Cambodians into rural peasants working collectively on the land. This vision prompted the mass relocation of people from Phnom Penh and other cities to the countryside, where they were made to work in agricultural communes ($\checkmark$).

Second, the Khmer Rouge drew heavily on Maoist communist principles, particularly the ideas of self-reliance and collectivised agriculture. Inspired by Mao's Great Leap Forward, the regime rejected foreign influence and imposed centrally planned food production. People were forced into communes with strict controls over their labour and output, leading to widespread starvation and suffering due to unrealistic production targets and poor planning ($\checkmark$).

Finally, the regime's Year Zero ideology aimed to wipe out all remnants of Cambodia's past in order to build a new revolutionary society. The Khmer Rouge attempted to erase the country's cultural identity by destroying libraries, religious sites, schools, and historical records. This campaign extended to banning religion, dismantling traditional family structures, and re-educating the population according to revolutionary ideals. Together, these ideological beliefs justified the regime's radical and often violent transformation of Cambodian society ($\checkmark$).
