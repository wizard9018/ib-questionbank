# HI0914

To what extent is Source A useful in explaining the justification for the United States' use of the Domino Theory to guide its foreign policy in Asia during the Cold War?
[5 marks]

| |  | |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
|**Source A** $\hspace{3em}$ | $\hspace{3em}$ |  An excerpt from the final section of “The Communist Manifesto” (1848) by Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, which is often titled $\text{\textquotedblleft}$Position of the Communists in Relation to the Various Existing Opposition Parties." |

[Source: Marx, K. & Engels, F. (1848). *The Communist Manifesto.* Retrieved June 24, 2025 from https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1848/communist-manifesto/. Public Domain.]

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:
\
Any **three** of the following with a conclusion. Both values and limitations should be addressed.

Value of the Source:
- The source clearly expresses the internationalist aims of communism, supporting the idea that revolution would not remain isolated in one country.
- It explains the ideological motivation behind communist support for revolutionary movements globally, an idea central to the Domino Theory, which assumed that if one nation fell to communism, others nearby would follow.
- This aligns with U.S. concerns in Asia (e.g., Vietnam, Korea, Laos, Cambodia), where communism was seen not as a national phenomenon but part of a coordinated global threat.

Limitations of the Source:
- The source is theoretical and ideological, written in 1848, long before the Cold War or Asian decolonisation movements, so it lacks specific reference to the context of 20th-century Asia.
- It does not account for nationalist motivations in Asian communist movements (e.g., Vietnamese resistance to colonialism), which were often locally driven rather than directed by Marxist ideology alone.
- The source reflects Marx and Engels’ original vision, which was later interpreted differently by leaders like Mao or Ho Chi Minh, who adapted Marxism to fit local realities.
\

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Firstly, the source is valuable because it explicitly states that communists “support every revolutionary movement against the existing social and political order of things.” This suggests that communism aims to be international in scope, actively encouraging revolution beyond national borders. This ideological stance supports the premise of the Domino Theory, that if one country in a region became communist, others would likely follow, justifying U.S. involvement in countries like Korea and Vietnam to prevent this spread ($\checkmark$).

Secondly, the phrase “they labour everywhere for the union and agreement of the democratic parties of all countries” reinforces the idea that communists did not see revolutions as isolated events, but as interconnected efforts to dismantle global capitalism. From an American Cold War perspective, this confirmed fears of coordinated subversion, and thus the U.S. believed early intervention in one country could prevent a regional cascade ($\checkmark$).

However, a key limitation of the source is that it was written in 1848, long before the Cold War or the rise of communist movements in Asia. While it outlines the original goals of Marxist ideology, it does not address the specific historical and nationalist motivations that shaped revolutions in places like Vietnam or China. Leaders like Ho Chi Minh combined communist ideals with nationalist aims, seeking independence from colonial powers rather than merely participating in a global proletarian revolution ($\checkmark$).

In conclusion, the source is useful in showing the ideological foundation for the Domino Theory by articulating communism's revolutionary and international aims. However, its usefulness is limited by its lack of reference to Cold War geopolitics and the diverse motivations behind Asian communist movements. Therefore, it helps explain the extent to which U.S. policy was ideologically driven, but not the full complexity of the region's Cold War conflicts ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
