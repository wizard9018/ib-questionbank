## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:
\
Any **two** of the following with explanation and a conclusion:

To promote and preserve a peaceful international order
- Rusk states that U.S. involvement in Southeast Asia was part of a “general policy since World War II” aimed at creating “a decent world community” where nations could live peacefully and leave their neighbours alone.
- Emphasises a moral and idealistic purpose. The U.S. as a global guardian of peace, order, and cooperation.
- Suggests continuity with broader U.S. postwar goals such as the United Nations Charter and containment of aggression.

To defend nations in Southeast Asia from external aggression (“pressures from the North”)
- Identifies the central problem as communist pressure and interference from North Vietnam (“the north”).
- Claims peace could be achieved if northern forces “left their neighbours to the south alone.”
- Portrays the U.S. as defending smaller, independent states (like South Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia) from communist expansion or domination.
- Reflects the Cold War containment mindset: the U.S. must stop aggression wherever it arises to preserve global stability.

To uphold international law and protect U.S. rights
- Justifies military action in the Gulf of Tonkin as necessary because U.S. ships were “under attack… in international waters in which we have a perfect right to be.”
- Frames intervention as a defensive and legal response protecting freedom of navigation and U.S. personnel.
- Emphasises legitimacy and restraint, not aggression, to present U.S. actions as law-abiding within the international system.

To demonstrate U.S. credibility and deter further communist aggression
- Argues that if the U.S. had not responded to attacks, “Hanoi and those who might be standing behind Hanoi” could have misjudged U.S. resolve.
- Warns that a failure to act might make the U.S. seem a “paper tiger.”
- Reinforces the Cold War doctrine of credibility: showing strength to prevent further challenges from communist powers (including China and the USSR).
- Suggests that global perception of U.S. power was as important as the specific regional conflict.

To ensure stability and self-determination for Southeast Asian nations
- Asserts that if interference from the North stopped, “these peoples of that area could have their peace and could have a chance to work out their own lives in their own way.”
- Positions U.S. involvement as supporting independence and local autonomy, not colonial control.
- Reflects Cold War-era belief that American intervention protected freedom and self-rule against totalitarianism.

To prevent the escalation of communist influence (Domino Theory context)
- Though not explicitly stated in the extract, Rusk's reasoning aligns with containment and domino logic: preventing one state's fall to communism to protect others in the region.
- His framing of “pressures from the north” reflects the U.S. belief that Vietnam's security was tied to global Cold War balance.
- Implies that Southeast Asia was a testing ground for U.S. resolve against communism. Failure here could invite further aggression elsewhere.

To align with postwar U.S. moral identity
- Rusk's tone underscores that the U.S. was acting as a benevolent power, seeking peace rather than conquest.
- Portrays involvement as a moral duty, consistent with America's self-image as leader of the free world.
- Reinforces the Cold War narrative that U.S. intervention served universal ideals, not narrow national interests.

[5 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
The source suggests that the United States viewed its role in Southeast Asia as moral and idealistic ($\checkmark$). Rusk describes U.S. policy since World War II as aimed at building “a decent world community” where nations “leave their neighbours alone.” This reflects a belief that the U.S. was acting as a guardian of peace and international order, consistent with its self-image as leader of the “free world” ($\checkmark$).

It also presents the U.S. as defensive and law-abiding, claiming that American ships were “under attack… in international waters in which we have a perfect right to be” ($\checkmark$). The language suggests a sense of restraint and legitimacy, portraying intervention as protection, not aggression ($\checkmark$).

Overall, the tone is confident, moral, and paternal. The U.S. saw itself not as an aggressor but as a responsible global power, defending peace, legality, and freedom in Southeast Asia during the Cold War ($\checkmark$).
