- Both leaders claim to support peace, presenting their nation as fundamentally defensive, claiming to be resisting aggression rather than initiating conflict.
    - Stalin: “As for the Soviet Union, it will continue... to pursue the policy of averting war and maintaining peace.”

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Both Truman and Stalin claim that their nations are acting in the interest of peace. Truman states that U.S. involvement in Korea aims to “prevent a third world war,” ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$). while Stalin asserts that the Soviet Union seeks to “maintain peace” ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$). Each presents their actions as defensive, arguing that they are responding to external aggression rather than initiating conflict ($\checkmark\, \text{similarity}$).

Both leaders also accuse the opposing side of manipulation and dishonesty. Stalin claims that Western governments use “lies to deceive their peoples” ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$). and falsely portray their war efforts as defensive. Similarly, Truman dismisses communist rhetoric as deliberately misleading, stating that in “commie” language, “liberation means conquest” ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$). Each side positions itself as morally just while portraying the other as deceptive and dangerous ($\checkmark\, \text{similarity}$).

However, their portrayals of the causes of the Korean War sharply contrast. Truman blames the Soviet Union, describing a “monstrous conspiracy” ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$) to destroy global freedom. In contrast, Stalin places the blame on capitalist elites in the West who, he claims, seek war for “super-profits.” Where Truman sees communist ideology as the central threat, Stalin sees imperialist greed as the true cause of conflict ($\checkmark\, \text{difference}$).

Finally, their attitudes toward U.S. intervention diverge sharply. Truman justifies military action as a moral duty, warning that “aggression anywhere” ($\checkmark\, \text{evidence}$) threatens global peace. Stalin condemns this involvement, framing it as part of a capitalist agenda that treats war as a profitable venture. While Truman casts the U.S. as a global protector, Stalin portrays it as the primary warmonger ($\checkmark\, \text{difference}$).
