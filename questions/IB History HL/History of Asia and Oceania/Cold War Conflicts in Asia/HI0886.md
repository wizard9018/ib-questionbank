# HI0886

Evaluate the accuracy of Truman's portrayal of communist expansionism in Source B.

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:
\
Any **three** of the following with supporting evidence and a conclusion. Both accuracies and overstatements or inaccuracies must be addressed in the response.

Overstatements and Inaccuracies in Truman's Portrayal
- Truman claims a unified global communist strategy directed by Moscow
    - Quote: “The Communists in the Kremlin are engaged in a monstrous conspiracy to stamp out freedom all over the world.”
    - Historical accuracy:
        - Today, we know that Soviet and Chinese interests were not fully aligned and that Mao and Stalin had strategic disagreements and mutual distrust.
        - Stalin was initially hesitant to support North Korea’s invasion, only giving approval after the U.S. withdrew troops from South Korea and signalled little interest in defending it (Dec 1949–Jan 1950).
        - This shows that the communist bloc was not a single, unified command structure, as Truman suggested.
- Truman claims the Korean War was part of a plan to conquer all of Asia
    - Quote: “The attack on Korea was part of a greater plan for conquering all of Asia.”
    - Historical accuracy:
        - There is no definitive evidence of a master plan for all of Asia being orchestrated by Moscow or Beijing.
        - The Korean War was primarily initiated by Kim Il-Sung, who persistently lobbied Stalin and Mao for support to invade the South. While both leaders eventually gave their support, it was reluctant and calculated, not part of a pre-agreed global strategy.
- Truman quotes a ‘secret intelligence report’ to suggest a broader communist conspiracy
    - Quote: “Java, Indochina, Malaya, India, Tibet, Thailand, Philippines, and Japan are our ultimate targets ... We must unify the people of Asia and crush the United States.”
    - Historical accuracy:
        - This rhetoric reflects Cold War fear and propaganda more than documented strategic planning. While communist movements existed in some of these regions, they were often nationalist in nature and not always controlled by Moscow or Beijing. Modern scholarship shows these movements had local motivations rather than being directed parts of a unified global offensive.

Elements of Accuracy in Truman's Portrayal
- Stalin did approve North Korea's invasion
    - Though not the mastermind Truman claimed, Stalin did ultimately give Kim Il-Sung the go-ahead:
        - This fits with Truman's claim that the Soviet Union backed the aggression. Furthermore, Stalin provided military equipment, advisors, and planning assistance.
        - His involvement remained behind the scenes to avoid provoking U.S. retaliation directly against the USSR.
- China entered the war decisively
    - After the U.S. crossed the 38th parallel, Mao sent over 300,000 troops to support North Korea (late 1950).
        - This demonstrates that the communist bloc did act together to defend North Korea, even if their reasons differed. However, Mao’s intervention was partly to protect China’s borders and demonstrate strength after the revolution, not just to serve Soviet interests.
- Communist ideology did promote global revolution
    - Marxist-Leninist ideology did advocate the spread of revolution.
        - The Soviet Cominform (Communist Information Bureau) aimed to coordinate global communist parties. However, in practice, Soviet and Chinese actions were often constrained by national interests.

Conclusion
- Truman's portrayal contains some truth, communist powers did support North Korea and were ideologically hostile to the U.S.
- However, his depiction of a monolithic, centrally coordinated plan for Asian conquest was exaggerated.
- Modern historical research reveals a more complex picture with local leaders (like Kim) having agency. Furthermore, Stalin and Mao acting cautiously and were not always aligned.
- Truman's framing served a political purpose: to rally public support and present the war as a clear-cut moral struggle, but it oversimplified the geopolitics behind the Korean War.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Truman's portrayal of communist expansionism in Source B is only partially accurate. He describes a coordinated, global communist strategy led from Moscow, claiming, “The Communists in the Kremlin are engaged in a monstrous conspiracy to stamp out freedom all over the world.” However, this view oversimplifies the situation. Soviet and Chinese interests in Korea were not fully aligned ($\checkmark$). Stalin and Mao had serious strategic disagreements and mutual distrust ($\checkmark$).

That said, elements of Truman's interpretation do reflect reality. Stalin did ultimately approve the invasion and supported North Korea with weapons, advisors, and military planning ($\checkmark$). While he stayed behind the scenes to avoid direct confrontation with the U.S., his involvement fits Truman's claim that the USSR backed North Korean aggression ($\checkmark$).

Furthermore, communist ideology did promote global revolution. The Soviet Cominform aimed to coordinate communist movements internationally, and Marxist-Leninist theory supported the spread of revolution ($\checkmark$). However, in practice, Soviet and Chinese decisions were often shaped by national interests rather than a unified master plan ($\checkmark$).

In conclusion, Truman was right to see communism as a threat, but his portrayal of a centrally coordinated campaign to dominate Asia was exaggerated. Historical research reveals a more complex reality involving cautious leadership, ideological rivalry, and local initiative, particularly from Kim Il-Sung ($\checkmark$).
