# HI1246

“Political factors were the most significant cause of 20th-century wars.” With reference to **two** wars, to what extent do you agree with the statement?

Possible suitable examples include:

$\textbf{\underline{First World War}}$:

**Political factors**
- The alliance system with the Triple Alliance vs Triple Entente created blocs, turning local crises into continental war.
- Nationalism, especially in the Balkans, destabilised empires and directly led to the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand.
- Weak diplomatic crisis management during the July Crisis escalated tensions instead of containing them.

**Militarism**
- Arms race between major powers such as  Britain and Germany increased tension and suspicion.
- Fixed war plans like the Schlieffen Plan created pressure for rapid mobilisation and  reduced diplomatic flexibility.
- Military leaders held significant influence over political decision-making in the days following the assassination of the Austrian ArchDuke.

**Economic and imperial rivalry**
- Competition for colonies intensified hostility particularly between Britain, France and Germany.
- Naval rivalry damaged Anglo-German relations.
- Economic competition increased distrust but rarely caused direct confrontation.

**Long-term social and ideological tensions**
- Decline of multi-ethnic empires such as the Ottoman and Austro-Hungarian Empire created instability.
- Nationalist movements challenged declining imperial authority.

**Overall evaluation**
- These factors created a climate of tension but required political decisions and diplomatic failure to result in war.

$\textbf{\underline{Second World War}}$:

**Political factors**
- The Treaty of Versailles created political resentment and instability in Germany that was exploited by Hitler.
- Failure of collective security due to the weakness of the League of Nations, allowed aggression to go unchecked.
- The policies of Appeasement followed by Britain and France emboldened Hitler and made war more likely.
- Totalitarian leadership and expansionist foreign policies of Germany, Italy and Japan drove the conflict.

**Economic factors**
- The Great Depression caused mass unemployment and social unrest particularly in Germany leading to Hitler's ascension to power. In Japan, it led to expansionist policies with the Manchurian crisis.
- Economic hardship undermined democratic governments and increased support for extremist parties.
- Desire for economic self-sufficiency or autarky encouraged territorial expansion by Germany.

**Ideological factors**
- Fascist ideology promoted militarism, expansion and rejection of international cooperation.
- Ideological hostility between fascism, communism and democracy increased global tensions.
- Nazi racial ideology justified aggressive expansion for Lebensraum or living space.

**Military factors**
- Rearmament by Germany, violated the Treaty of Versailles and altered the balance of power.
- Lack of military preparedness in Britain and France encouraged Appeasement.
- Early expansionist successes with Anschluss and the Munich Pact reinforced aggressive foreign policies.

**Social factors**
- Propaganda mobilised public support for war and expansion.

**Overall evaluation**
- These factors explain why aggressive regimes gained support but political failures and decisions shaped partially by economic factors allowed war to occur.

$\textbf{\underline{Chinese Civil War (1927-1949)}}$:

**Political factors**
- Weakness and failures of the Guomindang (GMD) government with corruption and authoritarian rule undermined popular support.
- Reliance on urban elites and landlords limited mass appeal.
- Fundamental ideological and political conflict between Nationalism put forward by the Guomindang (GMD) and Communism supported by the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) over China's future.
- The CCP offered an alternative political vision centred on peasant mobilisation and reform.
- Inability to sustain cooperation after the First United Front (1914-1927) led to renewed conflict.
- Mao Zedong's leadership strengthened CCP unity, discipline and adaptability.
- CCP's effective political mobilisation and use of propaganda contrasted with GMD inefficiency.
CCPs political control over the Red Army ensured loyalty and coherence.

**Social factors**
- Peasant grievances over land ownership and exploitation created widespread unrest and GMDs failure to address social inequality weakened its position.
- CCP land reform policies gained strong rural support.

**Military factors**
- CCP's use of guerrilla warfare offset GMD numerical and technological advantages
- Red Army discipline and treatment of civilians contrasted with GMD brutality.
- Strategic errors by Chiang Kai-shek weakened Nationalist campaigns.

**Economic factors**
- Hyperinflation in Nationalist-controlled areas destroyed savings and living standards.
- Economic mismanagement reduced confidence in the GMD government
- War fatigue and economic hardship pushed civilians towards the CCP

**Foreign intervention**
- The Japanese invasion in 1937 disrupted the civil war and weakened the GMD more than the CCP.
- Soviet support and captured Japanese weapons benefited the CCP after 1945.
- US aid to the GMD was inconsistent and poorly utilised.

**Overall evaluation**
- While social, military and economic factors explain how the CCP won, political leadership and ideology explain why the conflict developed.

$\textbf{\underline{Spanish Civil War (1936-1939)}}$:

**Political factors**
- There was political polarisation and instability as the Second Spanish Republic that came to power in 1931 faced deep divisions between the left and right.
- Frequent government changes and weak coalitions undermined effective governance and radicalisation of politics made compromise increasingly impossible.
- There was a breakdown of democratic institutions and the failure to maintain law and order led to political violence and assassinations.
- The Ideological conflict was reflected in a political struggle between Republicanism, Socialism, Anarchism, and Fascism.
- Fear of communist revolution drove conservative and elite support for the Nationalists
- With the lack of respect for electoral outcomes and the right-wing opposition to the Popular Front victory in 1936, military intervention became seen as a political solution.
- Politicisation of the army and resentment of Republican reforms led Franco and other generals to launch a coup to overthrow the elected government.
- The failure of the coup to secure quick victory escalated into full civil war.

**Social factors**
- Deep class divisions between landowners and landless peasants.
- Anti-clericalism versus Catholic conservatism heightened tensions.
- Social unrest made political compromise more difficult

**Economic factors**
- Unequal land distribution and rural poverty caused long-term grievances.
- The impact of the Great Depression increased unemployment and hardship.
- Economic reforms of the Second Republic provoked resistance from elites.

**Military factors**
- The army's traditional power and autonomy encouraged intervention in politics.
- Army reforms initiated by the Spanish Second Republic deeply alienated the conservative military elite, particularly the "Africanists" serving in Morocco. This  fostered intense anger that led to the military conspiracy and the eventual coup attempt in July 1936.

**Foreign intervention**
- German and Italian support strengthened the Nationalists
- Soviet aid and International Brigades supported the Republicans
- Non-Intervention policy of Britain and France weakened the Republican government

**Overall evaluation**
- Political divisions and the collapse of democratic legitimacy were central to the outbreak of war while social and economic tensions undermined the stability of society, contributing to political divisions and the eventual outbreak of the military coup.

$\textbf{\underline{Falklands War (1982)}}$:

**Political factors**
- Long-standing territorial sovereignty dispute between Britain and Argentina over ownership of the Falkland Islands.
- Argentina viewed the islands as rightfully Argentine territory, the Las Malvinas that was  occupied by Britain.
- The invasion was a direct political attempt to resolve the dispute by force.
- There were domestic political pressures in Argentina as the ruling military junta faced declining legitimacy, economic crisis and public unrest.
- The invasion was used as a nationalist distraction to restore political authority with decision-making being concentrated among military leaders with little civilian oversight.
- Argentine leaders underestimated Britain's willingness to defend the islands with the belief that the UK would negotiate rather than fight, reflecting flawed political judgement.
- Failure of diplomatic efforts after the invasion led directly to war
- British political response under Margaret Thatcher's government viewed defence of the Falklands as essential to national prestige and credibility.
- Political commitment to sovereignty and international law justified military action while domestic political support strengthened resolve to reclaim the islands.

**Economic factors**
- Severe economic decline in Argentina increased pressure on the junta.
- While the Falklands themselves had limited economic value at the time, economic motives acted mainly through domestic political instability.

**Military factors**
- Argentine confidence was based on military control of the islands and Britain's distance.
- However, British naval capability enabled a military response.

**Nationalism and identity**
- Argentine nationalism strongly associated with the claim to the Falklands.
- British public opinion rallied around defence of sovereignty.

**International context**
- Cold War dynamics limited external intervention.
- The US attempted mediation but ultimately supported Britain.
- International law and UN resolutions framed the conflict.

**Overall evaluation**
- While economic and military factors shaped context and outcomes, it was political leadership, sovereignty claims and diplomatic failure that were the most significant causes of war.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
The twentieth century was marked by internal conflicts, driven by a complex interplay of political, social and economic factors. Political causes, particularly ideological divisions and struggles for power, were undeniably central to many wars. This is evident in both the Chinese Civil War (1927-1949) and the Spanish Civil War (1936-1939), where clashes between competing political ideologies played a decisive role ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). However, political conflict was intensified by social and economic grievances, which radicalized populations and undermined political stability  ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$).Therefore, while political factors were the most significant cause for the outbreak of both wars, they were shaped by underlying social and economic tensions  ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$).

In China, political factors were central to both the initial outbreak of civil war in 1927 and the renewed conflict after 1945 ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Following the fall of the Qing dynasty in 1911, China lacked a stable central government, leading to competition among political groups to determine China's future. The Chinese Communist Party (CCP), founded in 1921, promoted a revolutionary Marxist ideology, while the Guomindang (GMD) sought to establish a centralized nationalist state. Although the two parties formed the First United Front in 1923, this alliance was tactical rather than ideological. Its collapse in 1927 directly caused the outbreak of the first phase of the civil war ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Chiang Kai-shek's decision to purge Communists during the Shanghai Massacre in April 1927, in which thousands of CCP members were killed, demonstrated the depth of political hostility and marked the transition from political rivalry to armed conflict. This was a deliberate political decision aimed at preserving GMD authority and directly triggered the outbreak of civil war. This event shows that political decisions and ideological fear were immediate triggers of war ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

The resumption of full-scale civil war after 1945 was also politically driven, and the defeat of Japan played a crucial role ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). The Japanese surrender in August 1945 created a power vacuum in large areas of northern China. Both the CCP and the GMD rushed to occupy territory formerly under Japanese control, particularly Manchuria. Although negotiations, such as the Marshall Mission, attempted to form a coalition government, ideological differences and mutual suspicion made compromise impossible ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). The failure of these talks demonstrated that political rivalry over control of the Chinese state, intensified by Japan's defeat, was the immediate cause of the renewed civil war ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

However, the intensity of political conflict was shaped by social and economic conditions ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). China in the 1920s was overwhelmingly rural, with widespread peasant poverty, high rents and landlord dominance. The GMD's failure to implement meaningful land reform alienated large sections of the rural population, while the CCP's promise of land redistribution made revolutionary politics appealing. The political conflict was shaped by the CCP's social and economic policies in the Jiangxi and Yan'an soviets, which helped politicize large sections of the population. In the Jiangxi Soviet (1931-1934), the CCP implemented radical land reform, confiscating land from landlords and redistributing it to poor peasants. Rent reductions, the abolition of usury and campaigns against landlord oppression directly addressed rural economic grievances. Additionally, the GMD's reliance on urban elites and military force, rather than mass support, weakened its legitimacy and hardened political divisions ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Thus, while political conflict caused the outbreak of war, social and economic inequality intensified polarization and contributed to its inevitability ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

Similarly, political factors were crucial in causing the Spanish Civil War ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). The establishment of the Second Spanish Republic in 1931 fundamentally altered Spain's political structure, ending the monarchy and introducing reforms aimed at secularization, military reduction and regional autonomy ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). These changes polarized Spanish politics, as left-wing parties supported reform while conservatives, monarchists and much of the army viewed the Republic as a threat to traditional values and national unity ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). The Republic introduced political reforms that aimed to reduce the power of traditional institutions, which provoked strong opposition ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Regional autonomy was granted to Catalonia in 1932, with plans for Basque autonomy soon after. While this satisfied regional nationalists, it angered conservatives and the army, who viewed autonomy as a threat to Spanish unity ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). This deepened political polarization and weakened support for the Republic among key conservative institutions ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Political reforms targeting the Church and the army further destabilized Spain. The Republic reduced the Church's influence by secularizing education, legalizing divorce and removing state funding for religious institutions ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). These measures alienated devout Catholics and conservative groups, who increasingly viewed the Republic as anti-Spanish and anti-Christian ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Military reforms, led by Manuel Azaña, reduced the number of officers in the army and forced many into early retirement ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). While intended to modernize the army, these reforms humiliated senior officers and contributed to growing resentment within the military, directly increasing support for a coup ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

Political polarization in Spain was, however, simultaneously driven by deep-seated social and economic problems ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Land ownership in Spain was highly unequal, particularly in the south, where large estates (latifundia) dominated, and landless peasants faced chronic unemployment. The Republic's attempts at land reform were slow and limited, frustrating workers while alarming landowners. The 1932 land reform laws resulted in only 60,000 peasants being resettled by 1934. This failure angered landless workers, particularly in Andalusia, leading to strikes, land seizures and support for anarchist groups such as the CNT. Industrial workers were also frustrated by unemployment and poor conditions, and the perceived ineffectiveness of reform pushed many toward political extremism ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). These grievances intensified class conflict and translated directly into political instability. These social tensions strengthened anarchist and socialist movements on the left and authoritarian responses on the right. As a result, political compromise became increasingly impossible, making violent confrontation more likely. This instability convinced conservative elites and military leaders that peaceful political solutions were no longer possible, leading directly to the military uprising in July 1936 ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In conclusion, political factors were the most significant cause of both the Chinese and Spanish Civil Wars, as ideological conflict and key political decisions triggered the outbreak of violence ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). In China, the breakdown of cooperation between the GMD and the CCP, and Jiang Jieshi's anti-Communist purge, directly initiated the war. In Spain, political polarization following the establishment of the Second Republic and furthered by Republican reforms led directly to military rebellion ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Ideological conflict and struggles for power directly triggered violence; however, these political divisions were fueled by underlying socio-economic inequalities. Without these contributing factors, political tensions alone may not have escalated into full-scale civil war ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Thus, while political factors were the dominant cause of the outbreak of both wars, they were shaped and influenced by broader social and economic conditions ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
