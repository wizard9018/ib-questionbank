# HI1474

To what extent was ideology the main cause of **two** 20th-century wars?[15 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:

 

**General Guidance:**

- Address $\text{\textquotedblleft}$ideology” as a causal factor, explaining how political doctrines, belief systems, and competing visions of state organization shaped motivations for conflict.

- Explain causation as a multi-layered process, showing how ideology interacted with political instability, leadership decisions, economic pressures, and international dynamics.

- The strongest answers will compare the two wars in an integrated way rather than separately and evaluate the relative importance of ideology in both with other factors, reaching a balanced judgment.

**Possible suitable examples include:**

- Russian Civil War (Europe)

    - Ideology could be explored through the clash between Bolshevik revolutionary doctrine and the anti-communist visions of liberals, conservatives, and nationalists.

    - The Bolsheviks framed the war as a defense of the revolution and Marxist state-building, giving ideology a strong mobilizing role; the Whites opposed Bolshevism on ideological grounds of national unity and traditional order.

    - Ideology's role in justifying extreme measures (e.g., the Red Terror, propaganda, political repression) could be weighed.

    - Counterarguments might include non-ideological causes: state collapse, economic breakdown, military disintegration, and wartime exhaustion after 1914.

    - Regional, ethnic, and nationalist movements pursuing goals that were not primarily ideological could complicate the picture, as could contingent triggers such as the Czechoslovak Legion revolt (May 1918).

    - Foreign intervention (Britain, France, Japan, the US) could be assessed as strategically rather than ideologically motivated.

- Korean War (Asia)

    - Ideology could be explored through the confrontation between communism in the North and anti-communist nationalism in the South, with the 1945 division creating two states with mutually exclusive claims to the peninsula.

    - Kim Il Sung's invasion could be linked to ideological goals of revolutionary reunification; Stalin's approval and Soviet equipment, and later Chinese and US intervention, could be used to show ideology shaping international escalation.

    - Propaganda, purges, and mass mobilization on both sides could illustrate the war's ideological framing.

    - Counterarguments might include personal ambition, strategic calculations, and instability along the artificial 38th-parallel border.

    - Acheson's January 1950 defensive-perimeter speech could be considered a non-ideological factor that may have encouraged the invasion.

- Mexican Revolution (Americas)

    - Competing ideological visions of constitutionalism, land reform, and national modernization shaped revolutionary factions' motivations.

    - Zapatista agrarian ideology framed the conflict as a struggle for social justice and for indigenous rights.

    - The collapse of the Porfirian regime could be weighed against ideology as a rival explanation for the outbreak of the conflict.

    - Counterargument: much of the fighting after Díaz's fall was driven by personalist and regional power struggles among revolutionary leaders (Villa, Carranza, Obregón, Zapata) rather than a coherent ideological program, so factional rivalry and caciquismo might be weighed against ideology as competing explanations.

- Algerian War of Independence (Africa)

    - The FLN framed the conflict ideologically as a struggle for national liberation and for anti-colonial self-determination.

    - French counterinsurgency relied on ideological narratives, such as the defense of Algérie française and France's $\text{\textquotedblleft}$civilizing mission," to justify continued control and to resist decolonization.

    - Ideology shaped international support, as the FLN used anti-colonial discourse to secure legitimacy abroad.

    - Counterargument: settler-colonial economic structures such as land dispossession, pied-noir (European settler) control of the best agricultural land, and the socioeconomic exclusion of the Muslim majority could be weighed as non-ideological causes predating any nationalist doctrine.

[15 marks]

 

$\\$

 

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:

 

The extent to which ideology caused twentieth-century wars has been widely debated by historians. This essay examines the Russian Civil War and the Korean War to evaluate whether ideology was the main cause in each case. ($\checkmark$ focus) While both conflicts were shaped by ideological confrontation, they also emerged from structural instability, geopolitical pressures, and leadership decisions. A balanced assessment requires analyzing ideological motivations alongside non-ideological factors to determine their relative significance. ($\checkmark$ organization)

Ideology played a central role in the outbreak of the Russian Civil War. The Bolsheviks sought to consolidate power after the October Revolution by implementing a revolutionary program based on Marxism, class struggle, and the creation of a socialist state. ($\checkmark$ knowledge) Their opponents, known collectively as the Whites, represented a diverse coalition of liberals, conservatives, monarchists, and nationalists who rejected Bolshevik ideology and viewed the new regime as illegitimate. This ideological polarization created a political environment in which compromise became impossible. ($\checkmark$ analysis)

The Bolsheviks framed the conflict as a struggle for the survival of the revolution, using propaganda, class rhetoric, and the promise of social transformation to mobilize support. The Whites, in turn, justified their resistance through ideological appeals to national unity, anti socialism, and the restoration of traditional authority. ($\checkmark$ examples) Ideology therefore provided both sides with a clear narrative that defined enemies, justified violence, and shaped military and political strategies.

However, ideology alone cannot explain the outbreak of the Russian Civil War. The collapse of the Russian Empire during the First World War produced severe economic dislocation, food shortages, military fragmentation, and administrative breakdown. These structural conditions made conflict highly likely regardless of ideological differences. Regional and ethnic groups also pursued autonomy for reasons not primarily ideological, and foreign intervention by Britain, France, Japan, and the United States was motivated more by strategic concerns than ideological commitment. ($\checkmark$ analysis) As historian Orlando Figes argues, the war emerged from a combination of ideological confrontation and the collapse of state authority, while Richard Pipes emphasizes the ideological extremism of the Bolsheviks as the decisive trigger. ($\checkmark$ perspectives) Taken together, these interpretations suggest that ideology was a major cause, but not the sole or dominant one.

In contrast, ideology was the primary cause of the Korean War. After 1945, Korea was divided into two states with fundamentally incompatible ideological systems. The North, under Kim Il Sung, embraced communism, revolutionary nationalism, and alignment with the Soviet Union. The South, under Syngman Rhee, adopted anti communist nationalism, capitalist development, and alignment with the United States. ($\checkmark$ knowledge) Both governments claimed to be the legitimate ruler of the entire peninsula, and both viewed reunification as essential to national destiny.

Kim Il Sung's decision to invade the South in June 1950 was driven by ideological goals of revolutionary reunification and the belief that socialism must triumph in Korea. Soviet and Chinese support was also ideologically motivated, as both states saw the conflict as part of a broader struggle against Western capitalism. The United States intervened to defend the liberal capitalist order and prevent the spread of communism in Asia. ($\checkmark$ examples) Ideology therefore shaped not only the motivations of the Korean leadership but also the internationalization of the conflict.

Although ideology was dominant, non-ideological factors also contributed to the outbreak of the Korean War. Kim Il Sung sought personal prestige and strategic advantage, believing that the South was politically weak and militarily vulnerable. The division of Korea at the 38th parallel was an artificial boundary created hastily in 1945, producing long-term instability. The United States initially signaled limited commitment to defending South Korea, which may have encouraged Kim to act. ($\checkmark$ analysis) Historians such as Bruce Cumings emphasize structural and regional factors, presenting the conflict as a civil war rooted in divisions that predated 1950, while William Stueck situates it within the international confrontation between the superpowers. Cumings's interpretation explains the depth of Korean animosities, but the timing and scale of the June 1950 invasion, which were dependent upon Soviet approval and equipment, are better explained by the ideological confrontation Stueck describes, suggesting ideology remained decisive even if the conflict had civil roots. ($\checkmark$ perspectives)

Comparing the two wars reveals important differences in the role of ideology. In the Russian Civil War, ideology was significant but operated within a context of state collapse, wartime exhaustion, and social fragmentation. Ideology provided motivation and justification, but the structural crisis of 1917 to 1918 made conflict almost inevitable. In the Korean War, ideology was the dominant cause because the conflict emerged directly from the confrontation between communism and capitalism during the early Cold War. The internationalization of the conflict further demonstrates how ideology shaped alliances, interventions, and escalation. ($\checkmark$ analysis)

The comparison also shows that ideology can function differently depending on historical context. In Russia, ideology divided domestic actors and shaped internal conflict. In Korea, ideology structured the entire geopolitical environment, transforming a regional dispute into a global confrontation. ($\checkmark$ analysis) This difference is essential for evaluating the extent to which ideology was the main cause in each case.

Overall, ideology was a major cause in both wars, but its significance varied. In the Russian Civil War, ideology was important but interacted with structural collapse, economic crisis, and wartime conditions. In the Korean War, ideology was the primary cause, shaping motivations, alliances, and the scale of the conflict more decisively than any other factor. ($\checkmark$ conclusion) Therefore, ideology was the main cause to a greater extent in the Korean War than in the Russian Civil War, demonstrating that ideological confrontation can be decisive in some twentieth-century conflicts but not universally dominant.
