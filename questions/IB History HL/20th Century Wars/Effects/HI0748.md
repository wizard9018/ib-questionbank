# HI0748

To what extent does the message of Source A reflect contemporary reactions to the Treaty of Versailles?
[7 marks]

$\\$

| |  | |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
|**Source A** $\hspace{3em}$ | $\hspace{3em}$ |  Speech to Parliament by British Prime Minister David Lloyd George titled “NOT VENGEANCE—DISCOURAGEMENT.” Given 3 July 1919. |

:box[But it is said, $\text{\textquotedblleft}$Are you not punishing Germany for the crime of her rulers?" Well, I am sorry to have to answer this, but I must. If Germany had been committed to this War against the will of her people, I say at once we ought to have taken that into account in the terms of peace. But was that so? . . . The nation approved, the nation applauded; the nation had been taught to approve and to applaud. . . . It was an enthusiasm which was at its highest when German troops were marching through Belgium. Supposing that the German Chancellor had returned from the Peace Conference with a Germanic Peace—Belgium added to the Fatherland, the mines of Briey added to the mineral wealth of the Rhineland, the British Fleet surrendered. . . . the British Dependencies added to the German Colonies, huge indemnities imposed upon France and Great Britain for a war they never provoked. . . .\
\
That was what Germany was after. But suppose these things had happened. . . . The German people as a whole [would have received] these terms with delirious joy. I should have been glad had it been possible to say that this was a war which had been entered into against the will of the German people. But it was not, and therefore it is essential that nations must know, if they enter into unprovoked wars of aggression . . . what may lie in store for them when defeat falls upon their arms. I therefore have no hesitation in challenging anyone, either inside [or] outside this House, to point to a single Clause in this Treaty that is not in accordance with the stern and highest demands of justice and fair play.
]

- Reactions consistent with the Source:
    - Many in Britain supported punishing Germany harshly. Lloyd George had won the 1918 election with the slogan: “Make Germany Pay.”
    - French Prime Minister Georges Clemenceau pushed for a tough treaty, arguing Germany must be weakened to prevent future wars: “Germany must be made to pay for the damage she has done.”
    - U.S. Senator Henry Cabot Lodge, although critical of the League of Nations, supported strong measures against Germany: “The German people must feel the consequences of the war they supported.”
    - Popular British newspapers (e.g., The Times) largely echoed Lloyd George's sentiment that the German people were complicit and must face consequences.

- Reactions inconsistent with the Source:
    - Thought the Treaty too harsh:
        - U.S. President Woodrow Wilson promoted a more lenient and idealistic approach, especially with his Fourteen Points: “Peace without victory.”
        - British economist John Maynard Keynes in The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919), warned that the treaty was too harsh and would lead to economic collapse and future conflict: “The treaty includes no provisions for the economic rehabilitation of Europe.”
        - Germans saw the treaty as a national humiliation. The War Guilt Clause (Article 231) especially enraged them, as they felt they were being unfairly blamed: “A diktat, not a peace.”
        - Some British MPs, intellectuals and media saw the treaty as overly vindictive and short-sighted, risking future instability rather than securing peace.
        - David Lloyd George. Despite defending the Treaty of Versailles in his 1919 speech, Lloyd George later expressed concerns that the treaty was too harsh and might lead to future conflict. “We shall have to revise the Treaty. There will be no peace in Europe if Germany is to be kept permanently crippled... this Treaty, as it stands, will bring war within a generation.”
    - Thought the treaty too lenient
        - Marshal Ferdinand Foch (French military leader) said the treaty was “not a peace. It is an armistice for twenty years,” believing it failed to weaken Germany enough to prevent another war.

        - Winston Churchill (British politician and wartime minister), though he supported harsh terms, he felt the treaty didn't go far enough in breaking German militarism and could lead to future danger.
        - Belgian Government felt betrayed by the treaty, as Belgium received little in the way of reparations or territorial gain, despite suffering early invasion and occupation.
        - Some British media (e.g., the Morning Post) argued the treaty allowed Germany to remain too strong and that a future war was inevitable.
        - Polish nationalists and leaders believed the treaty failed to secure Poland's eastern borders and allowed Germany to remain a future threat to Polish independence.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Source A reflects some contemporary reactions to the Treaty of Versailles. The source presents the treaty as a just and necessary punishment rather than an act of revenge, arguing that “nations must know, if they enter into unprovoked wars of aggression, what may lie in store for them when.” This reflects a widely held view in Britain at the time ($\checkmark\, \text{source}$). Lloyd George himself had won the 1918 election promising to “Make Germany Pay,” and many British citizens saw harsh terms as justified ($\checkmark\, \text{OK}$). Similarly, U.S. Senator Henry Cabot Lodge supported a tough approach, believing the German people needed to face the consequences of the war they had supported ($\checkmark\, \text{OK}$).

However, other significant voices challenged this view. U.S. President Woodrow Wilson, author of the Fourteen Points, promoted a more forgiving peace, warning against imposing humiliating conditions that might breed resentment. His call for a “peace without victory” directly contrasts with the speech's insistence that Germany's national enthusiasm for the war justifies the treaty's severity ($\checkmark\, \text{OK}$). Moreover, Lloyd George himself later contradicted the message of his 1919 speech, warning that “this Treaty, as it stands, will bring war within a generation,” showing his growing concern that the treaty's terms were too harsh and potentially destabilizing ($\checkmark\, \text{OK}$). In addition, some believed the treaty was not strict enough. French military leader Marshal Foch stated that the treaty failed to ensure lasting security by not sufficiently weakening Germany ($\checkmark\, \text{OK}$). Polish leaders also felt the treaty did not do enough to guarantee national safety ($\checkmark\, \text{OK}$).

In conclusion, while Source A reflects the views of those who saw the treaty as fair punishment, especially in Britain, it does not represent the full range of contemporary reactions. There were significant voices calling for both a more lenient and a more severe peace, making the source only partially reflective of the wider international response to the Treaty of Versailles ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
