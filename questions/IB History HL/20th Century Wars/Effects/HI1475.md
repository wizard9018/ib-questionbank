# HI1475

$\text{\textquotedblleft}$Peacemaking after 20th-century wars created more problems than it solved." Discuss with reference to two wars, each chosen from a different region.[15 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:

 

**General Guidance:**

- Strong answers should clearly address the question by evaluating whether peacemaking after the two selected wars created more problems than it solved. 

- Responses should analyse both the successes and limitations of the peace settlements, considering the extent to which they resolved existing problems or gave rise to new ones. Answers that rely on lengthy description of the terms of the peace settlements, rather than evaluating their impact, are unlikely to score well. 

- The strongest answers will maintain a running comparison of the effectiveness of peacemaking following the two selected wars, supporting their arguments with specific and relevant evidence and assessing both the short- and long-term consequences of the settlements. 

- Conclusions will vary depending on the wars selected, but all should arrive at a balanced, well-supported judgement on the extent to which peacemaking created more problems than it solved.

**Possible suitable examples include:**

- **First World War (1914-1918)**

    - Responses should consider the extent to which the Paris Peace Settlement, including the Treaty of Versailles, successfully addressed the political, territorial and economic issues arising from the First World War. Candidates may examine the impact of territorial adjustments, disarmament, reparations and the creation of the League of Nations in promoting peace and stability, while also considering criticisms such as resentment among the defeated powers, unresolved territorial disputes, economic instability and the growth of revisionist movements.

- **Second World War in the Pacific (1941-1945)**

    - Responses should consider the extent to which peacemaking following the Second World War in the Pacific successfully addressed the political, territorial and economic consequences of the conflict in Asia. Candidates may examine the Allied occupation and reconstruction of Japan, constitutional and political reforms, the San Francisco Peace Treaty, the restoration of Japanese sovereignty and the promotion of regional stability, while also considering unresolved territorial disputes, the division of Korea, the Chinese Civil War, the growth of Cold War tensions in Asia and the legacy of Japanese wartime actions.

- **Iran-Iraq War (1980-1988)**

    - Responses should consider the extent to which peacemaking following the Iran-Iraq War resolved the political, territorial and military issues that had fuelled the conflict. Candidates may examine the effectiveness of the United Nations' role, including UN Security Council Resolution 598, in ending the conflict and restoring the pre-war status quo through ceasefire arrangements, the exchange of prisoners of war and the eventual acceptance of existing borders, while also considering the enormous economic costs of the conflict, continued hostility between Iran and Iraq and the wider impact of the war on regional stability. Responses should assess both the immediate and longer-term consequences of peacemaking before reaching a judgement on whether it created more problems than it solved.

- **Indo-Pakistan Wars (1965 and 1971)**

    - Responses should consider the extent to which peacemaking following the Indo-Pakistan Wars of 1965 and 1971 resolved the political, territorial and military issues that had contributed to conflict. Candidates may examine the effectiveness of the Tashkent Declaration (1966) and the Simla Agreement (1972) in restoring diplomatic relations, facilitating the return of territory and prisoners of war and establishing frameworks for the peaceful resolution of disputes, while also considering the continuing dispute over Kashmir, ongoing political and military rivalry, cross-border tensions and the regional consequences of the creation of Bangladesh.

- **Irish War of Independence (1919-1921)**

    - Responses should consider the extent to which peacemaking following the Irish War of Independence resolved the political and constitutional issues that had contributed to the conflict. Candidates may examine the effectiveness of the Anglo-Irish Treaty (1921) in establishing the Irish Free State, granting dominion status and reducing violence, while also considering the impact of partition, the oath of allegiance to the British Crown, the continued status of Northern Ireland and the divisions that led to the Irish Civil War.

[15 marks]

 $\\$

 

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:

 

Peacemaking after major wars is intended to resolve the causes of conflict and establish conditions for long-term stability. However, the success of peace settlements has varied depending on the aims and methods adopted by the victorious powers ($\checkmark$ focus). The contrasting experiences of the First World War and the Second World War in the Pacific demonstrate that peacemaking could either intensify instability or encourage recovery and cooperation ($\checkmark$ organization). The Treaty of Versailles and wider Paris Peace Settlement contributed to resentment and instability in Germany and Europe. In contrast, peacemaking after the Second World War in the Pacific focused more on reconstruction, political reform, and integration, particularly through the transformation of Japan ($\checkmark$ organization). Therefore, while peacemaking after the First World War created significant problems that contributed to future conflict, the experience of the Pacific War demonstrates that settlements could create lasting stability when they addressed the needs of both security and reconstruction ($\checkmark$ focus).\

\

The differing aims of post-war settlements were central to their outcomes, as the restrictive, security-focused approach after the First World War contrasted sharply with the reconstruction and reintegration strategy adopted in the Pacific after 1945 ($\checkmark$ focus). Following the First World War, the Allied powers sought both to prevent future German aggression and to establish a new European order, but many of the measures adopted were perceived as punitive by Germany. The Treaty of Versailles imposed territorial losses, military restrictions, and reparations, while Article 231 placed responsibility for the war on Germany. Although these measures aimed to protect European security, they contributed to resentment among Germans who viewed the settlement as a national humiliation ($\checkmark$ analysis). By contrast, after the Second World War in the Pacific, the Allied occupation of Japan sought to eliminate militarism while creating a stable democratic state. The United States-led occupation introduced political reforms, economic reconstruction, and a new relationship between Japan and the international community ($\checkmark$ knowledge). Therefore, the consequences of peacemaking differed because Versailles attempted to secure peace largely through restriction, whereas the Pacific settlement sought stability through transformation ($\checkmark$ analysis).\

\

The political consequences of peacemaking further demonstrate that settlements based on exclusion and punishment were less effective than those focused on integration and cooperation ($\checkmark$ focus). After the First World War, the creation of the League of Nations represented an attempt to establish a new international system based on collective security. However, its effectiveness was limited by the initial exclusion of defeated nations, the absence of the United States, weaknesses in enforcement, and the reluctance of major powers to act decisively against aggression in the 1930s. Furthermore, dissatisfaction with the settlement contributed to the growth of revisionist movements, particularly the rise of Hitler and the Nazi Party, who promised to overturn Versailles ($\checkmark$ analysis). In contrast, political reform in Japan after 1945 produced greater stability. The 1947 constitution established parliamentary democracy, expanded civil rights, and limited the influence of the military. Unlike Germany after 1919, Japan was gradually reintegrated into the international community and became a major ally of the United States during the Cold War ($\checkmark$ analysis). Thus, while both settlements attempted to prevent future conflict, the political settlement after the Pacific War was more successful because it encouraged acceptance of the new order rather than creating widespread opposition to it ($\checkmark$ perspectives) ($\checkmark$ analysis).\

\

However, neither settlement completely eliminated international tensions, demonstrating that even effective peacemaking could leave unresolved problems ($\checkmark$ focus). The territorial changes following the First World War attempted to apply self-determination but often created new disputes by leaving significant minority populations dissatisfied. Germans living outside Germany, particularly in areas such as the Sudetenland, later became a source of tension exploited by Hitler ($\checkmark$ examples). Similarly, peacemaking in the Pacific left unresolved issues that contributed to later instability in Asia. The division of Korea after the Japanese defeat created competing communist and non-communist states and contributed to the outbreak of the Korean War. In addition, unresolved territorial issues, particularly the Kuril Islands dispute and tensions surrounding Taiwan, demonstrated that the post-war settlement did not eliminate all regional conflicts ($\checkmark$ knowledge). However, whereas unresolved issues after the First World War largely reflected weaknesses within the peace settlement itself, many of the tensions that emerged in East Asia were increasingly shaped by the wider Cold War context ($\checkmark$ analysis).\

\

The economic consequences of peacemaking also reveal important differences between the two settlements ($\checkmark$ focus). The reparations imposed on Germany after the First World War contributed to economic difficulties and became a powerful political issue exploited by opponents of democracy, particularly during the economic crises of the 1920s and 1930s ($\checkmark$ knowledge). Although economic instability was not caused solely by Versailles, the settlement provided a focus for resentment and weakened confidence in the Weimar Republic. By contrast, economic recovery was central to the post-war settlement in Japan. Occupation reforms and economic assistance helped Japan rebuild its industrial economy and develop into a prosperous democratic state ($\checkmark$ perspectives). This signifies that peacemaking was more successful when it created conditions for recovery rather than reinforcing grievances among defeated powers ($\checkmark$ analysis).\

\

The consequences of peacemaking, therefore, depended more on the nature of the settlement imposed and the extent to which it addressed the underlying causes of conflict and created conditions for lasting stability($\checkmark$ focus). The Treaty of Versailles created significant problems because its emphasis on restrictions and punishment contributed to resentment, instability, and revisionist ambitions, leading to the outbreak of the Second World War. In contrast, the settlement after the Pacific War was more successful because it combined demilitarisation with reconstruction and political integration ($\checkmark$ perspectives). Therefore, peacemaking was most likely to create further problems when it sought to manage defeat, as with the Paris peace treaties after the First World War, rather than to establish a sustainable basis for cooperation, as the San Francisco Peace Treaty and the preceding occupation reforms did in the Pacific ($\checkmark$ conclusion).
