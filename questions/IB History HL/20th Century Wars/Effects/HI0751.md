# HI0751

Discuss the impact of one twentieth-century war on the role of women in **two** countries. [15 marks]

Candidates must demonstrate a clear understanding of the question’s demands. In this case, candidates should offer a considered and balanced review of the ways in which women's roles and/or status changed, and the reasons behind those changes, as a result of two twentieth-century wars.

A high-scoring response should:
- Clearly address how women's roles and/or status changed during and after each war. Students could choose to look at both roles and status, just roles or status, or role in one war and status in another.
- Identify and explain the reasons for these changes.
- Evaluate the extent and nature of change, including any limitations or reversals of progress after the wars, showing an understanding of short-term vs. long-term impacts.
- Use a well-structured argument with relevant historical examples and differing perspectives integrated into the answer.

Possible suitable examples include:
- World War I (1914-1918). In countries like Britain and France, women gained employment in munitions factories, public transport, and nursing roles as men went to the front. This challenged traditional gender norms and increased public visibility of women's work. In Britain, their wartime contributions were part of the reason women over 30 gained the vote in 1918.
- World War II (1939-1945). WWII expanded women's roles even further. In the US, women joined the workforce in large numbers (symbolized by “Rosie the Riveter”) and served in auxiliary military roles. Soviet women served as snipers, pilots, and medics. The war elevated women's status temporarily, but most returned to domestic roles post-war, though the experience helped inspire postwar feminist movements.
- Chinese Civil War (1927-1949). Women's roles changed significantly in Communist-held areas. The Chinese Communist Party promoted gender equality in theory, encouraging women to take part in land reform, guerrilla warfare, and party organization. However, traditional roles remained strong, and many reforms were more symbolic than fully transformative until later under Mao.
- Vietnam War (1955-1975). In North Vietnam, women were heavily involved in both military and logistical efforts. The Viet Cong used women as spies, fighters, and transport workers along the Ho Chi Minh Trail. Propaganda celebrated women's contributions, yet their status post-war did not improve substantially, and traditional roles reasserted themselves after reunification.
- Algerian War of Independence (1954-1962). Women played an important role in Algeria's anti-colonial struggle, especially in urban guerrilla warfare, carrying weapons and planting bombs for the FLN. Though praised for their bravery, the post-independence government sidelined them politically, and their legal and social status did not see major improvements.
- Spanish Civil War (1936-1939). On the Republican side, women briefly took up roles in militias and civil society. Feminist groups gained visibility, and some women fought on the front lines. However, after Franco's victory, women's rights were rolled back severely, and their social status became tied once again to motherhood and domesticity under authoritarian rule.
- Russo-Japanese War (1904-1905). This war had little direct impact on women's roles, as it was fought largely by professional male soldiers. While it contributed to political unrest in Russia that eventually led to the 1905 revolution, there was no significant shift in women's status or employment during the war itself.
- Iran-Iraq War (1980-1988). In Iran, women were mobilized in support roles such as nursing, logistics, and civil defense. The war also reinforced the regime's ideological messaging about women's roles within an Islamic framework. While their participation was notable, it did not lead to increased rights or public status; rather, the regime maintained tight control over gender norms.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
The First World War significantly altered the role and status of women in both Europe and the Pacific. These changes were primarily driven by the need to replace male labor in industry, agriculture, and other public sectors during wartime. Although some of these changes were temporary, the war helped shift public perceptions of women's capabilities and led to lasting social and political change, particularly in countries like Britain and Australia ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$).

In Europe, particularly Britain, WWI created urgent demand for labor as millions of men were conscripted. This led to large-scale recruitment of women into roles previously reserved for men. By 1918, over one million British women were employed in munitions factories under the Ministry of Munitions. Known as “munitionettes,” these women performed dangerous work and were vital to the war effort. They also entered jobs in transport, government clerical roles, and agriculture. This unprecedented expansion into the workforce not only challenged traditional gender roles but also began to shift societal attitudes ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Though many of these women were pushed out of jobs after the war, their wartime contributions were widely recognized. For instance, the Representation of the People Act 1918 granted the vote to women over 30 in Britain, partly due to their visible wartime service ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). This marked a significant step forward in women's political status ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In addition to employment, the war in Europe altered women's self-perception. The experience of economic independence and social contribution led many women to question the limitations placed on them pre-war. Women's organizations such as the Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU) temporarily suspended suffrage campaigns during the war, supporting national unity ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). After the war, however, their efforts resumed with renewed strength, and the wartime sacrifices were used as powerful arguments for equality ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). The war thus helped link female citizenship with national service, reinforcing calls for suffrage and social recognition ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In the Pacific, particularly Australia, the war had similar impacts, though on a smaller scale. As Australian men enlisted in large numbers, with over 400,000 serving overseas, women stepped in to fill gaps in the workforce. Women worked in clerical roles, manufacturing, and especially in nursing, with the Australian Army Nursing Service (AANS) playing a key role. Around 2,300 nurses served overseas in Egypt, France, and other war zones ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Though fewer women worked in heavy industry compared to Britain, the visibility of their contributions increased significantly. Women also formed patriotic groups like the Australian Women's National League, which raised funds and supported troops abroad ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). These roles reinforced women's capacity to contribute to national efforts and challenged the domestic limitations traditionally placed on them ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

Additionally, Australia saw political shifts influenced by wartime gender roles. Unlike Britain, Australian women already had the right to vote federally since 1902, but the war further strengthened their participation in civic life. For instance, women actively engaged in public debates over conscription during two national referenda in 1916 and 1917  ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Though conscription was defeated both times, women's participation in public discourse reinforced their political agency. The war also raised awareness of women's capacity for leadership and organization, laying the groundwork for future reforms in areas such as employment and education ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

However, it's important to recognize that in both Europe and Australia, many wartime changes were temporary. After the war, returning soldiers were prioritized for employment, and many women were forced out of their wartime jobs. Governments encouraged a return to traditional domestic roles through propaganda and policy. For example, in Britain, the Restoration of Pre-War Practices Act (1919) required women to leave jobs so men could return. Similarly, in Australia, women's wartime roles were not sustained in the post-war labor force ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Despite these reversals, the long-term impact was evident ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). The war had exposed women's abilities in public life, expanded their social roles, and laid the foundation for further rights movements in the 20th century ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$).

In conclusion, WWI brought significant, if uneven, changes to the role and status of women in both Europe and the Pacific. In Britain, women's expanded workforce roles and political recognition marked a turning point in gender equality. In Australia, women's contributions in nursing, fundraising, and civic engagement strengthened their public visibility and confirmed their political importance. While many gains were rolled back after the war, the conflict helped reshape societal views of gender and laid the groundwork for future progress in women's rights and status  ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
