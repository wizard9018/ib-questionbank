# PS0681

Discuss localisation of function in the brain. 

[22 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$: \

There is significant research evidence to suggest that the human brain displays some localisation of function. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ This means that different parts of the brain are specialised in different functions (e.g., memory). Therefore, if a person were to experience damage to a particular brain area, they may experience difficulties with, or loss of, the corresponding function. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Understanding if certain parts of the brain are localized for specific functions is a valuable area of research with some significant implications. For example, knowledge gained can predict future outcomes and consequences for patients with brain tumours, or for those who have experienced brain damage from an accident.  A great deal of research has suggested that some memory functions may be localized in the hippocampus. To discuss the possibility of localization of memory function in the hippocampus, we can examine Milner's case study of HM (as cited in Scoville & Milner, 1957) and Maquire's (2000) MRI study of London taxi drivers who have extensive experience memorising and navigating the streets of London. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$

One study that supports localisation of function is Milner's case study of HM. To treat ongoing seizures, which may have been the result of a bike accident as a child, HM underwent surgery to remove his hippocampus and surrounding tissue on both sides of his brain. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

After the operation, the seizures improved, but HM showed some problems with his memory. As a result of HM's memory problems, Milner conducted a longitudinal case study with HM. The case study aimed to understand the extent and possible causes of HM's memory impairments. Milner used many different methods (methodological triangulation) to carry out her case study. A detailed case and medical history was obtained. Observations were made of HM and detailed field notes were taken. Milner conducted interviews with HM, his doctors, and his family members. She also conducted cognitive tests with HM and a reverse mirror drawing task where HM was asked to trace an outline of a star, but he could only see the mirrored reflection. He did this once daily over a few days (as cited in Squire, 2009). $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

The results of the case study showed that HM's short-term memory seemed to operate okay, and he could remember things as long as he was continually focused on them. However, he could not form new long-term memories, and if he stopped focusing on something it was forgotten. Furthermore, each time he performed the star task, he had no memory of ever doing it before, but his performance kept improving. Therefore, he showed improvements in the performance of new skills, suggesting that the formation of procedural memory is different from other types of long-term memories. He could improve at new procedural tasks but he could not remember learning those tasks. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

These results showed that HM could not acquire new long-term declarative (explicit) memories, but he could still form new long-term procedural memories (knowing how to do things). This suggests that the hippocampus, which was removed from his brain, is important for the formation and consolidation of long-term explicit memories. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ Thus, the formation and consolidation of new long-term declarative memories may be localized in the hippocampus. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$

The extent to which this study supports the idea that some memory functions are localized in the hippocampus depends on the strengths and limitations of the study. Therefore, it is essential to evaluate the research. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ 

This case study collected rich, detailed data using methodological triangulation. Furthermore, the data gathered from these multiple sources corroborated with each other. This helps add to the credibility of the findings. Furthermore, the study observed HM in his natural setting over an extended period which increases the ecological validity. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

However, several potential limitations need to be considered. Firstly, HM had surrounding brain tissue removed along with his hippocampus. Thus, we cannot be certain that the removal of the surrounding brain tissue has not impacted his memory. The lack of confidence in the findings is also partly because this was a case study, and no independent variable was manipulated. Therefore, no cause-and-effect relationship can be established between the hippocampus and the formation of new long-term declarative memories. Furthermore, the case study was on a single patient; therefore, it may be difficult to generalise these results to a wider population. However, other research studies provide triangulated evidence for the role of the hippocampus in memory, adding further credibility to the findings. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

Maguire's (2000) study further supports the idea that some memory functions may be localised in the hippocampus. The study aimed to investigate changes in the human brain resulting from extensive memorisation and navigation experience. Participants were 16 healthy, right-handed male London taxi drivers chosen due to their extensive memorisation and driving experience. Their MRI scans were compared to those of 50 healthy, right-handed, non-taxi drivers. Magnetic fields and radio waves were used by the MRI scanner, and these were converted into 3D images of brain structure. Hippocampal grey matter volume was measured for each participant. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

The results showed that the taxi drivers had significantly larger posterior hippocampi compared to non-taxi drivers, indicating that the extensive memorisation and navigation of the streets of London may have resulted in neuroplastic changes in the hippocampi. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ This suggests that the hippocampi may play a significant role in memory and spatial navigation, providing further support for the idea that some memory function is localised in the hippocampus. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ 

As with Milner's case study of HM, this study has not established a cause-and-effect relationship between memory and the hippocampus. In this case, it is because the researchers have conducted a quasi-experiment where no variables were manipulated. The participants were already taxi drivers (or not), and participants could not randomly be allocated to conditions. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

The use of MRI in this study can, however, be considered a strength as it means that data was quantitatively and objectively measured, which reduces the possibility of researcher bias. Researcher bias was further reduced by ensuring that the researchers assessing each MRI were blind to whether it belonged to a taxi driver or a control participant.  $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ 

Although both of these studies suggest that the hippocampus may specialise in memory functions, it is important to note that many areas of the brain can perform multiple functions. Furthermore, different brain areas often collaborate and integrate information from diverse brain regions. The human brain is sophisticated, and there is still a great deal that we do not know about it. It is difficult and overly simplistic to definitively attribute a single function to a single area of the brain. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

In conclusion, there appears to be a reasonable body of triangulated evidence (including Milner's Case Study of HM, and Maguire's MRI research) to suggest that one of the localized functions of the hippocampus is that it plays a major role in memory functions. However, suggesting that memory is entirely localised in the hippocampus would be overly simplistic. Most brain functions, including memory, require a coordinated approach from several brain regions.  [F] text  $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$
