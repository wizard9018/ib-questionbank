$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:\
Neuroplasticity describes the brain's ability to reorganize itself by forming new neural connections throughout life. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$  These connections are shaped by the use or disuse of neural networks. Long term potentiation describes the strengthening of synaptic connections through repeated use. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$  As a series of neurons, or network, continues to be activated, the brain eventually dedicates more proteins to extend the dendrites of the postsynaptic neurons to facilitate further communication. With lack of use, dendritic pruning may occur. This describes the reduction of synaptic connections. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$  This natural process allows the brain to remain efficient. Two studies that explore long term potentiation and neural pruning are Maguire and Draganski. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$

The aim of Maguire was to investigate potential brain differences between the generalized population and a specialized profession. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$  She hypothesized that taxi drivers, who must constantly recall spatial navigation memories as they navigate around the city, might have structural differences in parts of the brain related to that function. Maguire used a set of control scans from an MRI database, and recruited 16 right-handed, male taxi drivers from London. All of them had to have passed a knowledge test which grants UK taxi drivers with their permit to operate and had to have been professionally driving for at least 1.5 years. Voxel based morphometry and pixel counting were used to measure the density and volume of the hippocampus respectively. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$  Maguire found that when comparing the control scans with the taxi drivers' scans, the taxi drivers had larger posterior hippocampi, and significantly smaller anterior hippocampi. The size of the posterior hippocampus correlated positively with the number of years the drivers had been working. In other words, this specialized skill and recall seemed to be related to the brain structure of the drivers. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$  Executing these same functions repeatedly led to the long-term potentiation and dendritic branching, which led to an increase in both density and volume of those neural networks. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$  While cause and effect cannot be determined from this study, the correlation between number of years driving and hippocampal volume suggests that experience does play a role in the extent of changes. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Bias was reduced in this study through its single-blind design: the researchers did not know which brain scan belonged to which participant group. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

Draganski explored how learning a new skill might change the structure of the brain. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ 24 volunteers were allocated to either a juggling condition or a non-juggling control group. All participants underwent a pre-test scan to serve as a comparison. Those in the juggling group were asked to master a simple juggling technique and let the researchers know when they had learned it. At this point, the juggling group participants underwent a second, post-test scan. They were then told to stop juggling, and after three months a final scan was taken. MRIs were taken around the same time for the control group. Voxel based morphometry was also used in this study. The researchers found that there were no changes in the density of any region of the brain for the control group. But in the juggling group, the density of the mid-temporal area increased after learning the skill and decreased after ceasing practice of juggling. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ This suggests that long term potentiation occurred in the first part of the experiment, and synaptic pruning in the three months after they stopped juggling. The use of a skill like juggling allowed researchers to observe changes in the brain over time. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$  Unlike Maguire, this is a true experiment and so causation can be inferred. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ There may be some sample bias in Draganski, however - all participants were in their early 20s and so similar rates of plasticity may not be observed in older people. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Finally, there may be some issues with internal validity as the participants were outside of the observation of researchers for most of the study. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

There are many applications for research on neuroplasticity. It has informed therapeutic strategies for stroke patients, who can regain lost functions through targeted exercises that encourage the brain to rewire itself. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Similarly, understanding neuroplasticity has shed light on the potential for recovery in children who have suffered from neglect, suggesting that interventions can lead to improvements in cognitive functions previously thought to be permanently impaired. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ However, it's important to note that many studies on neuroplasticity, including studies like Maguire, are correlational. It is not possible to ethically study the impacts of certain variables of interest using experimental human studies: this includes trauma, socialization, education quality, or access to extracurricular programming. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Because these variables are explored retrospectively or in the context of natural experiments, they do not establish cause-and-effect relationships. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

In conclusion, the study of neuroplasticity through research by Maguire, Draganski, and others provides valuable insights into the brain's remarkable ability to adapt and reorganize itself in response to learning and experience. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ While the correlational nature of much of this research presents challenges in establishing causality, the findings have significant implications for education, rehabilitation, and understanding the biological foundations of human behavior and cognition.
