$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$: \

Researchers have found a correlation between behaviour and the development of certain brain regions. It is argued that our behaviour directly effects our brain and some regions of the brain regions correspond to the demands of our environment. The understanding that our brain regions can change and adapt as per the demands of our environment has opened new ways and helped researchers realise the potential of the brain and how it can be trained effectively. One such phenomenon found by researchers is known as neuroplasticity. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$

Neuroplasticity is defined as a process that explains how the brain changes itself to suit the demands of the environment.$\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ The brain is considered to be plastic in nature, meaning that it is malleable and can change itself depending upon the environment, this is known as brain plasticity.$\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ The brain consists of millions of neurons that pass information to each other, forming neural pathways. These neural pathways get strengthened through dendritic branching, indicating that neurons are firing continuously, leading to a growth in its dendrites.$\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ This leads to structural plasticity or changes in the structure of the brain. Research in this field is often quantitative in nature and is conducted under highly controlled lab conditions with the use of latest scanning technologies such as MRI to understand the changes that are occurring in the brain.$\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Laboratory experiments randomly allocated participants to conditions. An independent variable is then manipulated in order to see the effect on a dependent variable that can be easily measured. Apart from that, the extraneous variables are often controlled to help establish a strong cause and effect relationship.$\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Quasi-experiments, on the other hand, can be utilised when the random allocation of participants to groups is not possible. Two studies investigating neuroplasticity conducted by Draganski (2004) and by Maguire (2000) will be evaluated in this essay.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ 

Draganski (2004) wanted to investigate if learning a skill, such as juggling, would lead to changes in the brain's structure.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ A self-selected sample of 24 young volunteers was divided into two conditions - one who learned juggling and the other was a control group. A baseline MRI test was conducted on all participants, after which the experimental group learnt the juggling routine. A second MRI was done once they had mastered the routine, and a final third one was done after a couple of months when they had stopped practising the routine and could not do it successfully. While the first MRI showed no differences between both the groups, the second MRI showed the jugglers had a significantly larger grey matter in the mid-temporal region as measured through VBM (Voxel-based morphometry) a technique used to assess the differences in the anatomy of the brain. After a period of three months, once the jugglers had stopped practising their routine and were unable to perform it, a  third scan was conducted, showing a decrease in the grey matter in the same parts of the brain. The mid-temporal region of the brain was the part of the brain with the most growth as it was a skill involving visual-motor coordination and it helps in visual memory. There was no change in the grey matter of the control group.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ 

The results from this study can be linked to the concept of Neuroplasticity as the increased grey matter after the second scan could have occurred due to dendritic branching as the jugglers were continuously practising a new skill until they were an expert in it - the effect of environment. There is further evidence of plasticity as it could be seen that the grey matter had decreased once the jugglers had stopped practising and were no longer able to perform it successfully, showing the process of neural pruning.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ One of the key strengths of this study is the use of MRI scans which helped to measure the changes in the grey matter that occurred after each of the stages.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Additionally, the baseline pre-test helped to establish the size of the grey matter already present effectively eliminating it as a confounding variable in the study. However, as the participants were allowed to go home during the period between the stages, there is no control on how much they would have practiced the skill of juggling which could have acted as an extraneous variable effecting the results of the study.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

Maguire et. al (2000) wanted to investigate if the continuous practice of a skill, such as driving, would lead to changes in the brain's structure.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ A sample of 16 right-handed male taxi drivers was used who had a driving experience of minimum of 1.5 years in addition to passing the Knowledge test - a requirement to obtain a taxi licence in London. MRI scanning was used and the participant's grey matter was scanned and later compared to 50 other right-handed males who were not taxi drivers. Technology such as the VBM (Voxel-based morphology) was used which helps to determine the density of the grey matter along with pixel counting which helps to determine the size of the area being measured.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ Results from the VBM showed that the taxi drivers with many years of experience had a higher volume of right posterior hippocampus, an area in the brain associated with spatial memory. This indicates that dendritic branching had occurred as part of the process of neuroplasticity in the brain of the taxi drivers.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ The pixel counting also showed a larger area of posterior hippocampus in the taxi drivers as compared to the non-taxi drivers as a result of passing the knowledge test and driving continuously for the past few years.  $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ The findings above suggest that neuroplasticity was evident due to changes observed in the taxi driver's brain.

One of the strengths of the study is it's a quasi-experiment as the Independent variable (taxi drivers or non-taxi drivers) was naturally occurring.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Additionally, it was a correlational study as VBM results clearly showed that the more years the participants were driving caused changes to the brain structure helping to show a clear link between environment and brain plasticity.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ However, the all male sample is a clear disadvantage as it means the results cannot be generalized to the other half of the population as women may learn spatial skills differently from males. The results from this study can thus be linked to the concept of Neuroplasticity as there is clear indication to the changes in the grey matter of the participant's brain due to the advanced imaging techniques used.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ 

As can be seen from both the research cited above, the usage of advanced scanning techniques such as MRI is essential as it helps scientists to understand the level of changes taking place inside the brain and makes it easier to measure it. Additionally, the use of lab experiments makes it easier to establish the cause and effect relationship between the environmental demands and neuroplasticity in the brain.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ However, one of the limitations of using lab experiments is the data is often a snapshot and does not include the changes that could happen in the brain due to repeated exposure to the environment for an extended period of time.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ One way to overcome this is the use of case studies such as the one by Luby et.al (2003) who conducted a longitudinal research to test the effect of childhood poverty and the amount of grey matter in the hippocampus and amygdala. However, research in this field has far reaching implications as it helps scientists understand the functions of the brain better and understand the value of human environment interactions in a scientifically quantifiable manner.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$  

In conclusion, both Draganski (2004) and Maguire et al. (2000) provide compelling evidence for the concept of neuroplasticity by demonstrating how environmental demands can lead to structural changes in the brain. Draganski's study is particularly strong due to its controlled experimental design and use of baseline MRI scans, though the lack of control over individual practice time is a limitation. Maguire's quasi-experimental approach offers valuable insight into real-world applications of neuroplasticity, with clear links between experience and hippocampal development, though the gender-biased sample limits generalisability. Overall, these studies highlight the brain's remarkable ability to adapt, supporting the growing understanding of its malleability in response to environmental stimulation. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$
