# PS0646

Evaluate one or more research study/ies related to the influence of emotion on cognition.
[22 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\textcolor{#000000}{\textbf{Criterion A: Focus on the question}\ }$

**2 marks** if the issue or problem raised in the question was explained and addressed consistently throughout the response. The question used the command term *evaluate* paired with a *research* question stem, therefore, the response should identify, explain, and weigh the strengths and limitations of one or more studies on emotion's impact on cognition. These responses can also discuss advancements and challenges in completing research on the topic, though this should ideally be linked to the studies presented.

**1 mark** if the question is either simply restated, misinterpreted (though still related to emotion and cognition, *e.g. This essay will evaluate flashbulb memory*), or if the overall response does not address the prompt (*e.g. the candidate writes in their introduction that they will evaluate research, but only discusses how emotion may impact cognition*).

[2 marks]

$\textcolor{#F04438}{\textbf{Criterion B: Knowledge \& understanding}\ }$

**5-6 marks** if relevant \& appropriate concepts and terminology related to emotion and cognition are explained in detail (*e.g. flashbulb memory or other effects of emotion and cognition, the somatic marker hypothesis, special mechanism hypothesis, recall, etc.*). Because the question asks to evaluate studies, marks will also be awarded for criterion B for knowledge of research methods and how they are used in the study context.

**3-4 marks** if the relevant terminology is explained and has minimal errors (*e.g. effects of emotion on cognition are described accurately but additional vocabulary terms $\text{\textendash}$ such as flashbulb memory, the somatic marker hypothesis, etc $\text{\textendash}$ are not correctly applied*), or if the response only shows strong methodological knowledge.

**1-2 marks** if the relevant terminology has errors and/or is limited (*e.g. providing an unsubstantiated effect of emotion on cognition, or conflating emotion and cognition with other concepts such as models of memory or thinking and decision-making*), and if the answer does not demonstrate knowledge and understanding of research methodology.

[6 marks]

$\textcolor{#008c3a}{\textbf{Criterion C: Use of research}}$

**5-6 marks** if one or more relevant studies (*e.g., aim, procedures, results \& conclusion*) are thoroughly explained and used to support the answer or the arguments stated. Studies to be used are but not limited to:
- Neisser and Harsch (1992), on how the accuracy and confidence of flashbulb memories change over time
- Sharot et al (2007), provide biological support for flashbulb memories
- Kulkofsky et al (2011), suggest that culture and emotion interact to produce culturally-distinct trends in flashbulb memories
- Luethi et al (2009), on how cortisol and stress may have different impacts on different types of memory

**3-4 marks** if a relevant study was explained and used to develop arguments but with some errors (*e.g. the aim, procedure, results \& conclusions of the study were not clearly presented*).

**1-2 marks** if limited relevant research was used with superficial links to the arguments (*e.g. the study was not linked to the evaluation points mentioned or one of the multiple studies presented is not related to the topic*).

$\textcolor{#4472C4}{\textbf{Criterion D: Critical thinking}\ }$

**5-6 marks** if the answer showed relevant, consistent and well-developed evaluation points (*e.g. around 2-3 strengths and limitations*) of research on emotion and cognition. While this depends on the studies selected, common trends in research on emotion and cognition include high internal and low ecological validity, varied construct definitions for flashbulb memory, and possible cultural bias.

**3-4 marks** of the evaluation points (*e.g around 1-2 strengths \& limitations*) are present and clearly explained but are not sustained.

**1-2 marks** if the evaluation or discussion points are superficial and mainly descriptive (*e.g the evaluation points were only stated or described but not linked back to the study*)

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$: \
Flashbulb memories are described as particularly vivid, detailed, and accurate recollections of events that are potentially ‘protected' from the process of forgetting. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Initially, theorists proposed a special biological mechanism that suggested that a part of the brain became activated by surprising and emotionally laden events, and this mechanism was what created memories that are sharper than others. There is evidence for related biological processes, as seen in McGaugh and Cahill. Overall, though, the currently favoured explanation for flashbulb memory is best described as importance-driven, suggesting that personal relevance increases the likelihood of strong emotions and, therefore, flashbulb memories. Some studies, such as Neisser and Harsch, say that what best characterizes flashbulb memory is actually high confidence. Both studies present their own strengths and limitations in discussing emotion and cognition. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$

McGaugh and Cahill explored the role of adrenaline and the flight-or-fight response in the creation of flashbulb memories. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ In part one of their study, they randomly allocated participants to two conditions: one saw 12 slides and heard a visceral story about a car accident in which a boy had his limbs severed. The participants heard many details about his trip to the hospital and the work of surgeons who reattached his limbs. In the other condition, participants heard a comparatively dull story about a family who came to visit someone in a hospital and witnessed a safety drill while there. The first condition was designed to create a greater emotional experience, whereas the latter was not. Researchers hypothesized that memory would be greater in the first condition. Sure enough, participants rated the vivid, shocking story as more emotional, and recalled more details from the story and slides than the mundane story condition. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

In a follow-up study, researchers administered propranolol, a beta blocker that blocks the target cells for the hormone adrenaline. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ When the study was repeated as a 2x2 factorial design (one level being the propranolol, the other being the intensity of the story) $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$, the condition which heard the high-emotion story and was administered the beta blocker recalled information at the same rate and accuracy as participants in the mundane story conditions. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

This suggests that adrenaline, a stress hormone that activates the amygdala, plays an important role in creating memories from emotionally arousing events, such as flashbulb memories. Because the amygdala is associated with emotional learning, blocking the activation of this brain region reduces learning to the same rate as ‘safe,' ordinary events. The use of a true experiment allows us to understand this cause-and-effect relationship between adrenaline, amygdala activation, and flashbulb memories. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ The study is also highly replicable, so it's possible to establish the reliability of these findings. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Yet, a tradeoff of this study is that the high control over variables reduces our ability to presume that this is what we would observe in real life, outside of the confines of a lab. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

In the case study by Neisser and Harsch, after the Challenger explosion, around one hundred college students were invited to complete a questionnaire about where they were, what they were doing, and how they found out about the event. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ Two years later they were asked to complete the same surveys again - this makes this a longitudinal, prospective study. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ They were also asked to report their confidence that their memories surrounding the explosion were correct. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ There were large discrepancies between the accuracy of their memories and their confidence: even when they had very different recollections than their first survey, confidence tended to be high. The researchers say this is one thing that distinguishes flashbulb memory $\text{\textendash}$ not high accuracy, but very high confidence in the accuracy of those memories. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

In this study, which is an example of prospective, longitudinal research, the researchers were able to compare their responses to both the original and follow-up survey years later. In other studies that ask participants to share and describe the qualities of potential flashbulb memories, it can be difficult to measure or summarize the extent to which memories are accurate. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Neisser and Harsch is also an example of a naturalistic study. It provides a realistic sense of how memory of a real life, significant event changes over time. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ We can assume decent transferability to other national or international events, such as 9/11 or the Fukushima nuclear incident. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Similarly, there is no way to measure the exact emotional state without relying on self-reporting in Neisser and Harsch. It could be the case that people with the least accurate recall could be those who were least emotional about the event. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

Overall, both studies face their own strengths and limitations in attempting to describe factors that contribute to the formation of flashbulb memories. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$
