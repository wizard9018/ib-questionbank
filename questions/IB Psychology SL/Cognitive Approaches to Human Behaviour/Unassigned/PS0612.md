# PS0612

Discuss ethical considerations related to one or more studies that explore the reliability of cognitive processes.

[22 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\textcolor{#000000}{\textbf{Criterion A: Focus on the question}\ }$

**2 marks** if the issue or problem raised in the question is explained. The question uses the command term discuss; therefore, the response should offer a balanced review considering various factors, arguments, and hypotheses. The response can also explain why it is essential to evaluate ethical considerations that investigate the reliability of cognitive processes.

**1 mark** if the question is not accurately explained, has limited explanation, or is simply restated *(e.g., This essay will evaluate two studies that pose ethical considerations when exploring the reliability of cognitive processing*). No marks will be awarded for this criterion if the answer is not relevant to the question.

[2 marks]

$\textcolor{#F04438}{\textbf{Criterion B: Knowledge \& understanding}\ }$

**5-6 marks** if relevant and appropriate knowledge and terminology are explained in detail without errors and are explicitly linked to the studies in Criterion C (*e.g., protection, rights, undue stress or harm, consent, debrief, anonymity, right to withdraw*).

**3-4 marks** if the relevant terminology is explained and has minimal errors but is lacking in some detail or shows only knowledge about research methods, such as a brief explanation of the topic with little focus on evaluation of study (*e.g., consent is the practice in which individuals agree they will be involved and understand the consequences of being involved: this is vital in psychological practice*).

**1-2 marks** if the relevant terminology has errors and/or is limited or superficial in nature (*e.g., a study is explored, such as Loftus & Pickrell (1995), and the most prominent ethical considerations are not identified*), or f the answer only shows knowledge related to reliability of cognitive processing and does not explore studies that support it. No marks will be awarded for this criterion if no relevant terminology is explained.

**1-2 marks** if limited relevant research is used with insufficient detail, errors, or superficial links to the question (*e.g., the study is not linked to the evaluation points mentioned*). No marks will be awarded for this criterion if the answer does not include any relevant studies.

$\textcolor{#4472C4}{\textbf{Criterion D: Critical thinking}\ }$

At least 2-3 discussion or critical thinking points are explained (*e.g., psychologists need to avoid harm on participants at all costs, therefore implanting false memories or making people question the reliability of their memory may have long-lasting effects that need to be considered carefully, etc.*)

**3-4 marks** if relevant critical thinking points are presented (as above), but they are not always fully developed. They may not be directly linked to the specific study or theory, or they may be limited in number. At least 1-2 discussion or critical thinking points are explained.

**1-2 marks** if the evaluation points are superficial or contain inaccuracies  and mainly descriptive or just stated (*e.g., evaluation points only stated but not explained properly*). No marks will be awarded for this criterion if the answer does not contain relevant critical thinking points, discussion, or evaluation.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:\

A code of ethics is a set of moral principles that governs the field of psychology and helps to maintain high standards of research and integrity for the discipline.$\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$. Carrying out research on humans that could potentially help a vast majority of people has its risks; testing humans can result in the harm of those individuals; and psychological discoveries, although well-meaning, can have negative consequences.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. That is why it is the duty of any practising psychologist for ethical considerations to be at the forefront of any study; before, during, and afterwards. Researchers need to make sure that participants give consent, are kept anonymous, will be free from undue stress or harm, can withdraw at any time, and are fully debriefed about the purpose of the experiment and how the data will be used when the experiment is finished. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$. It is vitally important to adhere to ethical codes, such as those established by the British Psychological Society or the American Psychological Association, to ensure that psychology as a discipline maintains its high standards and protects participants. This is especially important in studies that might lead to someone questioning the reliability of their memory, such as in Loftus $\&$ Pickrell (1995), or studies that cause the psychological stress of reliving trauma, as in Yuille $\&$ Cutshall (1986).$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$When experiences such as these can have long-lasting effects on participants, ethical considerations should be weighed against the benefits of the study.

$\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. 

Yuille $\&$ Cutshall (1986) aimed to study whether leading questions would affect the memory of eyewitnesses to a real crime scene.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. In this experiment, 13 eyewitnesses to a violent robbery were interviewed using two leading questions. Half of the participants were asked if they saw $\text{\textquotedblleft}$

a broken headlight,$\text{\textquotedblright}$

and the other half were asked if they saw $\text{\textquotedblleft}$

the broken headlight$\text{\textquotedblright}$ on the getaway car. Additionally, the same question was asked about the car's $\text{\textquotedblleft}$

yellow panel.$\text{\textquotedblright}$ The getaway car had neither of these features. The participants were also asked to rate their stress on the day of the event. The results of this study demonstrated that participants' corroboration with original accounts given in police reports was between 79$\%$ and 84$\%$. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. Eyewitnesses during this real-life event were therefore highly reliable. We can therefore conclude that emotional response to the event may have caused vividness in memory recall, increasing resistance to distortion.

The fact that recall was accurate and less open to distortion after the event is evidence that the emotions surrounding being witness to this crime were fairly strong , and perhaps indicate the amount of times the participants had thought about it since., $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. This in itself is an indication of the shock, or even trauma, of this experience. Care needs to be taken in studies to ensure that painful memories, or traumatic experiences, are not brought to the surface for participants to relive.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. If it is vital to the study that this happens, then care should be taken in the debrief to make sure the participants are supported after the fact.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. dvice should be given on which agencies, or people of contact, to refer to in moments of stress after the study. Concerningly, participants in Yuille $\&$ Cutshall (1986) were not aware of the true nature of the experiment, to protect the study's aim. In order to ensure this protection, the participants could not have been aware of the true risks or consequences of becoming involved.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. While omitting some details from participants is vital to protect results from demand characteristics, the benefits of doing so should not be at the expense of deception when participants' harm from involvement is a potential risk. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$.

Loftus $\&$ Pickrell (1995) aimed to study whether false autobiographical memories could be implanted in people.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. 3 males and 21 females agreed to take part in the study and, in its initial stages, relatives were contacted and asked to recount childhood memories of the participants. The participants received a questionnaire in the mail and were asked to write about 4 childhood memories: 3 real ones and 1 fabricated time when they were $\text{\textquotedblleft}$

lost in the mall$\text{\textquotedblright}$

After that, the participants were interviewed 2 times over a period of 4 weeks. They were asked to recall as much as they could about these 4 events and rate their confidence about each memory on a scale of 1-10. The participants were debriefed afterwards and asked whether they identified the false memory. Around 25$\%$ of the participants recalled the false memory, however, they also reported feeling less confident about this memory.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. While there were low levels of people who actually recalled the memory, the effect on those who were open to suggestion could be feelings of shame, or worse, cause them to question their cognitive capabilities.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

In the above study, there is clear deception of the participants: they would not have been told the true aim of the experiment and would not have been properly informed about the procedure beforehand so that the researchers could prevent demand characteristics.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. While the integrity of the data is very important, this study, more so than Yuille $\&$ Cutshall's, had the potential to harm participants.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. Those who did succumb to the false memory might become alarmed about how easily influenced they had been, and may have begun to worry about the lack of robustness of the memories they had from their own childhoods.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ It is a worrying prospect that with a bit of encouragement from those in positions of trust and responsibility, in this case the researchers, our own sense of our past can become distorted. Researchers did note that some of those who recalled $\text{\textquotedblleft}$

being lost in the mall$\text{\textquotedblright}$

may have done so because of a real memory they had or as an extension of an early memory they might have had.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ The propensity for harm to the participants needs to be carefully considered when embarking upon a study of this nature. The conclusions of this study are important to note for fields like counselling, psychology, and law enforcement, so the study is valuable. However, no study should come at the expense of participant safety.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ 

It can be seen through the exploration of both experiments that there exists an ethical dilemma in psychological research: true scientific experimentation is sometimes at odds with ethical treatment of participants.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ However, integrity in the discipline is vital for public trust. If there was no trust in psychology, its practitioners, and the research, then the discipline would become obsolete.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Participant safety and comfort must come first, while weighing how best to preserve reliable data through methodological practices.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$
