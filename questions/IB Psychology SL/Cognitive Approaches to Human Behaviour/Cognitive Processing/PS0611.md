# PS0611

Evaluate one or more models of memory

[22 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\textcolor{#000000}{\textbf{Criterion A: Focus on the question}\ }$

**2 marks** if the issue or problem raised in the question is explained. The question uses the command term discuss. Therefore, the response should offer a balanced review considering various factors, arguments, and hypotheses. The response can also explain why it is essential to evaluate research studies that investigate how models of memory help to demonstrate human memory functionality.  

**1 mark** if the question is not accurately explained, has limited explanation, or is simply restated  (*e.g., This essay will evaluate two studies that explore two different models of memory and how they help to show the structure of human memory*). No marks will be awarded for this criterion if the answer is not relevant to the question.

[2 marks]

$\textcolor{#F04438}{\textbf{Criterion B: Knowledge \& understanding}\ }$

**5-6 marks** if relevant and appropriate knowledge and terminology are explained in detail without errors and are explicitly linked to the studies in Criterion C (*e.g., Multi-Store Model, Working Memory Model, Long-Term Memory, Short-Term Memory, Phonological Loop, Episodic Buffer, Visuospatial Sketchpad*).

**3-4 marks** if the relevant terminology is explained and has minimal errors but is lacking in some detail or shows only knowledge about research methods, such as a brief explanation of the topic with little focus on evaluation of the study (*e.g., in the working memory model, there are three more different stores for short-term memories than there are in the Multi-Store Model, showing why we can multitask*).

**1-2 marks** if the relevant terminology has errors and/or is limited or superficial in nature (*e.g., if there is no recognition of the differences between models that are introduced*), or if the answer only shows knowledge related to memory models and does not explore studies that support it. No marks will be awarded for this criterion if no relevant terminology is explained. 

[6 marks]

$\textcolor{#008c3a}{\textbf{Criterion C: Use of research}}$

**5-6 marks** if one or more relevant studies are explained and used to support the answer or the arguments stated, and a specific link is made between the findings of the study(ies) and teh question. Relevant details, including the aim, procedure, results, and conclusion, from one or more studies, should be included. Studies that could be referred to include, but are not limited to:

- Investigation of the role of the hippocampus on memory formation: Milner (1966)

- Exploration of primacy and recency effects to investigate the role of both long-term and short-term memory: Glanzer $\&$ Cunitz (1966)

- Investigation of the effect that articulatory suppression would have on the short-term memory store: Landry $\&$ Bartling (2011)  

**3-4 marks** if a relevant study is explained and used to develop arguments but with some errors (*e.g., the aim, procedure, results, and conclusion of the study are not presented, or there are some errors with regard to the relevance of the findings or details of the methodology*).

**1-2 marks** if limited relevant research is used with insufficient detail, errors, or superficial links to the question. (*e.g., the study was not linked to the evaluation points mentioned*). No marks will be awarded for this criterion if the answer does not include any relevant studies.

[6 marks]

$\textcolor{#4472C4}{\textbf{Criterion D: Critical thinking}\ }$

**5-6 marks** if the answer shows relevant, consistent, and well-developed critical thinking points. Critical thinking points could include a combination of the following: strengths and limitations of the research methods used in supporting research studies and their conclusions, strengths and limitations of the relevant theories, potential applications of the findings, or an acknowledgement of complexities and alternative arguments. At least 2-3 discussion or critical thinking points are explained (*e.g., the working memory model separates short-term memory into three stores and it can be seen from multitasking exercises; the fact that we can perform two dissimilar tasks at the same time demonstrates that there may in fact be separate stores for different functions*). 

**3-4 marks** if relevant critical thinking points are presented (as above), but they are not always fully developed. They may not be directly linked to the specific study or theory, or they may be limited in number. For example, a response might state that the study is low in internal validity without explaining what this means in reference to the specific study. At least 1-2 discussion or critical thinking points are explained.

**1-2 marks** if the evaluation points are superficial or contain inaccuracies (e.g., evaluation points only stated but not explained properly). No marks will be awarded for this criterion if the answer does not contain relevant critical thinking points, discussion, or evaluation.

[6 marks]

$\textcolor{#999999}{\textbf{Criterion E: Clarity \& organization}\ }$

**2 marks** if the essay has an organised structure, including a clear introduction (which outlines the focus of the essay), body paragraphs (which outline the relevant study or studies as described in Criterion C; followed by evaluation of those studies), and concluding paragraphs or statements (which link back to the question).

**1 mark** if the organisation and clarity are not sustained throughout the answer (*e.g. unfinished response, lack of a clear introduction, lack of body paragraphs, and/or lack of a conclusion; a structure that is not logical or easy to follow; or incomplete description of key terms and studies*). No marks will be awarded for this criterion if the response does not meet any of the standards above.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:\

Human memory allows us to encode, store, and retrieve information from past experiences in a way that helps us to process the present and predict the future. Not only is memory vital for survival in the environment, it dictates how we learn, determines the way we make decisions, and helps to form our understanding of who we are as individuals.$\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$. The structure of our memory systems and the way that information is stored has long been a focus of cognitive psychology. Understanding how memory works is incredibly helpful in fields of clinical psychology, education, and marketing.$\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$. Atkinson $\&$ Shriffin (1986) first introduced the Multi-Store Model (MSM), which posited that both short-term and long-term memory stores were located in different regions of the brain, with rehearsal and recall determining movement between each.$\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$. Later, Baddeley $\&$ Hitch (1974) challenged the notion that short-term memory is a single store, proposing that there are three different area that deal with different stimuli: the Phonological Loop handles auditory information, the Visuospatial Sketchpad is used for visual stimulus and spatial memories, and the Episodic Buffer ties all of the information together in a sequence. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$. While models of memory help us to understand how memory functions, models are hypothetical representations, and their strengths and limitations need to be evaluated before accepting them as precise.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. 

Milner (1966) aimed to study the role of the hippocampus on memory formation. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. In a longitudinal study on a brain-damaged patient, H.M, who had his hippocampus removed to prevent dangerous seizures, the researcher performed observations, psychometric testing, cognitive testing, interviews, and MRI scans to assess the effect that this level of damage to the brain would have on H.M's ability for recall. H.M could not acquire new semantic and episodic memories, suggesting that the hippocampus is responsible for transfer of information from short-term to long-term memory. However, he was able to remember his childhood very well. H.M also had some capacity for procedural memories, showing these are not stored in the same way. Working memories would be remembered after a short time, if rehearsed, but then forgotten after a time.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. As H.M was able to remember his childhood, but could not encode new long-term memories, yet was able to perform procedural tasks, this study allows us to reach the conclusion that short-term and long-term memory stores are distinct systems.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. 

As Milner (1966) was a case study, it is not able to be replicated to strengthen the findings, however, because it was longitudinal in nature, Milner was able to observe H.M's memory functionality and behaviour over time.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$.The study is high in ecological validity, as H.M was being observed in his natural environment and the researcher was able to witness him performing everyday tasks. While the study did rely on the researcher's observations and subjective reports, Milner also used several methods to triangulate the data, providing more credibility to the study's conclusions.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. The Milner study contributed greatly to our understanding of how memory works, helping to give rise to the formation of memory models in psychology, like the Multi-Store Model (MSM), that posits the distinct stores of the short-term memory and long-term memory.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$.

Landry $\&$ Bartling (2011) designed a dual task technique to investigate if articulatory suppression would influence recall of a written list of phonologically dissimilar letters in a series.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. When two tasks command the functionality of the same short-term memory store, interference will affect the efficacy level when encoding memory. 34 psychology undergraduates took part in this independent sample design. The experimental group read a series of numbers while carrying out an articulatory suppression task and the control group did that same task without articulatory suppression. Each trial was scored for the accuracy of recall.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. The mean percent of accurate recall was higher in the control group, supporting the hypothesis that if one area of the short-term memory is overloaded, the phonological loop in this case, the recall performance will be poorer. We can conclude from this experiment that articulatory suppression prevents rehearsal in the phonological loop because of overload, providing evidence for the three separate stores as posited by the Working Memory Model (WMM).$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. 

Memory models are hypothetical representations of how our memory works, making a complex process easy to understand while highlighting structural functionality.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. However, oversimplifying the memory system undermines the importance of the influence that emotions, culture, and individual differences could have on the way that memory works.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. Milner (1966) and Landry & Bartling (2011) have provided evidence to support that different types of memory function as separate entities, with the WMM building complexity around the framework that the MSM provided. Both models provide helpful information that allows us to understand perception, attention, and recall.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. It is clear that there are still gaps in our knowledge, for example, neither model explains why we are able to recall information without much rehearsal, while when we make every attempt to revise and encode knowledge, we still cannot remember it. Further research will help to inform future models of memory and can help to inform the gaps in our knowledge and address the limitations of the models we presently refer to.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$.
