$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:\

Ethical guidelines, which psychologists must adhere to when conducting cognitive research, help ensure the protection of research participants and the wider community. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Cognitive processing relates to mental processes such as memory, thinking, and decision-making. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Some common and important ethical guidelines in psychological research are informed consent, anonymity, confidentiality, right to withdraw, avoiding deception, no undue stress/harm, and debriefing. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ The ethical consideration of avoiding deception can be discussed with reference to Brewer and Treyens (1981), while the importance of informed consent can be discussed with reference to Glanzer and Cunitz (1966). Both studies investigated aspects of cognitive processing.$\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$

Deception can involve lying to a participant, or it can involve not revealing all relevant details (i.e., deception by omission). $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Avoiding deception means that where possible, all details of a study should be truthfully revealed to a participant before the commencement of the study. Deception is sometimes necessary to prevent participants from working out the aim of the study, to avoid demand characteristics. In some instances, if minor deception was not used, researchers would not get a true indication of how mental processes guide behaviour. If the collection of valid data necessitates some deception, such deception should be essential, and minor and should not cause any harm. In terms of cognitive processing, schema theory states that our prior knowledge (stored in the form of schemas) influences our processing, storage, and retrieval of information. Schemas are mental representations of everything in our experience and knowledge. They are stored in our minds and are built from prior experiences, knowledge, and beliefs. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$

Brewer and Treyens (1981) conducted research that aimed to investigate the role of schema in the encoding of memory and retrieval of memory. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ A total of 86 participants were told that they were waiting in an $\text{\textquotedblleft}$office" until the researchers were ready for them to begin the experiment. This was untrue, as waiting in the office was part of the research. The room had multiple objects that are found in an office like a desk and typewriter. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ It also consisted of objects not found in an office, such as a skull and a piece of bark. After this, the participants were called into another room one by one and given a questionnaire to recall the objects they had seen. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$The results of the study found that participants were more likely to remember items that are regular office items like a desk and typewriter. It was also found that participants were more likely to have false memories of items that were not actually in the office setting but were rated high on schema expectancy of an office.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ The study demonstrates how schema influences memory recall, as more participants remembered objects congruent with what their schema expected. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ 

In this study, valid results could not have been obtained if participants were aware of the true aims of the research. Had they known the true aims of the study, they may have intentionally memorised items in the room. Without deception, the researchers would not have had an accurate picture of how schemas influence memory recall. While avoiding deception is important for protecting participants from stress or embarrassment, this study was still ethical, despite the use of deception. The deception was minor and did not cause any harm to the participants. The participants were debriefed after the study, where the deception was revealed. Therefore, this study highlights the relevance of the ethical consideration of avoiding deception when studying cognitive processing. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$

Another important ethical consideration is informed consent. Informed consent should be voluntarily gained from all participants before the study begins. It should be in writing and should be signed by each participant. Parental or guardian consent is also required if a person is under the age of 16-18 (country dependent) or has any mental impairment. Informed consent should clearly outline what participation in the study will involve. It should outline the participants' rights, and how those rights will be protected (*e.g., the right to withdraw*). $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$        

        

Memory is a cognitive process in which we encode, store and retrieve information. The multi-store model of memory (MSM) suggests that memory consists of three memory stores, sensory, short term, and long-term. Each of these stores differed in duration and capacity of memory storage.$\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ 

Research conducted by Glanzer and Cunitz investigated the serial position effect and provided support for short-term and long-term memory being separate storage systems. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ The serial position effect is the tendency to recall the first and the last items on a list better than items in the middle. All the participants were asked to memorise a list of words followed by free recall. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ In condition 1 participants were asked to recall immediately after hearing the words. In condition 2, researchers introduced a delay between the end of the list and the start of recall with a filler task of 30 secs. The purpose of the filler task was to prevent the rehearsal $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ Results of the immediate recall condition showed that participants were better at remembering words at the start of the list, called the primacy effect (because they had moved to long-term memory), and at the end of the list called the recency effect (because they were still in short-term-memory). Results of the delayed recall condition showed that participants were able to recall the words from the start of the list but were no longer able to recall the words from the end of the list. The results showed that there are different memory stores with different memory mechanisms. Due to the filler task, the recency effect was not seen because without rehearsal, 30 seconds exceeded the duration of long-term memory. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ 

To meet ethical guidelines, informed consent would need to be obtained from all participants before this study. Participants were all over the age of 16, therefore, they could provide their own informed consent. The informed consent would need to outline what their participation would involve and that their participation was voluntary. For example, they would need to be made aware that they would undergo a series of simple recall tests. They would also be informed about how their data would be securely stored, and how it would be used. Participants would also need to be guaranteed before they signed the informed consent, that their data would be kept confidential, and that they can withdraw themselves and their results at any time without consequence. Thus, highlighting how informed consent helped to protect participants' rights in this study. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$

The two studies discussed demonstrate the importance of avoiding deception and informed consent when conducting cognitive research into schema theory and memory. These are two of many important ethical guidelines that need to be considered when researching cognitive processing. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ It is worth noting, however,  that the relative importance of different ethical considerations when investigating cognitive processing may vary depending on the context or hypothesis of the study. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$
