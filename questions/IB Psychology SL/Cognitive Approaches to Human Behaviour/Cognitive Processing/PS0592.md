$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:\

Schemas are the mental representation of everything in our experience and knowledge.$\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ Schema stored in our mind are built from prior experiences, knowledge and beliefs.There are three types of schema, namely social schema which is our interpretation of the world and others.$\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ The second is self schema which is our perception of ourselves and last type is called script schema which is a sequence of events to be carried out. All these types constitute what we know as schema theory which is an organisation and categorization of knowledge from the world around us. As a cognitive process, schema theory influences the way we perceive, process, and remember any kind of information. Schema theory can be evaluated with reference to studies conducted by Bartlett (1932) and Brewer and Treyens (1981). $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ 

The strengths of schema theory are that application of schema theory is extensively used in the psychological treatment under Cognitive Behaviour theory (CBT).$\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ This theory is testable in nature as there is enough empirical evidence to support it. It also helps to understand how memory works in our mind and how information is categorized.$\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Schema theory also has some limitations such as it has low construct validity as schema is difficult to operationalize and is a vague concept.$\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ It also has low predictive validity as it is difficult to predict accurately what an individual will recall in certain situations. It is also difficult to explain where and how schemas are formed in the brain.$\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$

The study is conducted by Bartlett (1932) with an aim to study if memory is reconstructive in nature. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$He told all participants a native American legend called ‘The War of the Ghosts'. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$The participants in the study were British and the story was filled with unknown names and concepts for them to hear. All participants were divided in two conditions, one group was asked to use repeated reproduction (condition 1), where participants heard the story and were told to reproduce it after a short time and then to do it again repeatedly.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ The second group was told to use serial reproduction, in which they had to recall the story on their own and repeat it to another person (condition 2). The results of the study found that there was no significant difference in recollection in both the conditions. Participants had shortened the story and omitted details they thought were unimportant according to them. Many details of the story were changed  by some participants to match the British cultural background as a result of cultural schema. For example, canoe was changed to boat.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ 

The study helps to understand how schemas influence memory. It showed that our existing knowledge significantly impacts how we interpret, encode, store, and retrieve information. Bartlett concluded that memory is a reconstructive process which is influenced by our schema.$\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$

The strengths of the study are that participants gave informed consent before the study. There was no mental or physical harm done on the participants. There is high replicability as it is an experiment method.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ While the limitations of the study are that there is low ecological validity as this type of a study may not be the most accurate way to measure recall. There is low construct validity because memory is not operationalized by the researcher and participants may look at it differently.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

The second research is conducted by Brewer and Treyens (1981) with the aim to investigate the role of schema in the encoding of memory and retrieval of memory.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ A total of 86 participants were told to sit in an office setting where the room had multiple objects that are found in an office like a desk and typewriter.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ It also consisted of objects not found in an office, such as a skull and a piece of bark. After this, the participants were called into another room one by one and given a questionnaire to recall the objects they had seen. The questionnaire comprised 131 objects- 61 were in the room, and 70 were not. Out of 86, 30 participants gave the written recall test, 29 did the drawing recall, and 27 did the verbal recognition test. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$The results of the study found that participants were more likely to remember items that are regular office items like desk and typewriter. It was also found that participants were more likely to have false memories of items that were not actually in the office setting but were rated high on schema expectancy of an office.$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ 

The study demonstrates how schema influences memory recall. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$For example, more participants remembered objects congruent with what their schema expected them to find in an office like desk and chair compared to objects that are not usually found in an office like a skull.

The strengths of the study are that informed consent was acquired from all participants before the study. There is evidence of high internal validity as cause and effect can be established between the IV and DV.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ There is high ecological validity as it was a natural office setting. The testability of the research is also high. The limitations are that the study has low population validity as the sample is not representative of the entire population which results in low generalization.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

Both the studies explained above demonstrate how schema theory works.$\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ To add, we can say that schema theory is supported by an ample amount of research and can provide us with an explanation of how we form mental representations of information we learn through experience, and how we categorise this information. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$  However, it is good to note that although the schema theory can explain how new information is categorised through our existing knowledge, it does not account for situations where new information cannot be linked with existing knowledge. This limits the schema theory's ability to explain how new information is organised.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$
