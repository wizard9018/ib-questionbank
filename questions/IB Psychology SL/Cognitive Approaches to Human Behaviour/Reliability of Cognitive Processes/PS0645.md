# PS0645

Discuss one or more research methods used in studies related to the reliability of one or more cognitive processes.
[22 marks]

---

**1 mark** if the question is either simply restated, misinterpreted (though still related to the reliability of one or more cognitive processes, *e.g. This essay will evaluate research related to errors in thinking and decision-making*), or if the overall response does not address the prompt (*e.g. the candidate writes in their introduction that they will discuss research methods, but only discuss cognitive bias*).

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$: \
Understanding the reliability of cognitive processes such as memory requires the use of multiple research methods. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ Two methods - experiments and case studies - present different strengths and limitations that supplement one another as we seek to establish factors that may make memory more or less reliable. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ Studies like Brewer and Treyens use experimental methods to take advantage of random allocation and other controls to improve internal validity but suffer from reduced ecological validity. Case studies, such as Neisser and Harsch, present opportunities to study memory in realistic settings.

Brewer and Treyens sought to understand how schema may play a role in the encoding and recollection of memories related to an office scene. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ Participants were seated in an office without knowing that the experiment had taken place, in order to reduce the effect of participant variability in working memory or effort. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Some objects were ‘schema consistent' with an object, such as desks, a coffee pot, and paper. Some objects, such as tree bark, were inconsistent with the schema of an office and would be unexpected to see in an office. After spending time in the office, participants were invited to a new room and asked to recall the objects within that office either by writing what they remembered, drawing what they remembered, or by responding yes or no to verbal prompts from a researcher. Their findings showed that participants often recalled items consistent with their schema of an office regardless of the recall condition. Participants who drew or wrote objects tended to forget objects that were schema-inconsistent. When listening to a list of objects, they were more likely to remember seeing schema-inconsistent objects but also tended to falsely recall objects that were typical of offices but were not in the room. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ Because of the trends in recall across participants, this true experiment helps highlight how memory can be shaped by our preexisting knowledge, and can therefore be unreliable. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$

Brewer and Treyens involve an experimental design, $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$, which permits the use of random allocation and other strong controls to reduce the impact of confounding variables. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ In other words, internal validity is quite high: we can be relatively sure that with statistically significant findings, the independent variable (schema congruence/incongruence and recall method) is what is causing the difference between groups. Further, we can infer causation between the independent and dependent variables in true experiments. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ One limitation of this particular study is that it was not a repeated measures design, which could mean that some of the differences in performance could be due to individual differences in memory and schemas.

Neisser and Harsch, in contrast, is a case study. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ Case studies gather rich data using multiple methods over a long period of time for individuals or unique groups. $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ After the Challenger explosion, around one hundred college students were invited to complete a questionnaire about where they were, what they were doing, and how they found out about the event. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ Two years later, they were asked to complete the same surveys again, and some participants were asked to take part in interviews to understand their results further. They were also asked to report their confidence that their memories surrounding the explosion were correct. There were large discrepancies between the accuracy of their memories and their confidence: even when they had very different recollections than their first survey, confidence tended to be high. $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$ The researchers say this is one thing that distinguishes flashbulb memory - not high accuracy, but very high confidence in the accuracy of those memories.

Choosing to use a case study to investigate flashbulb memories is a useful way of addressing one recurring limitation of retrospective memory research: when asking participants to report memories, researchers often have no way of being able to verify those memories. In Neisser and Harsch, which is an example of prospective, longitudinal research, the researchers were able to compare their responses to both the original and follow-up survey years later. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ It is also an example of a naturalistic study. It provides a realistic sense of how memory of a significant event changes over time. We can assume decent transferability to similar, national events, such as 9/11. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

Both studies, despite their diverging methods, have two core issues. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$ For Brewer and Treyens, we have to make an assumption of what the schema is for each participant. There is no way to measure it and understand how schema has influenced each participant while recollecting. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ Similarly, there is no way to measure the exact emotional state without relying on self-reporting in Neisser and Harsch. It could be the case that people with the least accurate recall could be those who were least emotional about the event. $\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{CT}]}$ These are both unknowns that cannot necessarily be addressed through skilful use of research methods alone.
