$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$: \

Thinking and decision-making are higher-order cognitive processes. Thinking is a complex process of analyzing and synthesizing information for understanding $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$. Decision-making is a process of selecting an option from a variety of alternatives present $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$. Both of these cognitive processes are crucial to understanding and interpreting information on a daily basis.

There are two types of thinking: System 1 and System 2. System 1 thinking is a type of automatic and fast thinking that is usually instinct-based, whereas System 2 thinking is more detail-oriented and logical, requiring time before coming to a conclusion $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$. Both of these thinking types lead to cognitive biases such as the framing effect, confirmation bias, and asymmetric dominance. These cognitive biases are mistakes resulting from mental shortcuts (heuristics) that affect the process of decision-making $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$.

Confirmation bias occurs when humans tend to seek out information that supports their opinion or belief and overlook information that does not support it $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$. This bias is explained in the research conducted by Hill et al. (2008)$\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$. The aim was to investigate whether an expectation of guilt can affect the interviewing of a suspect in a crime situation$\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. There were a total of 61 participants (mean age of 21 years). In Study 1, all participants formulated questions they wanted to ask a suspect to determine if they cheated on a task. In Study 2, 45 $`$innocent' and $`$guilty' participants were accused of cheating on a task. They were interviewed with guilt questions or neutral questions. In Study 3, a total of 58 participants listened to audiotaped interviews from Study 2 $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. Following this, they rated aspects of the suspects' verbal behavior. The results of the study were as follows: in Study 1, more guilt-presumptive questions were formed even though participants were free to form their own questions. In Study 2, a significant connection was found between guilt/innocence and whether they confessed or denied. While in Study 3, all the ratings of independent observers who listened to tape recordings of the suspect interviews showed an influence of questioning style on the suspects' verbal behavior $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. These questions were then rated by people who were unaware of the two conditions on a scale of 0-6, where 0 was extremely presumptive of innocence and 6 was extremely presumptive of guilt $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. The study explains confirmation bias because participants holding an expectation of guilt generated more guilt-presumptive questions for the interview $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$.

The strengths of the study are high internal validity, as a cause-and-effect relationship can be observed between the independent variable (IV) and dependent variable (DV), and there is no evidence of researcher or participant bias in the study. The limitations of the study are that it can only be generalized to undergraduate students with a mean age of 21 years, as the sample was restricted to that $\textcolor{#1974D2}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. There are no ethical concerns in the study since there is no mental or physical harm done to students. There is evidence of deception, as the true aim was not shared with the participants to avoid demand characteristics $\textcolor{#1974D2}{[\textbf{CT}]}$.

The study explained above shows how a cognitive bias (confirmation bias) affects decision-making by only focusing on selected information that confirms their belief/opinion $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{F}]}$.

Another cognitive bias is called the framing effect, wherein people make choices based on the framing of the choice in a positive and negative manner or gain and loss $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$. In an experiment conducted by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in 1981, they aimed to observe the influence of the way a decision is made based on how a problem is framed in scenarios involving risks $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. The sample consisted of 307 university students who were given a questionnaire with the question $`$Imagine that the USA is preparing for an outbreak of an unusual Asian Disease, which is expected to kill 600 people $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$.' There were two options provided for two groups: Group 1 had Program A: 200 people will be saved, and Program B: There is a 1/3 possibility that 600 people will be saved, and a 2/3 probability that no people will be saved. Group 2 had Program A: 400 people will die, and Program B: There is a 1/3 probability that nobody will die and a 2/3 probability that 600 people will die $\textcolor{#056608}{[\textbf{S}]}$. The study results found that Program A was selected by 72$\%$ of people in Group 1 and 22$\%$ in Group 2, whereas Program B was selected by 28$\%$ of people in Group 1 and 78$\%$ of people in Group 2. It is seen through this study that the outcomes of the programs were the same, but only because they were framed in gains and losses format, participants gave different responses $\textcolor{#C34A2C}{[\textbf{KT}]}$.

One strength of the study is that no physical harm was done, and informed consent was acquired from the participants $\textcolor{#1974D2}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. There is evidence of high internal validity, which is supported by how Tversky and Kahneman designed their experiments with careful control over extraneous variables. They manipulated framing by presenting information differently to various groups, ensuring that any observed differences in decision-making were attributable to the framing manipulation rather than other factors.$\textcolor{#1974D2}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

The study has some limitations, such as low ecological validity since it was a lab experiment conducted under controlled conditions $\textcolor{#1974D2}{[\textbf{CT}]}$. It cannot be generalized to all age groups since the experiment was conducted on university students only.$\textcolor{#1974D2}{[\textbf{CT}]}$

Both studies effectively show the influence of cognitive biases on thinking and decision-making.
