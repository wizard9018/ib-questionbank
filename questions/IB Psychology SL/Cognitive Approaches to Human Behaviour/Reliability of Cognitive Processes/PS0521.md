# PS0521

Explain one cognitive bias, making use of one research study.
[9 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$: \

A maximum of **9 marks** are awarded if the response includes all of the following:

$\textcolor{#000000}{\textbf{1. Direct Answer}\ [\textbf{DA}]}$\

A direct answer to the question provided in the first few sentences *(e.g. “Cognitive biases are illlogical, systematic errors in thinking that negatively affect decision making. there are several cognitive biases such as framing effects use of information judgment and anchoring bias.”)*

$\textcolor{#F04438}{\textbf{2. Key Terms}\ [\textbf{KT}]}$\

Accurate knowledge and understanding of cognitive bias\

Important concepts and vocabulary terms to use and/or define include: 

- *Fundamental attribution bias - when a favourable outcome is seen as resulting from actions under personal control and unfavourable ones as outside personal control.*

- *Illusion of control - when the understanding of how events occur becomes distorted (creating superstitions).*

- *Hindsight bias - when outcomes are perceived as predictable even when there is no evidence.*

The command term $`$explain' is addressed. This requires students to give a detailed account of the theory, which includes possible reasons or causes for the theory in relation to one research study linked to the cognitive approach to understanding behaviour. 

$\textcolor{#008c3a}{\textbf{3. Study}\ [\textbf{S}]}$\

A clear focus on describing one study $\text{\textendash}$ including the aim, procedure, results, and conclusion of the study\

*Relevant studies include but are not limited to:*

- Marsh and Hanlon (2007) confirmation bias in observations 

- Brandt et al. (1975) self-serving bias in teachers of students success or failure

- Fluke at al. (2010) illusions of control in superstitions

- Englich and Mussweiler (2001) Anchoring bias in jury verdicts

$\textcolor{#4472C4}{\textbf{4. Link}\ [\textbf{L}]}$\

An explicit link exploring cognitive bias to one study and in reference to the question *(e.g. $`$An example of cognitive bias is anchoring bias. Anchoring bias is when we use the first piece of information we receive to influence our decisions. For example, in Englich and Mussweiler's (2001) study, the decisions the jury made were biased by the original recommendations they received in court. The timeframe created an anchor to help them make a decision on an ambiguous concept. They used this as an anchor in which to create their cognitive judgment, thus showing cognitive bias.')*

Marking limits:

- Cognitive bias and its related parts are not explained in the opening paragraph.

- Responses should clearly relate the theory to the cognitive approach and must focus on the cognitive aspects of the research.

- Explaining more than one study will mean marks are only given to the first study described.

- Describing a study but cognitive bias is not explicitly addressed *(maximum 3 marks awarded)*

- Explaining cognitive bias but does not refer to an appropriate study *(maximum 4 marks awarded)*

- Clear links between the theory of cognitive bias, the related parts and the study are needed to gain marks in the top mark band. *(If the link is only implicit a maximum of 6 marks will be awarded)*

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$: \

Cognitive biases are illogical, systematic errors in thinking that negatively affect decision-making. When making decisions, we often rely system one thinking, to make quick intuitive judgements. $\textcolor{#F04438}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ In order to reduce the amount of thinking required, system one makes use of heuristics or mental shortcuts to make decisions. While heuristics can help us to make decisions quickly, they sometimes lead to illogical or incorrect decisions or conclusions. $\textcolor{#F04438}{[\textbf{KT}]}$

One example of a cognitive bias that results from the use of heuristics is anchoring bias. $\textcolor{#000000}{[\textbf{DA}]}$  Anchoring bias is when the first piece of information we receive influences our decisions. $\textcolor{#F04438}{[\textbf{KT}]}$ For example, when we hear that the original price for an item was \$100 but is now marked down to \$80, we find that more attractive than walking in and seeing the same item for \$80. The value of the goods is ambiguous $\text{\textendash}$ that is, we don't know how much it should be, so this information helps us to form an opinion and make a decision.$\textcolor{#F04438}{[\textbf{KT}]}$

Englich and Mussweiler (2001) explored the idea of anchoring bias in jury decision-making. They aimed to see if anchoring bias would affect a jury's verdict.$\textcolor{#008c3a}{[\textbf{S}]}$ A sample of Law students was given a rape case and asked, based on the evidence, how long the punishment should be in one group they were told that the prosecution recommended a 34-month sentence and in the other group - a 2-month sentence.$\textcolor{#008c3a}{[\textbf{S}]}$ The study showed that when given the suggestion of 34 months, the students recommended on average a longer sentence.$\textcolor{#008c3a}{[\textbf{S}]}$ Since they would not have an absolute sense of how many months to punish the offender, they use the anchor that they were given to make their decision this is an example of how cognitive bias may affect one's behaviour.$\textcolor{#4472C4}{[\textbf{L}]}$
