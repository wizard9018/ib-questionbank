# PH1021

**Maximum mark: 15**

A radially insulated metal rod of diameter 10 cm, length 150 cm, and thermal conductivity 200 W m$^{-1}$ $\degree$C$^{-1}$ is embedded vertically in the top face of a large ice block and heated continuously at constant power supplied to the free end. At thermal equilibrium, the temperature of the heated end of the rod is 100$\degree$C.

![Screenshot 2025-07-18 at 5.25.28 PM](<images/Screenshot 2025-07-18 at 5.25.28 PM.png>)

1. Explain, on a microscopic level, how heat is transferred to the ice from the hot end of the rod. [2 marks]

2.  1. Show that a power of approximately 105 W enters the rod. Assume that the length of rod contained inside the ice block is negligible. [3 marks]

    2. The graph shows how the mass of liquid water $m$ changes with time $t$. Ice has a specific latent heat of fusion of approximately 334 J g$^{-1}$.

        ![Screenshot 2025-07-18 at 5.26.28 PM](<images/Screenshot 2025-07-18 at 5.26.28 PM.png>)

        Estimate the percentage of the total supplied power that is lost to the surroundings. [3 marks]

    3. The rod is replaced with a radially insulated rod of equal length, half the diameter and two times the thermal conductivity. The free end of the rod is heated until it reaches an equilibrium temperature of $100\degree$C.

        Determine the ratio $\dfrac{
        \textrm{New\,\,rate\,\,of\,\,melting}}{\textrm{Old\,\,rate\,\,of\,\,melting}}$. [2 marks]

3. The metal rod is stripped of its insulation without altering the power supplied to the free end, and the melting rate of the ice is measured in thermal equilibrium.

    1. Describe how the melting rate of the ice is affected by the change. [2 marks]

    2. The uninsulated rod is next removed and then embedded vertically in the bottom face of the ice block. The same power is supplied to the free end of the rod and the equilibrium ice melting rate is again measured.

        Discuss how the change of position alters the melting rate of the ice. [3 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  1. Kinetic/vibrational energy transferred through particle collisions $\checkmark$

        Heat is transferred from hot to cold end because particles at higher temperature collide with/pass energy to lower energy particles $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$: \
        Heat transfer by conduction is caused by kinetic energy transfer through particle collisions. As heat is supplied to the free end of the rod, the particles vibrate more and the kinetic energy of particles at that end increases, causing them to collide with particles below and transfer energy to them. Considering particles at a given height up the rod, the kinetic energy of particles colliding from above is greater than the kinetic energy of particles colliding from below, meaning that the net direction of heat transfer is down the rod, from the hot end to the cold end.

2.  1. Use of $\dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{kA\Delta T}{L}$ $\checkmark$

        - $\Delta T=100\degree\textrm{C}$ and $A=\pi \Bigl (\dfrac{D}{2} \Bigr )^2 \approx 78.5\textrm{ cm}^2$ $\checkmark$

        - $\dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{(200\textrm{ W m}^{-1}\degree\textrm{C}^{-1})(0.00785\textrm{ m}^2)(100\degree\textrm{C})}{1.5\textrm{ m}} \approx$ :answer[$104.6$ W] $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$: \
        Assuming that no heat is lost radially from the rod (the rod is radially insulated), the power transfer along the rod is given by

        - $\dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{kA\Delta T}{l}$

        From the stated diameter $D$, the cross-sectional area $A$ is

        - $A = \pi \Bigl (\dfrac{D}{2} \Bigr)^2 = 25\pi \textrm{ cm}^2 \approx 78.5\textrm{ cm}^2$

        Finally, as the system is in thermal equilibrium, the cold end of the rod must be at a temperature of $0\degree$C (the melting temperature of ice), meaning that the temperature difference $\Delta T$ from the hot end to the cold end of the rod is $100\degree$C.

        Hence,

        - $\dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{(200\textrm{ W m}^{-1} \degree\textrm{C}^{-1})(0.00785\textrm{ m}^2)(100\degree\textrm{C})}{1.5\textrm{ m}} \approx 105$ W

    2.  ::indent
        - $\dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t} = 0.2\textrm{ g s}^{-1}$ $\checkmark$

        - $\dfrac{\Delta Q_{melt}}{\Delta t} = L \dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t} = (334)(0.2) = 66.8 \ll \textrm{W}\gg$ $\checkmark$

        - $P_{lost}=\dfrac{105-66.8}{105}=0.364=$ :answer[$36.4\,\%$] $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$: \
        The power used to melt the ice, $\dfrac{\Delta Q_{melt}}{\Delta t}$, is related to the melting rate $\dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}$ and specific latent heat of fusion $L$ by the equation

        - $\dfrac{\Delta Q_{melt}}{\Delta t} = L \dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}$

        From the gradient of the graph, it follows that

        - $\dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t} = 0.2\textrm{ g s}^{-1}$

        Hence,

        - $\dfrac{\Delta Q_{melt}}{\Delta t} = (334\textrm{ J g}^{-1})(0.2\textrm{ g s}^{-1})=66.8$ W

        Since the power input to the rod is approximately $105$ W, the percentage of power lost to the surroundings is given by:

        - $P_{lost}=100\Bigl(1-\dfrac{\Delta Q_{melt}}{\Delta Q_{total}} \Bigr)\% \approx 100\Bigl (1-\dfrac{66.8}{105} \Bigr)\%=36.4\%$

    3. ::indent
        - $\dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} \propto kA, \hspace{1em} k_{new}=2k_{old}, \hspace{1em} A_{new}=\dfrac{A_{old}}{4}$ $\checkmark$

        - $\dfrac{\Delta Q_{new}}{\Delta t} =$ :answer[$\dfrac{1}{2}$] $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$: \
        The rate of heat transfer through thermal conduction is given by the equation

        - $\dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{kA\Delta T}{l}$

        The new rod has the same equilibrium temperature distribution as the original, but it also has two times the thermal conductivity and half the diameter. The rate of heat transfer by conduction in the new scenario is therefore given by

        - $\dfrac{Q_{new}}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{k_{new}A_{new}\Delta T}{L} = \dfrac{\Delta T}{L}(2k_{old})\Bigl(\dfrac{A_{old}}{4}\Bigr) = \dfrac{1}{2} \dfrac{\Delta Q_{old}}{\Delta t}$

        Thus, the rate of melting of the ice is halved.

3.  1.  Radiation/convection results in heat loss from the rod $\checkmark$

        The effect of increased radiation/convection is to decrease melting rate $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$: \
        Removing the insulation causes heat to be lost radially from the rod through radiation and convection. Thus, since the power supplied to the rod remains constant, the rate of melting is decreased by the loss of insulation.

    2.  Convection causes heat transfer in the direction away from ice when rod is embedded in top face of block $\checkmark$

        Convection causes heat transfer towards ice when the rod is embedded in bottom face of block $\checkmark$

        Embedding rod in bottom face causes higher melting rate than embedding in top face $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$: \
        In convection, heating of the surrounding air reduces its density, resulting in warmer air rising and cool air displacing the heated air to yield upward heat transfer.

        In the first case, wherein the rod is embedded in the top face of the ice block, convection causes the heat lost by the rod to rise upwards from the heated free end. Consequently, heat lost from the rod through convection does not contribute to ice melting.

        In the second case, the rod is instead embedded in the bottom face of the ice block. As convection causes upward heat transfer, some of the heat lost from the rod through convection reaches the ice block and contributes to melting. Therefore, the rate of melting in the second scenario is greater than in the first.
