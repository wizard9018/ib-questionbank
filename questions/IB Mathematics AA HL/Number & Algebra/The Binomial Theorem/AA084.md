# AA084

**Maximum mark: 25**

Jack and John have decided to play a game. They will be rolling a die
seven times. One roll of a die is considered as one round of the game.
On each round, John agrees to pay Jack \$4 if $1$ or $2$ is rolled, Jack
agrees to pay John \$2 if $3,4,5$ or $6$ is rolled, and who is paid wins
the round. In the end, who earns money wins the game.

1.  Show that the probability that Jack wins exactly two rounds is
    $\dfrac{224}{729}$.  [3 marks]

2.
    1.  Explain why the total number of outcomes for the results of the
        seven rounds is $128$.

    2.  Expand $(1 + y)^7$ and choose a suitable value of $y$ to prove
        that

        $$
        128 = \binom{7}{0} + \binom{7}{1} + \binom{7}{2} + \binom{7}{3} + \binom{7}{4} + \binom{7}{5} + \binom{7}{6} + \binom{7}{7}.
        $$

    3.  Give a meaning of the equality above in the context of the
        seven\
        rounds.[4 marks]

3.
    1.  Find the expected amount of money earned by each player in the
        game.

    2.  Who is expected to win the game?
    3.  Is this game fair? Justify your answer. [3 marks]

4.  Jack and John have decided to play the game again.
    1.  Find an expression for the probability that John wins five
        rounds on the first game and two rounds on the second game. Give
        your answer in the form

        $$
        \binom{7}{r}^2\bigg[\frac{1}{3}\bigg]^s\bigg[\frac{2}{3}\bigg]^t
        $$
        where the values of $r,s$ and $t$ are to be found.

    2.  Use your answer to (d) (i) and seven similar expressions to
        write down the probability that John wins a total of seven
        rounds over two games as the sum of eight probabilities.

    3.  Hence prove that
        $$
        \binom{14}{7} = \sum_{k = 0}^7 \binom{7}{k}^2.
        $$
        [9 marks]

5.  Now Jack and John roll a die $12$ times. Let $A$ denote the number
    of rounds Jack wins. The expected value of $A$ can be written as

    $$
    \mathrm{E}[A] = \sum_{r=0}^{12} r\binom{12}{r} \left[\dfrac{a^{12-r}}{b^{12}}\right]
    $$

    1.  Find the value of $a$ and $b$.
    2.  Differentiate the expansion of $(1 + y)^{12}$ to prove that the
        expected\
        number of rolls Jack wins is $4$. [6 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  The probability that Jack wins a round is $\dfrac{1}{3}$.
    Hence the probability that Jack wins exactly two rounds is

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \binom{7}{2}\bigg[\dfrac{1}{3}\bigg]^2\bigg[\dfrac{2}{3}\bigg]^5 &= \bigg[\dfrac{7\cdot6}{2}\bigg]\bigg[\dfrac{1}{3^2}\bigg]\bigg[\dfrac{2^5}{3^5}\bigg]  \\[5pt]
    &= \dfrac{7\cdot2^5}{3^6} \\[9pt]
    &= \dfrac{224}{729}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

2.
    1.  :::answer
        On each round, either Jack wins or John wins. Hence in seven rounds, there are $2^7 = 128$ possible outcomes.

    2.  Using the binomial theorem, we have
        $$
        (1 + y)^7  =  \sum_{k = 0}^7 \binom{7}{k}\hspace{0.05em}y^k \hspace{0.3em}
        $$
        Hence, if we substitute $y = 1$ in the equation $(1)$, we get

        $$
        \hspace{0.7em} 128 = \sum_{k = 0}^7 \binom{7}{k}
        $$

    3.  :::answer
        The total number of outcomes equals to the sum of the number of ways Jack can win $k$ rounds over $k = 0,1,2,\dots,7$.

3.
    1.  The expected amount of money earned by Jack in the game is
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \hspace{0.2em} \mathrm{E}[\$ \text{ earned by Jack}] &= 7\left[(\$\hspace{0.05em}4)\cdot\frac{1}{3} + (-\$\hspace{0.05em}2)\cdot\frac{2}{3}\right] \\[3pt]
        &= \$\hspace{0.05em}0.00
        \end{aligned}
        $$

        The expected amount of money earned by John in the game is

        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \mathrm{E}[\$ \text{ earned by John}] &= 7\left[(-\$\hspace{0.05em}4)\cdot\frac{1}{3} + (\$\hspace{0.05em}2)\cdot\frac{2}{3}\right] \\[3pt]
        &= \$\hspace{0.05em}0.00
        \end{aligned}
        $$

    2.  :::answer
        Since no one is expected to earn money in the game, no one is expected to win the game.

    3.  :::answer
        Yes, this game is fair because no one is expected to win or lose the~game.

4.
    1.  The probability that John wins a round is $\dfrac{2}{3}$.
        Hence the probability that John wins five rounds on the first
        game and two rounds on the second game is

        $$
        \left[\binom{7}{5}\bigg[\dfrac{2}{3}\bigg]^5\bigg[\dfrac{1}{3}\bigg]^2\right]\left[\binom{7}{2}\bigg[\dfrac{2}{3}\bigg]^2\bigg[\dfrac{1}{3}\bigg]^5\right]  =  \hspace{0.525em}\binom{7}{5}^2\bigg[\dfrac{2}{3}\bigg]^7\bigg[\dfrac{1}{3}\bigg]^7
        $$

    2.  Similarly as in (d) (i), the probability that John wins a total
        of seven rounds over two games is

        $$
        \sum_{k = 0}^7 \binom{7}{k}^2\bigg[\dfrac{2}{3}\bigg]^7\bigg[\dfrac{1}{3}\bigg]^7 = \hspace{0.525em}\bigg[\dfrac{2^7}{3^{14}}\bigg]\sum_{k = 0}^7 \binom{7}{k}^2
        $$

    3.  Equivalently, the probability that John wins $7$ rounds out of
        $14$ is

        $$
        \binom{14}{7}\bigg[\dfrac{2}{3}\bigg]^7\bigg[\dfrac{1}{3}\bigg]^7 = \binom{14}{7}\bigg[\dfrac{2^7}{3^{14}}\bigg] \hspace{0.75em}
        $$
        Hence we obtain
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \binom{14}{7}\cancel{\bigg[\dfrac{2^7}{3^{14}}\bigg]} &= \cancel{\bigg[\dfrac{2^7}{3^{14}}\bigg]}\sum_{k = 0}^7 \binom{7}{k}^2 \\
        \end{aligned}
        $$

        $$
        \binom{14}{7} = \sum_{k = 0}^7 \binom{7}{k}^2 \hspace{0.15em}
        $$

5.
    1.  The expected value of $A$ is
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \hspace{3em} \mathrm{E}[A] &= \sum_{r=0}^{12} r\binom{12}{r}\bigg[\dfrac{1}{3}\bigg]^r\bigg[\dfrac{2}{3}\bigg]^{12-r} \\[6pt]
        &=  \sum_{r=0}^{12} r\binom{12}{r}\left[\dfrac{2^{12-r}}{3^{12}}\right]
        \end{aligned}
        $$

        Hence
         $a = 2$
        and  $b = 3$

    2.  Using the binomial theorem, we have
        $$
        (1 + y)^{12}  =  \sum_{k = 0}^{12} \binom{12}{k}\hspace{0.05em}y^k \hspace{1.2em}
        $$
        Hence, if we differentiate both sides of the equation $(2)$, we
        get
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}\left[(1 + y)^{12}\right] &= \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}\left[\sum_{r=0}^{12} \binom{12}{r} y^{r}\right] \hspace{0.9em} \\[6pt]
        12(1 + y)^{11} &= \sum_{r=0}^{12} r\binom{12}{r} y^{r-1}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

        To obtain $\dfrac{3^{12}}{2^{11}}$ on the left hand side and
        $2^{1-r}$ on the right hand side of

        the equation $(3)$, we need to substitute $y = \dfrac{1}{2}$ in
        this equation.
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        12\left(1 + \dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{11} &= \sum_{r=0}^{12} r\binom{12}{r} \bigg[\dfrac{1}{2}\bigg]^{r-1} \hspace{0.5em} \\[6pt]
        4\cdot\dfrac{3^{12}}{2^{11}} &= \sum_{r=0}^{12} r\binom{12}{r} \big[2^{1-r}\big]
        \end{aligned}
        $$

        Hence, if we rewrite the equation $(4)$, we obtain

        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \hspace{4.5em} 4 &= \sum_{r=0}^{12} r\binom{12}{r} \left[\dfrac{2^{12-r}}{3^{12}}\right] \\
        \end{aligned}
        $$

        $$
        4 = \mathrm{E}[A] \hspace{1.9em}
        $$
