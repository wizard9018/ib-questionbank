# AA1328

**Maximum mark: 6**

In the expansion $(2x+1)^n$, where $n\in\mathbb Z^+$, the coefficient of $x^3$ is equal to six times the difference between of the coefficient of $x^2$ and the coefficient of $x$.

Find the value of $n$.

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

Let's use the binomial theorem from the formula booklet, to start expanding this binomial. 

$$

\begin{aligned}

(a+b)^n&= a^n+\binom{n}{1}a^{n-1}b +...+ \binom{n}{r}a^{n-r}b^r +...

\end{aligned}

$$

The given information relates to coefficients of the $x$, $x^2$, and $x^3$ terms so we want to expand in ascending powers of $x$. For that we need to switch the terms of the binomial so that $a=1$ and $b=2x$.

Substituting these values into the formula we get

$$

\begin{aligned}

(1+2x)^n &= (1)^n + \binom{n}{1}(1)^{n-1}(2x)+\binom{n}{2}(1)^{n-2}(2x)^2+\binom{n}{3}(1)^{n-3}(2x)^3 + ...\\[12pt]

&=1+\binom{n}{1}2x+\binom{n}{2}4x^2+\binom{n}{3}8x^3+...\tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}

\end{aligned}

$$

We are told that six times the difference of the coefficients of the $x^2$ term and $x$ term is equal to the coefficient of the $x^3$ term.

Using this relationship we can form an equation.

$$

\begin{aligned}

6\left[4\binom{n}{2}-2\binom{n}{1}\right]&=8\binom{n}{3}\tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}\\[12pt]

24\binom{n}{2}-12\binom{n}{1}&=8\binom{n}{3}\\[12pt]

6\binom{n}{2}-3\binom{n}{1}&=2\binom{n}{3}\hspace{5em}[1]

\end{aligned}

$$

Now let's use the formula for the binomial coefficient.

$$

\begin{aligned}

    \binom{n}{r}&=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}

\end{aligned}

$$

Evaluating the formula when $r=1$, $r=2$, and $r=3$ we get

$$

\begin{aligned}

    \binom{n}{1}&=\frac{n!}{1!(n-1)!}=n\\[12pt]

    \binom{n}{2}&=\frac{n!}{2!(n-2)!}=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\\[12pt]

    \binom{n}{3}&=\frac{n!}{3!(n-3)!}=\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{6}\tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}

\end{aligned}

$$

Now we substitute these into equation [1] and solve for $n$.

$$

\begin{aligned}

 6\left(\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\right)-3n&=2\left(\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{6}\right)\tag*{\textbf{M1}}\\[12pt]

3n(n-1)-3n&=\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{3}\\[9pt]

9n(n-1)-9n&=n(n-1)(n-2)

\end{aligned}

$$

From here we can factorise $9n$ on the L.H.S.

$$

\begin{aligned}

9n(n-1)-9n&=n(n-1)(n-2)\\[6pt]

9n(n-1-1)&=n(n-1)(n-2)\tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}\\[6pt]

9n(n-2)&=n(n-1)(n-2)

\end{aligned}

$$

As we know that $n\in\mathbb Z^+$ we know that $n\neq0$. Also, because the $x^3$ term exists, $n>2$. Therefore we can divide both sides by $n$ and by $n-2$.

$$

\begin{aligned}

 9\cancel{n}\cancel{(n-2)}&=\cancel{n}(n-1)\cancel{(n-2)}\\[6pt]

 9&=n-1\\[6pt]

n&=10\tag*{\textbf{A1}}

\end{aligned}

$$
