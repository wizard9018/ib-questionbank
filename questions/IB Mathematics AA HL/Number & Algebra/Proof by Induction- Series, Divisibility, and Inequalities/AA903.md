# AA903

**Maximum mark: 17**

The following diagram shows the graph of
$y=\arctan(2x-3)+\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ for $x\in \mathbb{R}$,\
with asymptotes at $y=\dfrac{\pi}{4}$ and $y=\dfrac{5\pi}{4}$.

![081334c23d135e25efc729fff5cf10e0f4fe7363.svg](images/081334c23d135e25efc729fff5cf10e0f4fe7363.svg)

1.  Describe a sequence of transformations that transforms the graph of\
    $y=\arctan x$ to the graph of $y=\arctan(2x-3)+\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ for
    $x\in \mathbb{R}$.[3 marks]

2.  Show that
    $\arctan p - \arctan q \equiv \arctan \left(\dfrac{p-q}{1+pq}\right)$.[3 marks]

3.  Verify that
    $\arctan(x+2)-\arctan(x+1) = \arctan\left( \dfrac{1}{(x+1)^2+(x+1)+1}\right)$.[3 marks]

4.  Using mathematical induction and the results from **part (b)** and
    **(c)**, prove that[8 marks]

    $$
    \sum_{r=1}^n \arctan\left(\dfrac{1}{r^2+r+1}\right) = \arctan(n+1)-\dfrac{\pi}{4} \hspace{0.8em} \text{for } n\in \mathbb{Z}^{+}.
    $$

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  The function can be expressed as
    $y=\arctan\left(2\left(x-\dfrac{3}{2}\right)\right)+\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$,
    hence we get the following transformations in order

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \text{transformation $1$}&: \hspace{0.5em}\hspace{0.525em}\text{horizontal stretch by a factor of $\dfrac{1}{2}$} \\[10pt]
    \text{transformation $2$}&:
    \hspace{0.5em}\hspace{0.525em}\text{horizontal translation $\dfrac{3}{2}$ units right}\\[10pt]
    \text{transformation $3$}&: \hspace{0.5em}\hspace{0.525em}\text{vertical translation $\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ units up}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Note that the vertical shift, which is transformation $3$, can also be performed before the horizontal transformations.

2.  Using the compound angle identity, we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \tan\left(\arctan p - \arctan q \right) &= \dfrac{\tan(\arctan p)-\tan(\arctan q)}{1+\tan(\arctan p)\tan(\arctan q)} \\[12pt]
    &=\dfrac{p-q}{1+pq} \\[12pt]\\
    \intertext{Finally, taking the $\arctan$ on both sides}\\
    &\hspace{-2.85cm}\arctan p - \arctan q \equiv \arctan \left(\dfrac{p-q}{1+pq}\right)
    \end{aligned}
    $$

3.  Using the formula from part (b), we obtain
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \arctan(x+2)-\arctan(x+1) &= \arctan\left(\dfrac{(x+2)-(x+1)}{1+(x+2)(x+1)}\right)\\[12pt]
    &= \arctan\left(\dfrac{1}{((x+1)+1)(x+1)+1}\right)\\[12pt]
    &\hspace{0.2em}=\arctan\left(\dfrac{1}{(x+1)^2+(x+1)+1}\right)
    \end{aligned}
    $$

4.  Let $\mathrm{P}(n)$ be the following statement:
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \sum_{r=1}^n \arctan\left(\dfrac{1}{r^2+r+1}\right) &= \arctan(n+1)-\dfrac{\pi}{4} \hspace{3em} \big[\,\text{for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}^{+}$}\,\big]
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Using mathematical induction, we will prove that $\mathrm{P}(n)$
    holds for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}^{+}$.

    :u[Base case]. For $n = 1$, from part (a), we have

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \arctan\left(\dfrac{1}{(1)^2+1+1}\right) &= \arctan\left(\dfrac{1}{3}\right) \\[10pt]
    &= \arctan(2)-\arctan(1) \hspace{3em} \big[\text{by part (b,c)}\big]\\[12pt]
    &= \arctan(2)-\dfrac{\pi}{4}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Hence P$(1)$ holds.

    :u[Inductive hypothesis]. We assume that $\mathrm{P}(k)$
    holds for some $k \geq 1$. In other words, we assume that

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \sum_{r=1}^k \arctan\left(\dfrac{1}{r^2+r+1}\right) = \arctan(k+1)-\dfrac{\pi}{4} \hspace{0.5em}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    for some $k \geq 1$.

    :u[Inductive step]. To show that $\mathrm{P}(k+1)$ holds,
    we need to show that
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \sum_{r=1}^{k+1} \arctan\left(\dfrac{1}{r^2+r+1}\right) = \arctan(k+2)-\dfrac{\pi}{4}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    If we add the $k+1$ term to both sides of the equation (IH), we
    obtain
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \sum_{r=1}^{k+1} \arctan\left(\dfrac{1}{r^2+r+1}\right) &= \arctan(k+1)-\dfrac{\pi}{4} + \arctan\left(\dfrac{1}{(k+1)^2+(k+1)+1}\right)\\[10pt]
    \big[\text{by part (c)}\big]\hspace{3em} &= \arctan(k+2) - \arctan(k+1) + \arctan(k+1)-\dfrac{\pi}{4}\\[12pt]
    &= \arctan(k+2)-\dfrac{\pi}{4}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Hence $P(k+1)$ holds
    as well.

    :u[Conclusion]. P$(1)$ holds, and for every $k \geq 1$, if
    P$(k)$ holds then P$(k+1)$ also holds.

    Hence, by mathematical induction, we conclude that P$(n)$ holds for
    all $n \in \mathbb{Z}^{+}$.
