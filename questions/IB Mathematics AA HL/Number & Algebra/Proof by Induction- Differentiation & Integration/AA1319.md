# AA1319

**Maximum mark: 14**

Let $f(x)=(2x+1)e^{2x}$.

1.    Show that $f'(x)=4e^{2x}(x+1)$. [2 marks]

2.    Use mathematical induction to prove that $f^{(n)}(x)=2^{n+1}e^{2x}\left(x+\dfrac{n+1}{2}\right)$
    for $n\geq 1$ and $n \in \mathbb{Z}$. [6 marks]

Let another function $g$ be such that $g(x)=\cos{kx}$, where $k \in \mathbb{R}$.

Consider the function $h$ which is defined by $h(x)=\dfrac{g(x)}{ f(x)}$.

It is known that there is no $x^2$ term in the Maclaurin series for $h(x)$.

3.    Find the possible values of $k$. [6 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1. The function $f$ is a product of two functions of $x$.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    f(x)&=\textcolor{red}{(2x+1)}\textcolor{blue}{e^{2x}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    We must use the product rule to differentiate it.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \hspace{8em}&\text{If }& &\hspace{-5em}&y&=\textcolor{red}{u}\textcolor{blue}{v}&\\[6pt]
    \hspace{8em}&\text{then }&&\hspace{-5em}&\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}&= v\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}+u\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Let
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    u&=2x+1&v&=e^{2x}\\[12pt]
    \frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}&=2&\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}&=2e^{2x}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Substituting these into the product rule formula
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    f'(x)&=e^{2x} \times 2 + (2x+1) \times 2e^{2x}\tag*{\textbf{{M1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    and factorising the result we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &=2e^{2x}(1 + 2x+1)\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}\\[6pt]
    &=2e^{2x}(2x+2)\\[6pt]
    &=2e^{2x}\times 2(x+1)\\[6pt]
    &=4e^{2x}(x+1)\hspace{1em}\text{...as required.}\tag*{\textbf{{AG}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

2. Let's begin with the base case. As we are told that $n$ is an integer, for which $n \geq 1$, then the base case (smallest $n$ value) occurs when $n=1$.

    We substitute $n=\textcolor{red}{1}$ into the given formula to verify it matches the result from part (a).
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    f^{(1)}(x)&=2^{\textcolor{red}{1}+1}e^{2x}\left(x+\frac{\textcolor{red}{1}+1}{2}\right)\\[12pt]
    &=2^2e^{2x}\left(x+\frac{2}{2}\right)\\[12pt]
    &=4e^{2x}(x+1)\\[6pt]
    &=\text{answer from part (a)}\tag*{\textbf{{R1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    The formula works for the base case.
    Next we will make the assumption.
    We will assume that when $n=k$ the formula is true.
    Therefore
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    f^{(k)}(x)&=2^{k+1}e^{2x}\left(x+\dfrac{k+1}{2}\right)\tag*{\textbf{{M1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    In the next step we must prove that the formula is true when $n=k+1$ by using the assumption (when $n=k$).
    Let's begin by writing down the formula for when $n=\textcolor{red}{k+1}$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    f^{(\textcolor{red}{k+1})}(x)&=2^{\textcolor{red}{k+1}+1}e^{2x}\left(x+\dfrac{\textcolor{red}{k+1}+1}{2}\right)\\[12pt]
    &=2^{k+2}e^{2x}\left(x+\dfrac{k+2}{2}\right)\hspace{2em}[1]
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Differentiating the assumed case for $n=k$ should produce the expression on the R.H.S. of $[1]$, confirming the formula is true.

    Let's factorise out the constant from the assumption and then use the product rule, in a similar way to part (a), to differentiate it
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\big[f^{(k)}(x)\big]&=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\bigg[2^{k+1}e^{2x}\left(x+\dfrac{k+1}{2}\right)\bigg]\tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}\\[12pt]
    &=2^{k+1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\bigg[e^{2x}\left(x+\dfrac{k+1}{2}\right)\bigg]\\[12pt]
    &=2^{k+1}\bigg[(e^{2x})'\left(x+\dfrac{k+1}{2}\right)+\left(x+\dfrac{k+1}{2}\right)'(e^{2x})\bigg]\\[12pt]
    &=2^{k+1}\bigg[2e^{2x}\left(x+\dfrac{k+1}{2}\right)+e^{2x}\bigg]\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Don't forget, we are differentiating with respect to $x$ and we treat $k$ as a constant. Hence why $\left(x+\frac{k+1}{2}\right)'=1$.

    Now look at the result, $[1]$, which we are trying to prove. It looks like we should try to factorise our result as much as possible.

    To best match the answer we should take out a factor of $2e^{2x}$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &=2^{k+1}\times2e^{2x}\bigg[\left(x+\dfrac{k+1}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\bigg]
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Next we rewrite the factor as $2^{k+1}\times 2 = 2^{k+2}$ and simplify inside the bracket
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &=2^{k+2}e^{2x}\bigg(x+\dfrac{k+1+1}{2}\bigg)\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}\\[6pt]
    &=2^{k+2}e^{2x}\bigg(x+\dfrac{k+2}{2}\bigg)\\[6pt]
    &=[1]\hspace{2em}\text{...as required}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    We must finish the proof with a summary sentence.

    We verified the base case, when $n=1$ and we proved that the formula works for $n=k+1$ if we assume it to be true for $n=k$
    $\text{Therefore, we conclude by mathematical induction, that the result is true for all }n\text{ where }n\text{ is a positive integer.}$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \tag*{\textbf{{R1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
3. In this question, we need to write the Maclaurin expansions for both $f$ and $g$ so that we can attempt to find their quotient. Then using the fact that the coefficient of $x^2$ is zero we can form and solve an equation to find $k$.

    Let's start with $g$. For this, we can use the formula given in the formula booklet
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \cos{x} &= 1 - \frac{x^2}{2!} + \frac{x^4}{4!} - \dots
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Recall that $g(x) = \cos{kx}$. We can rewrite the formula using the substitution $kx$ as $x$, giving
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    g(x) &= \cos{kx} \\[6pt]
    &= 1 - \frac{(kx)^2}{2!} + \frac{(kx)^4}{4!} - \dots \\[6pt]
    &= 1 - \frac{k^2x^2}{2} + \frac{k^4x^4}{24} - \dots\tag*{\textbf{{(A1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Now onto $f$. Let's recall the general formula for the Maclaurin series
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    f(x) &= f(0) + xf'(0) + \frac{x^2}{2!}f''(0) + \dots
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    To write the expansion for $f$, we need to evaluate $f(0)$, $f'(0)$, $f''(0)$ and $f'''(0)$.
    We can use the formula we proved in part (b), choosing $n = 1$ (which we already have), $n = 2$, and $n = 3$.
    First, evaluate the function $f$ at $n=0$ and $x = 0$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    f(0) &= (2(0) + 1)e^{2(0)} \\[6pt]
    &= 1
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Next, for $n = 1$ and $x = 0$, we use part (a) to get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    f^{(1)}(0) &= 4e^{2(0)} \\[6pt]
    &= 4
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Then, for $n = \textcolor{red}{2}$ and $x = 0$, we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    f^{(2)}(0) &= 2^{\textcolor{red}{2}+1}e^{2(0)}\left(0 + \frac{\textcolor{red}{2}+1}{2}\right) \\[6pt]
    &= 8 \times \frac{3}{2} \\[6pt]
    &= 12
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Finally, for $n = \textcolor{blue}{3}$, we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    f^{(3)}(0) &= 2^{\textcolor{blue}{3}+1}e^{2(0)}\left(0 + \frac{\textcolor{blue}{3}+1}{2}\right) \\[6pt]
    &= 16 \times 2 \\[6pt]
    &= 32
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Note, we are only looking for terms up to and including $x^2$, so you can find the answer without completing the $n=3$ step.
    Now we can put these results together to get the expansion for $f$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    f(x) &= (2x + 1)e^{2x} \\[12pt]
    &= f(0) + xf'(0) + \frac{x^2}{2!}f''(0) + \frac{x^3}{3!}f'''(0) + \dots \tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}\\[12pt]
    &= 1 + 4x + \frac{12x^2}{2!} + \frac{32x^3}{3!} + \dots \\[12pt]
    &= 1 + 4x + 6x^2 + \frac{16}{3}x^3 + \dots\tag*{\textbf{{(A1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Next we form $h(x)$.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    h(x) &= \frac{g(x)}{f(x)} \\[12pt]
    &= \frac{\left(1 - \frac{k^2x^2}{2} + \frac{k^4x^4}{24} - \dots \right)}{\left(1 + 4x + 6x^2 + \frac{16}{3}x^3 + \dots \right)}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    To find the result when dividing two infinite series in this way it is often better practice to rewrite the expression as a multiplication.

    To do this, let's assume the result is a new infinite series with coefficients of $a_n$, $n \in \mathbb{N}$, hence we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{\left(1 - \frac{k^2x^2}{2} + \frac{k^4x^4}{24} - \dots \right)}{\left(1 + 4x + 6x^2 + \frac{16}{3}x^3 + \dots \right)}&=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2+a_3x^3+...
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    We can now rewrite the quotient as a multiplication
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &\left(1 - \frac{k^2x^2}{2} + \frac{k^4x^4}{24} - \dots \right)=\left(1 + 4x + 6x^2 + \frac{16}{3}x^3 + \dots \right)(a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2+a_3x^3+...) \hspace{2em}[1]
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Next, as we need to find the $x^2$ term, we will write the terms up to $x^2$ for the R.H.S. of [1].
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    =&&+a_0&&+4a_0x&&+6a_0x^2+...\\[6pt]
    &&&&+a_1x&&+4a_1x^2+...\\[6pt]
    &&&&&&+a_2x^2+...\\[6pt]
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Note, by writing the terms in columns like this it makes it easier to combine the coefficients for each $x$ term.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &=a_0+(4a_0+a_1)x+(6a_0+4a_1+a_2)x^2+...\tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    We can now compare the coefficients of the series above with the L.H.S. of [1]
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &&\textcolor{red}{a_0}&&\textcolor{blue}{+(4a_0+a_1)}x&&\textcolor{green}{+(6a_0+4a_1+a_2)}x^2+...\\[6pt]
    &&\textcolor{red}{1}&&\textcolor{blue}{0}x&&\textcolor{green}{-\frac{k^2}{2}}x^2+...
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    and form the following equations
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        a_0&=1&&&&4a_0+a_1=0&&&&6a_0+4a_1+a_2=-\frac{k^2}{2}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Using $a_0=1$ with the second equation we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    4(1)+a_1&=0\\[6pt]
    a_1&=-4
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Finally, using those values for $a_0$ and $a_1$, we can find $a_2$ in terms of $k$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    6(1)+4(-4)+a_2&=-\frac{k^2}{2}\\[6pt]
    6-16+a_2&=-\frac{k^2}{2}\\[6pt]
    a_2&=10-\frac{k^2}{2}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    In the question we are told that the coefficient of the expansion of $h$ is zero. Therefore $a_2=0$. Hence we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    0&=10-\frac{k^2}{2}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}\\[12pt]
    \frac{k^2}{2}&=10\\[12pt]
    k&=\pm\sqrt{20}\\[12pt]
    &=\pm2\sqrt{5}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
