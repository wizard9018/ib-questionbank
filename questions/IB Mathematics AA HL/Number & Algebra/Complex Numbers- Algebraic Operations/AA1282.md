# AA1282

**Maximum mark: 14**

The complex number $z$ is a root of the equation $|z+4i|=|z-10i|$.

1.  Show that the imaginary part of $z$ is $3$.[2 marks]

2.  Let $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ be two possible values of $z$ such that $|z|=6$.

    1.  If $\omega_1$ is in the first quadrant sketch both solutions on an Argand diagram.

    2.  Hence find the arguments of $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$.[4 marks]

A different complex number, $v$, is defined such that
$$
\begin{aligned}
    v&=\dfrac{\omega_{1}^k\,\omega_2}{-i}
\end{aligned}
$$
Where $k$ is a real number that can take any value in the interval $-10\leq k \leq 10$ and $-\pi \leq \arg{(-i)} \leq \pi$.

3.  1.  Find $\arg{(v)}$ in terms of $k$ and $\pi$.

    2.  Hence find all possible values of $k$ such that $v$ is a real number.[8 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  We are told $z$ is a complex number, therefore $z$ is of the form $a+bi$ where $a,b \in \mathbb{R}$.

    Hence we can rewrite the equation in terms of $a$ and $b$ to get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        |a+bi+4i|&=|a+bi-10i|
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    If we collect together the real and imaginary parts it is easier to consider the magnitude of the number
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        |a+i(b+4)|&=|a+i(b-10)|
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Recall the magnitude of a complex number $|a+bi|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \sqrt{a^2+(b+4)^2}&=\sqrt{a^2+(b-10)^2}\\[6pt]
        a^2+(b+4)^2&=a^2+(b-10)^2\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{M1}}}\\[6pt]
        \cancel{a^2}+\cancel{b^2}+8b+16&=\cancel{a^2}+\cancel{b^2}-20b+100
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    We can find $b$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        28b&=84\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}\\[6pt]
        b&=3
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Therefore $\text{Im}\,(z)=3$ as required.

2.  1.  The red dashed circle of radius $6$ represents the fact that $|z|=6$. Therefore $z$ must lie somewhere on this circle.

        The blue horizontal line shows the possible values of $z$ when $\text{Im}\,(z)=3$, with $\omega_1$ being in the first quadrant.

        $z$ must lie on both the red circle and blue line

        ![AA1282-a1](images/AA1282-a1.JPEG)

        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            &\text{$\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ both have imaginary values of $3$}.\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}\\[6pt]
            &\text{Correct quadrants for $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$}.\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

    2.  Using trigonometric ratios we can find $\arg{(\omega_1)}$

        ![AA1282-a2](images/AA1282-a2.JPEG)

        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            \sin{[\arg{(\omega_1)}]}&=\frac{3}{6}\\[6pt]
            {[\arg{(\omega_1)}]}&=\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\\[6pt]
            \textcolor{red}{\arg{(\omega_1)}}&=\textcolor{black}{\dfrac{\pi}{6}}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Using the symmetries of the diagram
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            \sin{[\textcolor{blue}{\arg{(\omega_2)}}]}&=\pi-\sin{[\textcolor{red}{\arg{(\omega_1)}}]}\\[6pt]
            &=\textcolor{black}{\dfrac{5\pi}{6}}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

3.  1.  In this question we are combining complex numbers and working with their arguments.

        For questions of this nature it is most efficient to use Euler's form of a complex number.

        Recall
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            z&=re^{\normalsize i\theta}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Where $r$ is the magnitude of the number and $\theta$ the argument.

        Hence, using our answers from part (b) we can write
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            \omega_1&=6e^{\normalsize i\tfrac{\pi}{6}}\\[6pt]
            \omega_2&=6e^{\normalsize i\tfrac{5\pi}{6}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

        If we raise $\omega_1$ to the power $k$, using De Moivre's theorem, we get
        $$
         \begin{aligned}
            \omega_1&=6^{\normalsize k}e^{\normalsize i\tfrac{k\pi}{6}}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(M1)}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

        Next we need to consider how to write $-i$ in Euler's form.

        Here is a sketch of $-i$

        ![AA1282-a3](images/AA1282-a3.JPEG)

        We can see that the magnitude is $1$ and the argument is $-\tfrac{\pi}{2}$, angles measured in the clockwise direction are negative and we are told in the question that $-\pi \leq \arg{(-i)} \leq \pi$.

        Hence we get
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            -i&=e^{\normalsize -i\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(A1)}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

        Putting these results together for $v$ we get
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            v&=\dfrac{\omega_{1}^k\,\omega_2}{-i}\\[6pt]
            &=\dfrac{6^ke^{\normalsize i\textcolor{red}{\tfrac{k\pi}{6}}} \times 6e^{\normalsize i\textcolor{blue}{\tfrac{5\pi}{6}}}}{e^{\normalsize -i\textcolor{green}{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}}}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{M1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

        Recall we want to find the argument of $v$, hence we need to simplify our expression so that it is in standard Euler's form $\rightarrow z=re^{i\theta}$.

        We can now see the advantage of using Euler's form. To combine the angles we need to resolve the powers of $e$ using basic index laws
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            &=6\times6^k e^{\normalsize i\textcolor{red}{\tfrac{k\pi}{6}}+\textcolor{blue}{\tfrac{5\pi}{6}}-\left(\textcolor{green}{-\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\right)}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{M1}}}\\[6pt]
             &=6^{k+1} e^{\normalsize i\textcolor{red}{\tfrac{k\pi}{6}}+\textcolor{blue}{\tfrac{5\pi}{6}}+\textcolor{green}{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}}\\[6pt]
             &=6^{k+1} e^{\normalsize i\textcolor{red}{\tfrac{k\pi}{6}}+\textcolor{blue}{\tfrac{5\pi}{6}}+\textcolor{green}{\tfrac{3\pi}{6}}}\\[6pt]
             &=6^{k+1} e^{\normalsize i\textcolor{black}{\tfrac{k\pi}{6}}+\textcolor{black}{\tfrac{8\pi}{6}}}\\[6pt]
             &=6^{k+1} e^{\normalsize i\tfrac{\pi}{6}(k+8)}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        By looking at the exponent of $e$ we can find the argument of $v$ in terms of $k$ and $\pi$, hence
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            \arg{(v)}&=\dfrac{\pi}{6}\left(k+8\right)\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

    2.  $v$ is a real number when it lies entirely on the real axis (Re $z$).

        Therefore its argument must be a multiple of $\pi$. Note $0$ is a multiple of $\pi$.

        We can form an equation for the argument. We aren't sure how many multiples of $\pi$ we'll need so we can pick a few to get us started
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            \dfrac{\pi}{6}\left(k+8\right)&=... -2\pi,\hspace{1em}-\pi,\hspace{1em}0,\hspace{1em}\pi,\hspace{1em}2\pi...\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(M1)}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        We can now multiply by $6$ and divide by $\pi$ to get
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            k+8&=... -12,\hspace{1em}-6,\hspace{1em}0,\hspace{1em}6,\hspace{1em}12...\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Subtracting $8$ from each answer we get
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            k&=...-20,\hspace{1em}-14,\hspace{1em}-8,\hspace{1em}-2,\hspace{1em}4...
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Recall that $-10\leq k \leq 10$.

        Hence we reject the first two answers. We may have missed one on the positive side.
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            \frac{\pi}{6}(k+8)&=3\pi\\[6pt]
            k+8&=18\\[6pt]
            k&=10
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Hence all answers in the interval for $k$ are
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            k&=-8, -2, 4, 10\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
