# AA1331

**Maximum mark: 18**

Consider the equation $z^4+4=0$, where $z \in \mathbb{C}$.

The roots of the equation are $z_1, z_2, z_3$ and $z_4$.

Where $\text{Im}(z_2)>0$ and $\text{Re}(z_3)<\text{Re}(z_4)$.

1.
    1.    Verify that $z_1=\sqrt{2}e^{\text{i}\frac{\pi}{4}}$ is a root of the equation.

    2.    Find $z_2, z_3$ and $z_4$ expressing each in the form $re^{\text{i}\theta}$ where $r,\theta>0$.

    3.    Express $z_1$ in the form $a+b\text{i}$, where $a,b \in \mathbb{R}$.

    4.    Plot the point Z, which represents the root $z_1$, on an Argand diagram.

    5.    Hence, write down $z_2, z_3$ and $z_4$ in Cartesian form. [7 marks]

Consider the equation $\left(\dfrac{w+1}{w-1}\right)^4+4=0$ where $w \in \mathbb{C}$ and $w \neq 1$.

2.
    1.    Using $z_1$, show that one of the roots of the equation is $w_1=1-2\text{i}$.

    2.    Hence, find the remaining roots of the equation in Cartesian form. [8 marks]

On an Argand diagram, the roots of the equation are represented by the points A, B, C and D.

3.    Find the area of the quadrilateral ABCD. [3 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  1. The command term _verify_ means we can substitute the given root into the equation to confirm it is a solution.
        Hence, we need to show that
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \left(\sqrt{2}e^{\text{i}\frac{\pi}{4}}\right)^4+4&=0
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Let's simplify the L.H.S.
        Rewriting the indices, we get
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \left(\sqrt{2}e^{\text{i}\frac{\pi}{4}}\right)^4+4&=(\sqrt{2})^4e^{\text{i}\pi}+4\tag*{\textbf{{M1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        The number $e^{\text{i}\pi}$ has an argument of $\pi$ radians and a modulus of $1$.
        By making a sketch of $e^{\text{i}\pi}$ on an Argand diagram, we can see that  $e^{\text{i}\pi}=-1$.

        ![AA1331-a1](images/AA1331-a1.JPEG)

        Using this, we can simplify further to obtain
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        &=(\sqrt{2})^4(-1)+4\tag*{\textbf{A1}}\\[6pt]
        &=2^2 \times (-1)+4\\[6pt]
        &=-4+4\\[6pt]
        &=0
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Thus, we have verified that $\sqrt{2}e^{\text{i}\frac{\pi}{4}}$ is a root.

    2. To find the other roots, we can rearrange the equation and solve for $z$
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        z^4+4&=0\\[6pt]
        z^4&=-4
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Let's write $-4$ in Euler's form, using the method we described in part (a)(i)
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        z^4+4&=0\\[6pt]
        z^4&=-4\\[6pt]
        &=4e^{\text{i}\pi}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        The fundamental theorem of algebra tells us that a complex number of the form $z^n=0$ has $n$ roots.

        Therefore, to find all $4$ roots of $z$ we take our previous result and add $2n\pi$ to the argument, where $n=0,1,2,3$, to represent each of the symmetrically distributed roots of $z$.
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        z^4&=4e^{\text{i}(\pi+2n\pi)}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Taking the fourth root of both sides and using index laws we get
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        z&=\left(4e^{\text{i}(\pi+2n\pi)}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\\[6pt]
        &=4^{\frac{1}{4}} \left(e^{\text{i} (\pi+2n\pi)}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\\[6pt]
        &=\sqrt{2}e^{\text{i}\frac{(\pi+2n\pi)}{4}}\tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Notice when $n=0$ we get the root $z_1$,  previously verified in part (a).

        Hence, we can find the remaining three roots by substituting in $n=1,2$ and $3$.
        $$
        \begin{aligned}    z_2&=\sqrt{2}e^{\text{i} \frac{3\pi}{4} }&&&z_3&=\sqrt{2}e^{\text{i} \frac{5\pi}{4} }&&&z_4&=\sqrt{2}e^{\text{i} \frac{7\pi}{4} }\tag*{\textbf{A1}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

    3. We have expressed the number in Euler's form
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        re^{\text{i}\theta} \rightarrow \sqrt{2}e^{\text{i}\frac{\pi}{4}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        where $r$ is the modulus and $\theta$ the argument, therefore
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \text{modulus}&=\sqrt{2}&&&\text{argument}&=\frac{\pi}{4}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Hence we can write the number in $r\,\text{cis}\theta$ form which simplifies to the desired form.
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        z_1&=\sqrt{2}\left(\cos{\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}+\text{i}\sin{\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}\right)\\[6pt]
        &=\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}+\text{i}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)\tag*{\textbf{A1}}\\[6pt]
        &=1+\text{i}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

    4. As $\text{Re}(z_1)=1$ and the $\text{Im}(z_1)=1$ we can plot the point $\mathrm{Z}(1,1)$ on an Argand diagram

        ![AA1331-a2](images/AA1331-a2.JPEG)

        $$
        \begin{aligned}z_1\text{ plotted at }\mathrm{Z}(1,1)\tag*{\textbf{A1}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

    5.  Note, writing a number in Cartesian form means expressing it as $a+b\text{i}$, where $a,b \in \mathbb{R}$. This is the same form as our answer from part (a)(iii).

        As we know the roots are symmetrically distributed about the origin on an Argand diagram we can (optionally) add them to our diagram from the previous part

        ![AA1331-a3](images/AA1331-a3.JPEG)

        this visual helps us write down the Cartesian form of the remaining roots
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        z_2&=-1+\text{i}&z_3&=-1-\text{i}&z_4&=1-\text{i}\tag*{\textbf{A1}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

2.  1.  In this question we must __show that__ $1-2\text{i}$ is a root. We cannot verify (by substitution) like in part (a)!

        Make sure you understand the A.A. command terms.

        Note, that the given equation
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \left(\frac{w+1}{w-1}\right)^4+4&=0
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        is of the form $z^4+4=0$.
        Hence we can say that
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \frac{w+1}{w-1}&=z\tag*{\textbf{{M1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        We have the $4$ roots to the equation $z^4+4=0$ from part (a) hence, if we can write $w$ in terms of $z$, we can find the roots of $w$.
        Let's make $w$ the subject of the above
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        w+1&=zw-z\\[6pt]
        1+z&=zw-w\\[6pt]
        1+z&=w(z-1)\\[6pt]
        w&=\frac{z+1}{z-1}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        As instructed, we use $z_1=1+\text{i}$
        Substituting $z=1+\text{i}$ we get
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        w&=\frac{1+\text{i}+1}{1+\text{i}-1}\\[6pt]
        &=\frac{2+\text{i}}{\text{i}}\tag*{\textbf{{M1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        To rewrite $w$ into the desired form we multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator $\dfrac{-\text{i}}{-\text{i}}$
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        &=\frac{2+\text{i}}{\text{i}}\times \frac{-\text{i}}{-\text{i}}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}\\[6pt]
        &=\frac{-2\text{i}-\text{i}^2}{-(-1)}\\[6pt]
        &=\frac{-2\text{i}-(-1)}{1}\\[6pt]
        &=1-2\text{i}\hspace{2em}\text{... as required}\tag*{\textbf{{AG}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

    2. Note, before starting this part recall the equation we are solving
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \left(\frac{w+1}{w-1}\right)^4+4&=0
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        By multiplying throughout by $(w-1)^4$ we can (optionally) rewrite it as
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        (w+1)^4+4(w-1)^4&=0
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        This is useful as we can see, when fully expanded, we would get a real polynomial in $w$, i.e. all of the coefficients of $w$ are real, this means we can expect the roots to be in conjugate pairs.
        If we let the root we found in the previous part be $w_1$ then we can find the remaining roots of $w$ by using the remaining roots of $z$.
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        w_2&=\frac{z_2+1}{z_2-1}&&&w_3&=\frac{z_3+1}{z_3-1}&&&w_4&=\frac{z_4+1}{z_4-1}\\[12pt]
        &=\frac{-1+\text{i}+1}{-1+\text{i}-1}&&&&=\frac{-1-\text{i}+1}{-1-\text{i}-1}&&&&=\frac{1-\text{i}+1}{1-\text{i}-1}\tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}\\[12pt]
        &=\frac{\text{i}}{-2+\text{i}}&&&&=\frac{-\text{i}}{-2-\text{i}}&&&&=\frac{2-\text{i}}{-\text{i}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        In a similar fashion to part (b)(i) we multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator giving
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        &=\frac{\text{i}}{-2+\text{i}}\times \frac{-2-\text{i}}{-2-\text{i}}&&&&=\frac{-\text{i}}{-2-\text{i}}\times \frac{-2+\text{i}}{-2+\text{i}} &&&&=\frac{2-\text{i}}{-\text{i}}\times \frac{-\text{i}}{-\text{i}}\\[12pt]
        &=\frac{-2\text{i}-\text{i}^2}{(-2)^2-\text{i}^2}&&&&=\frac{2\text{i}-\text{i}^2}{(-2)^2-\text{i}^2} &&&&=\frac{-2\text{i}+\text{i}^2}{(-\text{i})^2}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Recall $\text{i}^2=-1$
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        &=\frac{1-2\text{i}}{4-(-1)}&&&&=\frac{1+2\text{i}}{4-(-1)^2} &&&&=\frac{-2\text{i}+(-1)}{-1}\\[12pt]
        w_2&=\dfrac{1-2\text{i}}{5}&&&w_3&=\dfrac{1+2\text{i}}{5} &&&w_4&=1+2\text{i}\tag*{\textbf{{A1A1A1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Notice, $w_2=(w_3)^*$ and $w_1=(w_4)^*$, thus, as expected, the roots are in conjugate pairs.
        This is one way to verify your answers.

3.  Note, it doesn't matter which roots we label A, B, C or D as long as they are in the correct order to form a quadrilateral.
    Making a sketch of the roots on an Argand diagram and labelling the points ABCD we get

    ![AA1331-a4](images/AA1331-a4.JPEG)

    Notice that the points form a trapezoid (trapezium).
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \tag*{\textbf{{(R1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    The area of a trapezoid is calculated using the formula
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    A&=\frac{a+b}{2}\times h
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    where $a$ and $b$ are the lengths of the two parallel sides and $h$ is the perpendicular distance between them.
    Hence we need to find the lengths of AD and BC.
    Using the Imaginary (vertical) value for these points we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \text{AD}&=\frac{2}{5}-\left(-\frac{2}{5}\right)&\text{BC}&=2-(-2)\\[12pt]
    &=\frac{4}{5}&&=4
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    The height $h$ is the horizontal distance between points A or D and B or C, therefore
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    h&=1-\frac{1}{5}\\[12pt]
    &=\frac{4}{5}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Hence, the area of ABCD is
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    A_{\text{ABCD}}&=\frac{\frac{4}{5}+4}{2}\times \frac{4}{5}\tag*{\textbf{{M1}}}\\[12pt]
    &=\left(\frac{4}{5}+4\right)\times \frac{2}{5}\\[12pt]
    &=\frac{24}{5}\times \frac{2}{5}\\[6pt]
    &=\dfrac{48}{25}\hspace{0.5em}\text{units}^2\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}\\[12pt]
    \bigg(&=1\tfrac{23}{25}\bigg)
    \end{aligned}
    $$
