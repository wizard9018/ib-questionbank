# AA1297

**Maximum mark: 16**

Consider the planes $\Pi_1$, $\Pi_2$ and  $\Pi_3$ with the following equations.
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \Pi_1&:\hspace{1.8em}x-y+z=-4\\[3pt]
    \Pi_2&:\hspace{1.3em}2x+y-z=-1\\[3pt]
    \Pi_3&:\hspace{1em}-x+y+kz=-3
\end{aligned}
$$
Where $k \in \mathbb{R}$.

The system of equations that represents the three planes is inconsistent.

1.  1.  Find $k$.

    2.  Describe the geometrical relationship of the three planes.[3 marks]

$L$ is the line of intersection between $\Pi_1$ and $\Pi_2$ and it crosses the $xy-$plane at point $D$.

2.  1.  Verify that the vector equation of $L$ can be written as
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            \bm{r}=\begin{pmatrix*}[c] -\tfrac{5}{3} \\ 0 \\ -\tfrac{7}{3} \end{pmatrix*}+ \lambda \begin{pmatrix*}[c]0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix*}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

    2.  Hence find the coordinates of point $D$.[4 marks]

The point $D'$ is found by reflecting $D$ in $\Pi_3$.

3.  Find the coordinates of $D'$.  [9 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  1.  A system is inconsistent when there is no solution of any type - either a single solution or infinite solutions.

        We should use Guassian Elimination to attempt to solve the system in terms of $k$

        Let's begin by adding the equations for $\Pi_1$ and $\Pi_3$ this gives
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            x-y+z+(-x+y+kz)&=-4+(-3)\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(M1)}}}\\[6pt]
            z+kz&=-7\\[6pt]
            z(k+1)&=-7
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        We can see here that if $k=-1$ then we have $z \times 0=-7$ which leads to an impossible (inconsistent) equation to solve.

        Therefore when $k=-1$ the system is inconsistent.
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

    2.  There are a number of different geometrical arrangements of planes that could lead to an inconsistent solution.

        Let's check for parallelism first.

        If the L.H.S. of one plane is a multiple of the L.H.S. of another plane and the constants on the R.H.S.'s are different then the planes are parallel.

        Using $k=-1$ and rewriting $\Pi_3$, we get
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            \Pi_1&:\hspace{1.8em}x-y+z=-4\\[3pt]
            \Pi_3&:\hspace{1em}-(x-y+z)=-3
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        We can't do a similar manipulation with $\Pi_2$.

        $\text{Hence plane }\Pi_1\text{ and }\Pi_3\text{ are parallel}$

        and

        $\Pi_2\text{ is oblique as it is distinct to }\Pi_1\text{ and }\Pi_3\text{ and parallel to neither.}$
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \tag*{\textbf{R1}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

        Although not required by the question, here is a visual showing the two parallel planes and the oblique plane.

        ![AA1297-a1](images/AA1297-a1.JPEG)

2.  1.  There are a number of ways to answer this question.

        One way to check that the line is the intersection of both planes is to verify that the line lies in both planes for any value of $\lambda$.

        Hence we will substitute each component of line $L$ into the L.H.S. of $\Pi_1$ and $\Pi_2$ and ensure the result equals the R.H.S.
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \tag*{\textbf{M1}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

        First let's try $\Pi_1$
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            \Pi_1:\,\, \text{L.H.S.}&= x-y+z\\[6pt]
            &= \left(-\tfrac{5}{3}\right)-(\lambda)+\left(-\tfrac{7}{3}+\lambda\right)\\[6pt]
            &=-\tfrac{12}{4}\\[6pt]
            &=-4\\[6pt]
            &=\text{R.H.S.}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        As the L.H.S. equals the R.H.S. for any value of $\lambda$ this means $L$ lies in $\Pi_1$.

        Let's do the same again for $\Pi_2$
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            \Pi_2:\,\, \text{L.H.S.}&=  2x+y-z\\[6pt]
            &= 2\left(-\tfrac{5}{3}\right)+(\lambda)-\left(-\tfrac{7}{3}+\lambda\right)\\[6pt]
            &=-\tfrac{3}{3}\\[6pt]
            &=-1\\[6pt]
            &=\text{R.H.S.}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Hence $L$ also lies in $\Pi_2$.

        $\text{As }L\text{ lies in both }\Pi_1\text{ and }\Pi_2$
        and as
        $\Pi_1\text{ and }\Pi_2\text{ are not parallel it must be the line of intersection of both planes.}$

        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

    2.  The value of the $z-$component of a line that intersects the $xy-$plane is zero.

        This diagram can help you visualise it

        ![AA1297-a2](images/AA1297-a2.JPEG)

        We need to find the value of $\lambda$ that makes the $z-$component of $L$ equal zero.
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            r_z: \, -\tfrac{7}{3}+\lambda&=0\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(M1)}}}\\[6pt]
            \lambda&=\tfrac{7}{3}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Hence the coordinates of $D$ when $\lambda=\tfrac{7}{3}$ are
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            &D\left(-\tfrac{5}{3}+0,0+ \tfrac{7}{3} \times 1, -\tfrac{7}{3}+\tfrac{7}{3}\right)\\[6pt]
           &\boxed{D\left(-\tfrac{5}{3},\tfrac{7}{3},0 \right)}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

3.  Before we begin answering the question let's outline a method.

    We can make a rough sketch of the information to help us.

    The diagram below shows $\Pi_1$ and $\Pi_3$.

    $\Pi_2$ is not shown.

    ![AA1297-a3](images/AA1297-a3.JPEG)

    In order to find $D'$ we need to use point $D$.

    Due to the symmetry of reflection, the point $D'$ will lie on the line which is normal to plane $\Pi_1$ and goes through $D$.

    This is shown as the red line above.

    The point $X$ is the point on $\Pi_3$ which lies on the red line.

    Note that the vector from $D$ to $X$, shown in red, is equal to the vector from $X$ to $D'$, shown in blue.

    Hence, we can form an equation using displacement vectors such that
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \overrightarrow{DX}&=\overrightarrow{XD'}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    The will allow us to find the coordinates of $D'$.

    We can summarise this information into four steps.

    - Find the equation of the red line.

    - Find the position vector of $\overrightarrow{OX}$.

    - Solve the displacement vector equation above to find the coordinates of $D'$.

    Let's begin by finding the equation of the red line.

    Recall the Cartesian form of plane $1$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \Pi_1&:\hspace{1.8em}x-y+z=-4
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Written in this form, the coefficients of the L.H.S. give the normal, $\bm{n_1}$, to $\Pi_1$. Hence
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \bm{n_1}=\begin{pmatrix*}[c] 1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix*}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(A1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Therefore using the coordinates of $D$ and $\bm{n_1}$ as the direction we can write down the vector equation of the red line, $\bm{r_R}$, as
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \bm{r_R}=\begin{pmatrix*}[c] -\tfrac{5}{3} \\ \tfrac{7}{3} \\ 0 \end{pmatrix*}+\mu\begin{pmatrix*}[c] 1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix*}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(A1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    where $\mu \in \mathbb{R}$.

    The next step is to find the coordinates of $X$.

    For this we substitute the components of $\bm{r_R}$ into the plane equation for $\Pi_3$ and solve for $\mu$. Hence we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    -x+y-z&=-3\\[6pt]
        -\left(-\tfrac{5}{3}+\mu\right)+\left(\tfrac{7}{3}-\mu\right)-(\mu)&=-3\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(M1)}}}\\[6pt]
        \mu&=\tfrac{7}{3}\hspace{2em}\text{[solved on G.D.C.]}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Hence the position vector of $\overrightarrow{OX}$ is
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \overrightarrow{OX}&=\begin{pmatrix*}[c] -\tfrac{5}{3} \\ \tfrac{7}{3} \\ 0 \end{pmatrix*}+\left(\tfrac{7}{3}\right)\times\begin{pmatrix*}[c] 1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix*}\\[6pt]
    &=\begin{pmatrix*}[c] -\tfrac{5}{3}+\tfrac{7}{3} \\ \tfrac{7}{3}-\tfrac{7}{3} \\ \tfrac{7}{3} \end{pmatrix*}\\[6pt]
    &=\begin{pmatrix*}[c] \tfrac{2}{3} \\ 0 \\ \tfrac{7}{3} \end{pmatrix*}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(A1)}}}\\[6pt]
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Using the coordinates of $D\left(-\tfrac{5}{3},\tfrac{7}{3},0 \right)$ we can write down the position vector $\overrightarrow{OD}$ but first we can rewrite the equation mentioned earlier in terms of position vectors
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \overrightarrow{DX}&=\overrightarrow{XD'}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{M1}}}\\[6pt]
        \overrightarrow{OX}-\overrightarrow{OD}&=\overrightarrow{OD'}-\overrightarrow{OX}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Substituting in what we have found we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \begin{pmatrix*}[c] \tfrac{2}{3} \\ 0 \\ \tfrac{7}{3} \end{pmatrix*}-\begin{pmatrix*}[c] -\tfrac{5}{3} \\ \tfrac{7}{3} \\ 0 \end{pmatrix*}&=\overrightarrow{OD'}-\begin{pmatrix*}[c] \tfrac{2}{3} \\ 0 \\ \tfrac{7}{3} \end{pmatrix*}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    We can simplify and rearrange to find $\overrightarrow{OD'}$, giving
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \begin{pmatrix*}[c] \tfrac{7}{3} \\ -\tfrac{7}{3} \\ \tfrac{7}{3} \end{pmatrix*}&=\overrightarrow{OD'}-\begin{pmatrix*}[c] \tfrac{2}{3} \\ 0 \\ \tfrac{7}{3} \end{pmatrix*}\\[6pt]
        \overrightarrow{OD'}&=\begin{pmatrix*}[c] \tfrac{7}{3} \\ -\tfrac{7}{3} \\ \tfrac{7}{3} \end{pmatrix*}+\begin{pmatrix*}[c] \tfrac{2}{3} \\ 0 \\ \tfrac{7}{3} \end{pmatrix*}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}\\[6pt]
        &=\begin{pmatrix*}[c] 3 \\ -\tfrac{7}{3} \\ \tfrac{14}{3}\end{pmatrix*}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Hence the coordinates of point $D'$ are $\left(3,-\tfrac{7}{3},\frac{14}{3}\right)$.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
