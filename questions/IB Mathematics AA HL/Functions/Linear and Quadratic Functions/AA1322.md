# AA1322

**Maximum mark: 14**

Consider the function $f(x)=x^2+2bx+3$, where $b \in \mathbb{R}$ and $f$ has two distinct real roots.

1.    Find the range of values of $b$. [2 marks]

Consider another function $g(x)=x^2+2Ax+3$, where $A$ is a continuous random variable such that $A \sim N(0,1)$.

2.    Show that the probability that $g$ has two distinct real roots is $0.08326$ to four significant figures. [3 marks]

The two roots of $g$ are $x_1$ and $x_2$.

Given that $g$ has two distinct real roots, where $|x_1|<\sqrt{3}$ and $|x_2|>\sqrt{3}$,

3.    find the probability that $|x_1|<1$. [9 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1. Recall the discriminant, $\Delta$, of a quadratic equation
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \text{If}\hspace{1em}\,ax^2+bx+c&=0\\[6pt]
    \text{then}\hspace{4.7em}\Delta&=b^2-4ac
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    This tells us how many real roots the equation has.

    Consider the equation  $f(x)=0$. If $f$ has two distinct real roots then $\Delta > 0$.

    Hence, using the parameter values of $a = 1$, $b$ is  $2b$ and $c = 3$ we can form the inequality
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    (2b)^2-4(1)(3)& > 0\tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    which we can solve to find the range of values of $b$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    4b^2-12&> 0\\[6pt]
    4b^2&> 12\\[6pt]
    b^2&> 3\\[6pt]b < -\sqrt{3} &\hspace{0.525em}\text{ and }b> \sqrt{3}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

2. Function $g$ is closely related to $f$ the only difference is that parameter $b$ is now $A$.

    Therefore using our previous answer we can write down the range of values of $A$ for which $g$ has real roots
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    A &< -\sqrt{3}&A &> \sqrt{3}\tag*{\textbf{{(A1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Recall that $A$ is the continuous random variable such that $A \sim N(0,1)$.

    Hence we need to find the probability that $A <-\sqrt{3}$ or $A > \sqrt{3}$.

    This is a region on the standard normal distribution and is shown as the shaded regions below

    ![AA1322-a1](images/AA1322-a1.JPEG)

    Hence, the probability $g$ has real distinct roots is
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &=\mathrm{P}(A < -\sqrt{3}) + \mathrm{P}(A> \sqrt{3})\tag*{\textbf{{M1}}}\\[6pt]
    &=\text{normCDF}(-\infty,-\sqrt{3},0,1)+ \text{normCDF}(\sqrt{3},\infty,0,1)\\[6pt]
    &=0.041632...+0.041632...\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}\\[6pt]
    &=0.083264...\\[6pt]
    &=0.08326\text{...as required.}\tag*{\textbf{{AG}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

3. Consider the given inequality, we can write it without the absolute value signs
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    |x_1|&<1\\[6pt]
    -1<x_1&<1
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    This means we need to find the value(s) of $A$ such that the root $x_1$ is in the range described above.

    To find the roots, $x_1$ and $x_2$, of $g$ we can use the quadratic formula
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \text{If}\hspace{1em}\,ax^2+bx+c&=0\\[6pt]
    \text{then}\hspace{4.7em}x&=\dfrac{-b\pm \sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    where $a$ is  $1$, $b$ is $2A$ and $c$ is $3$ which gives
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    x_{1,2}&=\dfrac{-(2A)\pm \sqrt{(2A)^2-4(1)(3)}}{2(1)}\tag*{\textbf{M1}}\\[6pt]
    &=\dfrac{-2A\pm \sqrt{4A^2-12}}{2}\\[6pt]
    &=\dfrac{-2A\pm \sqrt{4(A^2-3)}}{2}\\[6pt]
    &=\dfrac{-\cancel{2}A\pm \cancel{2}\sqrt{A^2-3}}{\cancel{2}}\\[6pt]
    &=-A\pm \sqrt{A^2-3}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Hence the two roots of $g$ are
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &&\text{root $1$}&=-A - \sqrt{A^2-3} &\text{ and }&&\text{root $2$}&=-A + \sqrt{A^2-3}\tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    However, we can't be sure which one is $x_1$ and which is $x_2$!

    But we can use a sketch of the graph of root $1$ and root $2$ against $A$ to learn more.

    The following image shows both root $1$ and root $2$ against $A$.

    ![AA1322-double](images/AA1322-double.JPEG)

    Recall, $x_1$ is defined as the root which has a value of $-\sqrt{3}<x_1<\sqrt{3}$.

    Therefore, if $-\sqrt{3}<\text{root }1<\sqrt{3}$ or $-\sqrt{3}<\text{root }2<\sqrt{3}$ then that root is $x_1$.

    Hence when the curve is between the two purple horizontal lines then that root represents $x_1$.

    This question is asking us to find when $-1<x_1<1$. Hence we need to find when either root $1$ or root $2$ is between the two $\textcolor{red}{\text{red}}$ horizontal lines.

    The boundary values are $A_1$ for root $1$ and $A_2$ for root $2$.

    And we can see that $0<x_1<1$ when $A<A_1$ (shown by the $\textcolor{blue}{\text{blue}}$ arrow above) and $-1<x_1<0$ when $A>A_2$ (shown by the $\textcolor{green}{\text{green}}$ arrow above).

    Using a G.D.C. we find $A_1=-2$ and $A_2=2$.

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(A1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Let's combine these two regions by splitting the double inequality
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    -1<x_1<1
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    into two single inequalities. This gives
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &0<x_ 1<1&&&-1<x_1<0&\\[6pt]
    &\text{occurs when }\textcolor{blue}{A<-2}&&&\text{occurs when }\textcolor{green}{A>2}&\\[6pt]
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Therefore
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \mathrm{P}\big(|x_1|<1\big)&=\mathrm{P}\big( -1<x_1<1\big)\\[6pt]
    &=\mathrm{P}(A < -2) + \mathrm{P}(A> 2)\tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}\\[6pt]
    &=\text{normCDF}(-\infty,-2,0,1)+ \text{normCDF}(2,\infty,0,1)\\[6pt]
    &=0.02275...+0.02275...\\[6pt]
    &=0.04550...\tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    However, we haven't quite finished yet. We were also given some extra information that $g$ has two distinct roots.
    This is conditional probability for which we use the formula
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(R1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \mathrm{P}(A|B)&=\frac{\mathrm{P}(A \cap B)}{\mathrm{P}(B)}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Where event $A$ is $|x_1|<1$ and event $B$ is $g$ has two distinct roots.

    Replacing $A$ and $B$ in the formula we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \mathrm{P}\bigg(|x_1|<1\,\bigg|\,g\text{ has two distinct roots}\bigg)&=\frac{\mathrm{P}\bigg(|x_1|<1\, \cap \,g\text{ has two distinct roots}\bigg)}{\mathrm{P}\bigg(g\text{ has two distinct roots}\bigg)}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    We can use the answer from part (b) as the denominator
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &=\frac{\mathrm{P}\bigg(|x_1|<1\, \cap \,g\text{ has two distinct roots}\bigg)}{0.08326...}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    To evaluate the numerator, we can use our answer from part (a), from which we can write down the range of values of $A$ for which $g$ has two distinct roots
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    A &< -\sqrt{3}&A &> \sqrt{3}\\[6pt]
    A &< -1.732...&A &> 1.732...
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Recall our values for $A$ when $|x_1|<1$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    A &< -2&A &> 2
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Notice that the set of values of $A$ for when $|x_1|<1$ fall entirely within the set of values for which $g$ has two distinct roots.

    Hence the intersection of both events is simply the event the set of values when $|x_1|<1$, which we found earlier in this part.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(M1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Hence, the numerator becomes
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &=\frac{\mathrm{P}\bigg(|x_1|<1\, \cap \,g\text{ has two distinct roots}\bigg)}{0.08326...}\\[12pt]
    &=\frac{\mathrm{P}\bigg(|x_1|<1\bigg)}{0.08326...}\tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}\\[12pt]
    &=\frac{0.04550...}{0.08326...}\\[12pt]
    &=0.5464...\\[6pt]
    &=0.546\tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
