# AA1274

**Maximum mark: 23**

Consider the function $\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{\cos{x}}{2+\sin{x}}$ for $-\pi \leq x \leq \pi$.

1.  Evaluate $f(0)$.[1 mark]
2.  Find all possible values of $a$ if $f(a)=0$.[2 marks]

3.  1.  Show that $\displaystyle f'(x)=-\frac{2\sin{x}+1}{(2+\sin{x})^2}$.

    2.  Hence find the $x-$coordinates of any stationary points of $f$.[7 marks]

4.  Given that $\displaystyle f''(x)=-\frac{2\cos{x}(1-\sin{x})}{(2+\sin{x})^3}$ find the nature of any stationary points of $f$.[5 marks]

5.  Hence sketch the graph of $f$, clearly showing the values of the axes intercepts and the $x-$coordinates of any stationary points.[3 marks]

The function $f$ is positive and decreasing in the region $s<x<t$.

The area enclosed by $f$ and the $x-$axis from $x=s$ to $x=t$ is $\ln{c}$ where $c \in \mathbb{Z}$.

6.  Find $c$.[5 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  To evaluate the function we substitute in $x=0$ which gives
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        f(0)&=\frac{\cos{0}}{2+\sin{0}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Using the fact that $\cos{0}=1$ and $\sin{0}=0$ we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        &=\frac{1}{2+0}\\[6pt]
        &=\dfrac{1}{2} \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
2.  Using $x=a$ and the function $f$ we can form an equation
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        f(a)&=\frac{\cos{a}}{2+\sin{a}}\\[12pt]
        &=0
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Only the numerator can provide solutions to the equation, hence we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \cos{a}&=0\tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Recall, the domain of $f$ is $-\pi \leq x \leq \pi$, we can use a sketch of $\cos{x}$ to find all solutions of $a$ in that domain

    ![AA1274-a1](images/AA1274-a1.JPEG)

    We know that the principal angle of $\cos{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}$ is $0$, which is shown in $\textcolor{green}{\text{green}}$, and using the symmetries of the cosine curve we can see that $-\tfrac{\pi}{2}$, shown in $\textcolor{red}{\text{red}}$, is also a solution.

    Hence $\displaystyle a=\pm\frac{\pi}{2}$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

3.  1.  To differentiate $f$ we need to use the quotient rule.
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

        For this we need the derivative of both the numerator and the denominator
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            &&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(\cos{x})&= -\sin{x}&\hspace{-1em} &&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(2+\sin{x})&=\cos{x}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Now we can use these results and the quotient rule to form an expression for $f'(x)$
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            f'(x)&=\frac{(2+\sin{x})(-\sin{x})-(\cos{x})(\cos{x})}{(2+\sin{x})^2}\tag*{\textbf{A1A1}}\\[12pt]
            &=\frac{-2\sin{x}-\sin^2{x}-\cos^2{x}}{(2+\sin{x})^2}\\[12pt]
            &=\frac{-2\sin{x}-(\sin^2{x}+\cos^2{x})}{(2+\sin{x})^2}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Notice we can use a trigonometric identity to simplify the numerator
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \tag*{\textbf{M1}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            &=\frac{-2\sin{x}-1}{(2+\sin{x})^2}\\[12pt]\
            &=-\dfrac{2\sin{x}+1}{(2+\sin{x})^2}   \hspace{2em}\text{... as required.}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
    2.  To find any stationary points we must solve $f'(x)=0$, hence we can form an equation using the result from part (c)(i)
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            -\frac{2\sin{x}+1}{(2+\sin{x})^2}&=0\tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        We only need to consider the numerator, hence we get
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            -2\sin{x}-1&=0\\[6pt]
            \sin{x}&=-\frac{1}{2}\tag*{\textbf{(A1)}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

        The principal solution (this means the solution in the first quadrant) is $x=\frac{\pi}{6}$.

        However, if we make a sketch of $\sin{x}$ with the same domain as $f$ we can see that the solutions to $\sin{x}=-\tfrac{1}{2}$ are both negative

        ![AA1274-a22](images/AA1274-a22.JPEG)

        Due to the symmetries of the graph we can see that the two angles, $x_1$ and $x_2$, marked in $\textcolor{red}{\text{red}}$ are
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            &&x_1&= -\pi + \frac{\pi}{6}&\hspace{-1em} && x_2&= 0-\frac{\pi}{6}& \\[6pt]
            &&&= -\frac{5\pi}{6}&\hspace{-1em} && x_2&=-\frac{\pi}{6}&
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Hence there are two stationary points with $x-$coordinates of $x=-\dfrac{5\pi}{6}$ and $x=-\dfrac{\pi}{6}$.
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

4.  We can use the second derivative to determine the nature of the stationary points.

    If $f''(x)>0$ the point is a minimum and if $f''(x)<0$ it is a maximum.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Let's first test the point $x=-\tfrac{\pi}{6}$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        f''(-\tfrac{\pi}{6})&=-\frac{2\cos{(-\tfrac{\pi}{6})}(1-\sin{(-\tfrac{\pi}{6})})}{(2+\sin{(-\tfrac{\pi}{6})})^3}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    To evaluate the expression above we need the exact values of $\sin{(-\tfrac{\pi}{6})}$ and $\cos{(-\tfrac{\pi}{6})}$.

    From previous work we know that $\sin{(-\tfrac{\pi}{6})}=-\tfrac{1}{2}$.

    We can use the symmetry of the cosine curve

    ![AA1274-a3](images/AA1274-a3.JPEG)

    to realise that $\cos{(-\tfrac{\pi}{6})}=\cos{(\tfrac{\pi}{6})}$.

    Hence $\cos{(-\tfrac{\pi}{6})}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$.

    Now let's substitute the two values into the earlier expression we had $f''(x)$ to get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        f''(-\tfrac{\pi}{6})&=-\frac{2\times\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\times(1-(-\tfrac{1}{2}))}{(2+(-\tfrac{1}{2}))^3}\tag*{\textbf{M1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    We don't need to evaluate the expression exactly we just need to know if it is positive or negative, let's do a little simplification
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        &=-\frac{2\times\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}(1+\tfrac{1}{2})}{(\tfrac{3}{2})^3}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    We can now see that quotient will result in a positive value. Hence, as the entire quotient is being multiplied by $-1$, the result is negative.

    Therefore
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        f''(-\tfrac{\pi}{6})&<0
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Hence $x=-\tfrac{\pi}{6}\text{ is a maximum value}$.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    We can use a similar process for the other stationary point $x=-\tfrac{5\pi}{6}$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        f''(-\tfrac{5\pi}{6})&=-\frac{2\cos{(-\tfrac{5\pi}{6})}(1-\sin{(-\tfrac{5\pi}{6})})}{(2+\sin{(-\tfrac{5\pi}{6})})^3}\tag*{\textbf{M1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    From previous work we know that $\sin{(-\tfrac{5\pi}{6})}=-\tfrac{1}{2}$.

    And using the symmetries of the cosine curve we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \cos{(-\tfrac{5\pi}{6})}&=\cos{(\tfrac{5\pi}{6})}\\[6pt]
        &=-\cos{(\tfrac{\pi}{6})}\\[6pt]
        &=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    We can now substitute these two values into the second derivative to get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        f''(-\tfrac{5\pi}{6})&=-\frac{2\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)(1-(-\tfrac{1}{2}))}{(2+(-\tfrac{1}{2})^3}\\[12pt]
        &=-\frac{-2\sqrt{3}(1+\tfrac{1}{2})}{(\tfrac{3}{2})^3}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    We can see that the quotient will now be negative and hence the overall value will be positive.

    Therefore
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        f''(-\tfrac{5\pi}{6})&>0
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Hence $x=-\tfrac{5\pi}{6}\text{ is a minimum value}$.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

6.  We need to identity the region in which $f$ is positive (above the $x-$axis) and is decreasing (which means the gradient is negative).

    This region is the highlighted red part of $f$ shown below

    ![AA1274-a7](images/AA1274-a7.JPEG)

    Hence we can see that the value of $s=-\frac{\pi}{6}$ and $t=\frac{\pi}{2}$.

    To find the area under the curve between these two values we need to evaluate the definite integral of $f$ from $-\frac{\pi}{6}$ to $\frac{\pi}{2}$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \text{Area}&=\int_{\textstyle -\frac{\pi}{6}}^{\textstyle \frac{\pi}{2}}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x  \\[12pt]
        &=\int_{\textstyle -\frac{\pi}{6}}^{\textstyle \frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\cos{x}}{2+\sin{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x \tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    This integral is a quotient. Often a good strategy to integrate a quotient is to use a substitution for the denominator.

    Hence let $u=2+\sin{x}$.

    To rewrite the integral we will need an expression for $\mathrm{d}x$ in terms of $\mathrm{d}u$, hence
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}&=\cos{x}\\[12pt]
        \mathrm{d}x&=\frac{1}{\cos{x}}\mathrm{d}u
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Let's now replace the original integral so that it is in terms of $u$. We will omit the limits for now.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \int \frac{\cos{x}}{2+\sin{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x &=\int \frac{\cancel{\cos{x}}}{u}\times\frac{1}{\cancel{\cos{x}}}\mathrm{d}u\\[12pt]
        &=\int \frac{1}{u}\mathrm{d}u\\[12pt]
        &=\ln|u|+C\tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Let's now substitute $u=2+\sin{x}$ and reintroduce the limits.

    As the integral is now definite we can omit the constant.

    This gives
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \text{Area}&=\bigg[\ln{|2+\sin{x}|}\bigg]_{\textstyle -\frac{\pi}{6}}^{\textstyle \frac{\pi}{2}}\tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    As $2+\sin{x}>0$ for all $x$ we can remove the absolute value signs.

    Substituting in the upper and lower limit we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        &=\ln{(2+\sin{\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)})}-\ln{(2+\sin{\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{6}\right)})}\tag*{\textbf{M1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Recall $\sin{\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)}=1$ and $\sin{\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{6}\right)}=-\tfrac{1}{2}$, hence we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        &=\ln{(2+1)}-\ln{(2+-(\tfrac{1}{2}))}\\[6pt]
        &=\ln{3}-\ln{\tfrac{3}{2}}\\[6pt]
        &=\ln{\tfrac{3}{\tfrac{3}{2}}}\\[6pt]
        &=\ln{2}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Hence $c=2$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
