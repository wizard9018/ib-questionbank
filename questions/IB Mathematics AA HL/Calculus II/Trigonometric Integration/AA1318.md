# AA1318

**Maximum mark: 14**

1.    

    1.    Expand and simplify $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}-k\right)^3$ in ascending powers of $k$. 

    2.    By considering a double angle identity, use a suitable substitution

for $k$ to show that $\cos^3{2x}=1-6\sin^2{x} +12\sin^4{x}-8\sin^6{x}$ [6 marks]

Consider the function $f$ such that $f(x)=16\sin{2x}\left(1-6\sin^2{x} +12\sin^4{x}-8\sin^6{x}\right)$.

2.    

    1.    Show that $\displaystyle \int_{0}^{a}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=2-2\cos^4{2a}$, where $a \in \mathbb{R}^+.$ 

    

    2.    It is given that $\displaystyle \int_{\textstyle \frac{\pi}{6}}^{a}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=-1$ where $\tfrac{\pi}{6}<a<\frac{\pi}{2}$.

    

        Find $a$. [8 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  1. Using the binomial theorem

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        (a+b)^n&=a^n+\binom{n}{1}a^{n-1}b+...+\binom{n}{r}a^{n-r}b^r+...+b^n\tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        with $a=\tfrac{1}{2}$, $b=-k$ and $n=3$ we expand $\left(\frac{1}{2}-k\right)^3$ to get

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        &=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3+\binom{3}{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}(-k)+\binom{3}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1}(-k)^2+\binom{3}{3}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{0}(-k)^3

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        We can use the third row of Pascal's triangle to help us.

        ![AA1318a0](images/AA1318a0.JPEG)

        Replacing the binomial coefficients with the values in the third row and simplifying we get

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        &=\frac{1}{8}+3\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)(-k)+3\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\times k^2-k^3\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}\\[12pt]

        &=\dfrac{1}{8}-\dfrac{3k}{4}+\dfrac{3k^2}{2}-k^3

        \end{aligned}

        $$

    2. The given relation has both a $\cos{2x}$ term and $\sin^2{x}$ terms.

        Considering the trigonometric identities in the formula booklet we can relate the two terms using

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \cos{2x}&=1-2\sin^2{x}\tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Dividing this by $2$ we get

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \frac{\cos{2x}}{2}&=\frac{1}{2}-\sin^2{x}\\[9pt]

        &=\left(\dfrac{1}{2}-k\right)

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Comparing the R.H.S. to the binomial in part (a)(i) we can see that $k=\sin^2{x}$ would be a suitable substitution.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \tag*{\textbf{{(A1)}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Hence using the answer from part (a) with $k=\sin^2{x}$ we get

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \left(\dfrac{1}{2}-\sin^2{x}\right)^3&=\frac{1}{8}-\frac{3(\sin^2{x})}{4}+\frac{3(\sin^2{x})^2}{2}-(\sin^2{x})^3

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Simplifying the R.H.S. we get

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        &=\frac{1}{8}-\frac{3\sin^2{x}}{4}+\frac{3\sin^4{x}}{2}-\sin^6{x}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Now, considering the L.H.S., we can use the identity to rewrite the contents of the bracket, to get

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \left(\dfrac{1}{2}-\sin^2{x}\right)^3&=\left(\dfrac{\cos{2x}}{2}\right)^3\\[12pt]

        &=\dfrac{\cos^3{2x}}{8}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Putting the two results together we have

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \dfrac{\cos^3{2x}}{8}&=\frac{1}{8}-\frac{3\sin^2{x}}{4}+\frac{3\sin^4{x}}{2}-\sin^6{x}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Multiplying by $8$ we get

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \cos^3{2x}&=1-\frac{3\sin^2{x}}{4} \times 8+\frac{3\sin^4{x}}{2}\times 8-\sin^6{x}\times 8\\[6pt]

        &\hspace{-3.4em}\cos^3{2x}=1-6\sin^2{x} +12\sin^4{x}-8\sin^6{x}\hspace{1em}\text{...as required}\tag*{\textbf{{AG}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

2.  1. The first thing to notice in this question is that part of the function $f$ is the same as the R.H.S. of the relation from part (a)(ii).

        It makes sense to replace this part of $f$ with the L.H.S. of the relation in part (a)(ii) as this will make $f$ much simpler.

        This gives

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        f(x)&=16\sin{2x}\cos^3{2x}\tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        To integrate this expression we can first notice that it is of the form

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        f(x)&=g'(x)\times [g(x)]^n

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        That means we can use a substitution of $u=g(x)$.

        Hence, for $f$, we use $u=\cos{2x}$.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        

        We can find an expression for $\mathrm{d}x$

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}&=-2\sin{2x}\\[9pt]

        \mathrm{d}x&=-\frac{1}{2\sin{2x}}\mathrm{d}u

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Rewriting the integral in terms of $u$, without limits for now, we get

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        &=\int16\sin{2x} \times u^3\times -\frac{1}{2\sin{2x}}\mathrm{d}u

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Assuming we picked the correct substitution, the functions of $x$ should cancel out

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        &=\int16\cancel{\sin{2x}} \times u^3\times -\frac{1}{2\cancel{\sin{2x}}}\mathrm{d}u\\[12pt]

        &=\int-8u^3\,\mathrm{d}u

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        and they do!

        We can now integrate this function of $u$, with respect to $u$, using the inverse power rule to get

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        &=\frac{-8}{4}u^4\\[9pt]

        &=-2u^4\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Now we can reintroduce the limits, factorise the $-2$, and write the result in terms of $x$

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        &=-2\bigg[\cos^4{2x}\bigg]_0^a

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Substituting in the limits we get

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        &=-2\bigg[\cos^4{2(a)}-(\cos^4{2(0)})\bigg]\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Recall $\cos{0}=1$.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        &=-2\bigg[\cos^4{2(a)}-1\bigg]\\[12pt]

        &=2-2\cos^4{2a}\hspace{1em}\text{...as required}\tag*{\textbf{{AG}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

    2. In this question we need to find the value of $a$ such that the definite integral is equal to $-1$.

        To do this we can form an equation, using our previous result, which we can solve to find $a$.

        But first let's rewrite the integral into a form so that we can use our previous answer.

        As $a>\tfrac{\pi}{6}$ we can say

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \int_{\textstyle \frac{\pi}{6}}^{a}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x&=\int_{0}^{a}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{0}^{\textstyle \frac{\pi}{6}}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Using the answer from part (b)(i) we get

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        &=(2-2\cos^4{2a})-\left(2-2\cos^4{\left(\frac{2\pi}{6}\right)}\right)\tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Recall $\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right)=\frac{1}{2}$.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        &=2-2\cos^4{2a}-2+2\times \frac{1}{16}\\[6pt]

        &=\frac{1}{8}-2\cos^4{2a}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        We can now use this simplified result to form an equation which we can solve to find $a$.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        -1&=\frac{1}{8}-2\cos^4{2a}\\[12pt]

        2\cos^4{2a}&=\frac{9}{8}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \cos^4{2a}&=\frac{9}{16}\\[12pt]

        \cos{2a}&=\sqrt[\scriptsize{4}]{\frac{9}{16}}\\[12pt]

        &=\pm\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        The principal angle for $\cos{\theta}=\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ is $\theta=\dfrac{\pi}{6}$.

        Using a sketch (or the unit circle) of the cosine curve along with the $\textcolor{blue}{\text{principal}}$ value we can identify the $\textcolor{red}{\text{other}}$ solutions.

        ![AA1318a1](images/AA1318a1.JPEG)

        From these values we can find four possible values of $a$.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        2a&=\frac{\pi}{6},&\frac{5\pi}{6},&&\frac{7\pi}{6},&&\frac{11\pi}{6}\\[9pt]

        a&=\frac{\pi}{12},&\frac{5\pi}{12},&&\frac{7\pi}{12},&&\frac{11\pi}{12}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Recall the interval for $a$,

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \frac{\pi}{6}&<a<\frac{\pi}{2}\\[12pt]

        \frac{2\pi}{12}&<a<\frac{6\pi}{12}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Using this we can identify the only valid value for $a$. Therefore

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        a&=\dfrac{5\pi}{12}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$
