# AA1379

**Maximum mark: 10**

Consider the differential equation $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} - \dfrac{2}{x}\,y = x^2 e^{x}$.

1. Show that $\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ is an integrating factor for this differential equation.[4 marks]

2. Given that $y=2$ when $x=1$, solve the differential equation, giving your answer in the form $y = f(x)$.[6 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1. This differential equation can be rewritten in the standard linear form.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        \dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} + P(x)y &= Q(x)\\[6pt]

        \dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} +\bigg(\textcolor{RVGreen}{- \dfrac{2}{x}}\bigg)\,y &= x^2 e^{x}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Then we can identify $P(x) = -\dfrac{2}{x}$.

    Applying the $\textbf{integrating factor (5.18)}$ we have

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        I(x) &= e^{\int P(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}\\[6pt]

        I(x) &= e^{\int -\frac{2}{x}\,\mathrm{d}x} \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Computing the integral in the exponent, we have

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        \int -\dfrac{2}{x}\,\mathrm{d}x &= -2\ln|x| \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Substituting and applying the laws of exponents and logarithms, we obtain

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        I(x) &= e^{-2\ln|x|}  \\[6pt]

        &= \left(e^{\ln|x|}\right)^{-2} \tag*{\textbf{M1}}\\[6pt]

        &= |x|^{-2} \tag*{\textbf{A1}} \\[6pt]

        I(x) &= \dfrac{1}{x^2}\hspace{0.5em}\text{... as required} \tag*{\textbf{AG}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

2. Multiplying the differential equation through by $I(x)$, we have

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        \dfrac{1}{x^2}\bigg(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} - \dfrac{2}{x}\,y &= x^2 e^{x}\bigg)\\[12pt]

        \dfrac{1}{x^2}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} - \dfrac{2}{x^3}\,y &= e^{x}\tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Recognising the left-hand side as the derivative of a product, we have

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(\dfrac{y}{x^2}\right) &= e^{x} \tag*{\textbf{(A1)}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Then integrating both sides with respect to $x$, we obtain

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        \int\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(\dfrac{y}{x^2}\right) &=\int e^{x}\tag*{\textbf{(M1)}} \\[12pt]

        \dfrac{y}{x^2} &= e^{x} + C  \\[9pt]

        y &= x^2\left(e^{x} + C\right) \tag*{\textbf{(A1)}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    To solve for $C$ we can apply the initial condition $y = 2$ when $x = 1$.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        2 &= 1^2\left(e^{1} + C\right) \\[6pt]

        C &= 2 - e \tag*{\textbf{(A1)}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Hence the solution is

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        y &= \hspace{0em}x^2\left(e^{x} + 2 - e\right) \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$
