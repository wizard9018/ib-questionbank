# AA1323

**Maximum mark: 11**

Consider the differential equation
$$
\begin{aligned}
    x\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}&=\frac{4x}{\sqrt{(9-4x^2)^3}}-2y
\end{aligned}
$$
where $0<x<\frac{3}{2}$.

1.    Show that $x^2$ is an integrating factor for this differential equation. [3 marks]

2.    Hence, find the general solution to the differential equation, giving your answer in the form $y=g(x)+\dfrac{C}{x^2}$, where $C$ is the constant of integration. [8 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1. The question is asking us to find an integrating factor for this differential equation.

    The following, from the formula booklet, is the form of such a differential equation.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}+P(x)y=Q(x)
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    and the integrating factor, $f(x)$, is given by $f(x)=e^{\int P(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}$.

    Hence, our first step is to rearrange the given equation to match this form and then identify $P(x)$.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    x\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}&=\frac{4x}{\sqrt{(9-4x^2)^3}}-2y\\[12pt]
    x\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}+2y&=\frac{4x}{\sqrt{(9-4x^2)^3}}\\[12pt]
    \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}+\frac{2y}{x}&=\frac{4}{\sqrt{(9-4x^2)^3}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    From this, we can see that
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\textcolor{red}{\,+\,P(x)}y&=Q(x)\\[12pt]
    \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\textcolor{red}{\,+\,\frac{2}{x}}y&=\frac{4}{\sqrt{(9-4x^2)^3}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Therefore, $P(x)=\dfrac{2}{x}$.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    We can now find the integrating factor, $f(x)$.

    The integrating factor is
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    f(x)&=e^{\int\frac{2}{x}\,\mathrm{d}x}\tag*{\textbf{{M1}}}\\[6pt]
    &=e^{2\int\frac{1}{x}\,\mathrm{d}x}\\[6pt]
    &=e^{2\ln{x}}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}\\[6pt]
    &=e^{\ln{x^2}}\\[6pt]
    &=x^2\hspace{0.5em}\text{... as required.}\tag*{\textbf{AG}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

2. The question asks us to write the solution in the form of $y=g(x)+\dfrac{C}{x^2}$.

    The first step, when using an integrating factor, is to multiply the entire equation by the integrating factor and then simplify.

    This gives
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    x^2 \times \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}+ x^2 \times \frac{2y}{x}&= x^2 \times \frac{4}{\sqrt{(9-4x^2)^3}}\tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}\\[12pt]
    \textcolor{red}{x^2 \times \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}+ 2xy}&= \textcolor{blue}{x^2 \times \frac{4}{\sqrt{(9-4x^2)^3}}}\hspace{3em}[1]
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    The next step is crucial when solving differential equations of this form.

    To understand why it works, we will first use the product rule to find the derivative of $f(x)y$. This is the derivative of the integrating factor multiplied by $y$.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \textcolor{blue}{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(x^2y\right)}&=\textcolor{red}{x^2 \times \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}+ 2xy}\hspace{3em}[2]
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Notice how the L.H.S. of $[1]$ is equivalent to the R.H.S. of $[2]$, shown in $\textcolor{red}{\text{red}}$.

    This means we can equate the  R.H.S. of $[1]$ with the  L.H.S. of $[2]$, shown in $\textcolor{blue}{\text{blue}}$, and form the following equation
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \textcolor{blue}{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(x^2y\right)}&=\textcolor{blue}{x^2 \times \frac{4}{\sqrt{(9-4x^2)^3}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    The next step is to integrate both sides with respect to $x$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \int\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(x^2y\right)\right)\,\mathrm{d}x&=\int \frac{4x^2}{\sqrt{(9-4x^2)^3}}\,\mathrm{d}x\\[12pt]
    x^2y&=\int\frac{4x^2}{\sqrt{(9-4x^2)^3}}\,\mathrm{d}x \hspace{2em} [3]\tag*{\textbf{{(A1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    We now have a rather challenging integral.

    Would a substitution of $u=x^2$ work? You could try this, but you would be left with an integral with both $x$ and $u$ in it. We need another strategy.

    Let's try $u=9-4x^2$, which forms part of the denominator of the quotient.

    We can then see that $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}=-8x$ noting that $-8x$ is a multiple of $4x$.

    By rewriting the integral
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &=\int \textcolor{red}{x} \times \textcolor{blue}{\frac{4x}{\sqrt{(9-4x^2)^3}}}\,\mathrm{d}x
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    as the product of two functions we can use integration by parts. As we then have a function ($\textcolor{red}{x}$) which we can differentiate and a function $\left(\textcolor{blue}{\dfrac{4x}{\sqrt{(9-4x^2)^3}}}\right)$ we can integrate.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Consider the formula for integration by parts.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \int \textcolor{red}{u} \times \textcolor{blue}{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}}\,\mathrm{d}x&=uv-\int v\times \dfrac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}\,\mathrm{d}x
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    The first decision we make is which part of integral should be $\textcolor{red}{u}$ and which part should be $\textcolor{blue}{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}}$.

    As mentioned previously we should choose
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    u&=x&\dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}&= \dfrac{4x}{\sqrt{(9-4x^2)^3}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Now we will find $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}$ and $v$.

    Let's consider $u$ first
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    u&=x\\[6pt]
    \dfrac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}&=1\tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Now onto $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    v&=\int{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}}\,\mathrm{d}x\\[12pt]
    &=\int \dfrac{4x}{\sqrt{(9-4x^2)^3}}\,\mathrm{d}x
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    As mentioned we can use a substitution, let $k=9-4x^2$.

    Hence we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}k}{\mathrm{d}x}&=-8x\\[12pt]
    \mathrm{d}x&=-\frac{1}{8x}\mathrm{d}k
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Rewriting $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}$ in terms of $k$ we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \int \dfrac{4x}{\sqrt{(9-4x^2)^3}}\,\mathrm{d}x&=\int \dfrac{4x}{\sqrt{k^3}}\times\frac{-1}{8x}\mathrm{d}k\\[12pt]
    &=-\frac{1}{2}\int k^{-\frac{3}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}k
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Using the inverse power rule to integrate we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &=-\frac{1}{2} \div \left(-\frac{3}{2}+1\right)k^{-\frac{3}{2}+1}\\[12pt]
    v&=k^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    We can now substitute all of the elements into the formula for integration by parts.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \int \dfrac{4x^2}{\sqrt{(9-4x^2)^3}}\,\mathrm{d}x&=x \times \left(k^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)-\int k^{-\frac{1}{2}} \times 1 \, \mathrm{d}x\\[12pt]
    &=\frac{x}{\sqrt{9-4x^2}}-\int \textcolor{red}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{9-4x^2}}} \, \mathrm{d}x
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    You may recognise $\textcolor{red}{\text{this}}$ integral from the formula booklet.
    It is of the form
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \int \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2-x^2}}\,\mathrm{d}x&=\arcsin{\left(\frac{x}{a}\right)}+C
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    where $a$ is $3$ and $x$ is $2x$.

    Because we are working with $2x$ (and not $x$), we need to use a substitution to make sure we don't make any errors when integrating.

    Let $p=2x$. Therefore $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}=2$. Hence $\mathrm{d}x=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}p$.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Rewriting our integral in terms of $p$ we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &=\int \frac{1}{\sqrt{3^2-p^2}} \times \dfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{d}p\\[12pt]
    &=\dfrac{1}{2}\int \frac{1}{\sqrt{3^2-p^2}}\, \mathrm{d}p
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    We can now apply the standard integral from the formula booklet
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &=\frac{1}{2}\arcsin{\left(\frac{p}{3}\right)}+C
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Rewriting back in terms of $x$ we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &=\frac{1}{2}\arcsin{\left(\frac{2x}{3}\right)}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Note, we can omit the constant of integration at this stage.

    Substituting the elements we have found into the integration by parts formula we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \int \dfrac{4x^2}{\sqrt{(9-4x^2)^3}}\,\mathrm{d}x&=\frac{x}{\sqrt{9-4x^2}}-\frac{1}{2}\arcsin{\left(\frac{2x}{3}\right)} + C
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Recall $[3]$, we can replace the L.H.S. of the above to get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    x^2y&=\frac{x}{\sqrt{9-4x^2}}-\frac{1}{2}\arcsin{\left(\frac{2x}{3}\right)} + C
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    To write the answer in the desired form we must divide by $x^2$ which gives
    $$
    \begin{aligned}y=\dfrac{1}{x\sqrt{9-4x^2}}-\dfrac{1}{2x^2}\arcsin{\left(\dfrac{2x}{3}\right)} + \dfrac{C}{x^2}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
