# AA1336

**Maximum mark: 20**

Consider the differential equation $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}-2=\dfrac{3}{x^2}(2xy+y^2)$, where $y\geq 0$ and $4 \leq x<6$.

It is known that $y=0$ when $x=4$, and the solution is of the form $y=f(x)$.

1.    Use Euler's method with a step length of $0.25$, to find an approximate value for $y$ when $x=5.5$. [4 marks]

2.
    1.    Use the substitution $y=vx$ to show that $x\dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}=3v^2+5v+2$.

    2.    Hence, by solving the differential equation, show that\
        $f(x)=\dfrac{x(0.5x-2)}{3-0.5x}$. [10 marks]

3.
    1.    Find the actual value of $y$ when $x=5.5$.

    2.    By considering the gradient of the curve $y=f(x)$, suggest a reason why Euler's method does not give a good approximation for the value of $y$ found in part (a). [2 marks]

The voltage along a wire is given by $V(d)=\dfrac{d(0.5d-2)}{3-0.5d}$, where $d \geq 0$ and $d\neq a$.

$V$, measured in volts, models the voltage at a point $d$ meters from a fixed point on the wire.

4.    Write down the value of $a$. [1 mark]

A scientist, looking for a fault, carefully measures the voltage along the wire and is surprised to find that no readings fall within the interval $s<V<t$, where $s, t \in \mathbb{R}$ and are measured in volts.

5.    Find $s$ and $t$. [3 marks]

1. First rearrange the differential equation to get it in the form $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}=h(x,y)$

    \begin{aligned}

    \end{aligned}
    $$
    The initial value and step length are
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    y(4) &= 0 \\[6pt]

    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Consider the formula for Euler's method where $h$ is the step length
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    x_{n+1}&=x_n+h\\[6pt]

    \end{aligned}
    $$
    It is important to note that we use $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}=h(x,y)$ for the function $f(x_n,y_n)$.

2.  1. First, consider the differential equation (D.E.).

        We have been given $y=vx$, which we can rearrange to $v=\dfrac{y}{x}$.

        Hence, we can write the D.E. in terms of $v$
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}-2&=\dfrac{3}{x^2}(2xy+y^2)\\[12pt]
        &=6\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)+3\left(\frac{y^2}{x^2}\right)+2\\[12pt]
        \textcolor{red}{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}}&=\textcolor{blue}{3v^2+6v+2}\hspace{2em}[1]\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Note that, since $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}=f(v)$ then we can say this equation is homogeneous.

        Next, use the given substitution. Let's use the product rule to differentiate that to get an expression for $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}$ in terms of $v$ and $x$
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}&=x \times  \dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x} + v \times  \dfrac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}x}\\[6pt]
        \textcolor{red}{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}}&= \textcolor{blue}{x   \dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x} + v }\hspace{2em}[2]\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Notice how the $\textcolor{red}{\text{L.H.S.}}$ of both [1] and [2] are equivalent, hence we can equate the $\textcolor{blue}{\text{R.H.S.'s}}$ of [1] and [2]. This gives
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        x   \dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x} + v&=3v^2+6v+2\\[6pt]
        &\hspace{-2.3em}x\dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}=3v^2+5v+2\hspace{1em}\text{...as required}\tag*{\textbf{{AG}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

    2. A substitution ($y=vx$) can be used to transform a homogeneous D.E. into a separable form.

        In the previous part we began this process.

        Reflecting on that answer, we can separate the variables $v$ and $x$, giving
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \frac{1}{3v^2+5v+2}\times \mathrm{d}v&=\frac{1}{x}\times \mathrm{d}x
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Now we integrate both sides to get
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \int\frac{1}{3v^2+5v+2} \mathrm{d}v&=\int\frac{1}{x} \mathrm{d}x\tag*{\textbf{{M1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        The R.H.S. is a standard integral.

        But the L.H.S. is a little trickier.

        We can't integrate it directly or use a substitution.

        But we can factorise the denominator to get
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \text{L.H.S.}&=\int\frac{1}{3v^2+5v+2} \mathrm{d}v\\[12pt]
        &=\int\frac{1}{(3v+2)(v+1)} \mathrm{d}v
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        This is useful, as it suggests we can use partial fractions to split the fraction into two parts such as
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \frac{1}{(3v+2)(v+1)}&\equiv\frac{A}{3v+2}+\frac{B}{v+1}\tag*{\textbf{{M1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        where $A$ and $B$ are to be determined.

        Note, we have used the equivalence symbol. This means the L.H.S. and the R.H.S. are equal for __all__ values of $v$. In other words, this is an identity.

        If we multiply the structure above by $(3v+2)(v+1)$ we get
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        1&\equiv A(v+1)+B(3v+2)
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        As this is true for all $v$ then we can pick any value we want for $v$ to find $A$ and $B$. Therefore it is a good strategy to pick a value that will cancel out either $A$ or $B$.
        Hence, let's pick $v=-1$ first
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        1&= A(-1+1)+B(3(-1)+2)\\[6pt]
        1&= -B\\[6pt]
        B&=-1\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Now let's pick $v=-\frac{2}{3}$
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        1&= A\left(\left(-\tfrac{2}{3}\right)+1\right)+B(3\left(-\tfrac{2}{3}\right)+2)\\[6pt]
        1&= \frac{1}{3}A\\[6pt]
        A&=3\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Putting those results together we get
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \frac{1}{(3v+2)(v+1)}&=\frac{3}{3v+2}-\frac{1}{v+1}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Hence we can rewrite the integral which gives
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \int\left(\frac{3}{3v+2}-\frac{1}{v+1}\right) \mathrm{d}v&=\int\frac{1}{x} \mathrm{d}x\\[12pt]
        3\int \frac{1}{3v+2} \mathrm{d}v-\int \frac{1}{v+1} \mathrm{d}v&=\int\frac{1}{x} \mathrm{d}x
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Using the standard integral
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \int \frac{1}{x}\,\mathrm{d}{x}&=\ln{|x|}+C
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        we get
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        3 \times \textcolor{red}{\frac{1}{3}} \times \ln{|3v+2|}- \ln{|v+1|}&=
        \ln x + \ln A\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Notice the additional factor of $\textcolor{red}{\frac{1}{3}}$, we need this to compensate for the chain rule as $(3v+2)'=3$.

        As this solution involves logarithms it is good practice to give the constant of integration as $\ln{A}$. We'll see why in a moment.

        Remember we need the solution to be in the form $y=f(x)$.
        To get to this let's rewrite both sides as a single logarithm.
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \ln{|3v+2|}- \ln{|v+1|}&=
        \ln x + \ln A\\[6pt]
        \ln\left(\frac{3v+2}{v+1}\right)&=
        \ln Ax
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        By choosing the constant as $\ln{A}$, it allows us to quickly write the R.H.S. as a single logarithm.

        Additionally, we removed the absolute value signs from both $\ln{|3v+2|}$ and $\ln{|v+1|}$ as $x,y>1$ means $v>1$ therefore the inner functions of both logarithms are greater than zero. Hence $|3v+2|=(3v+2)$ and $|v+1|=(v+1)$.

        As we have the equation in the form
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \ln{\big[g(x)\big]}&=\ln{\big[h(x)\big]}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        then we can equate the inner functions
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        g(x)&=h(x)
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        hence we get
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \frac{3v+2}{v+1}&=Ax\tag*{\textbf{{M1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Let's make $v$ the subject of the equation
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        3v+2&=Avx+Ax\\[6pt]
        3v-Avx&=Ax-2\\[6pt]
        v(3-Ax)&=Ax-2\\[6pt]
        v&=\frac{Ax-2}{3-Ax}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Now let's reuse the substitution $v=\frac{y}{x}$
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \frac{y}{x}&=\frac{Ax-2}{3-Ax}\\[6pt]
        y&=\frac{x(Ax-2)}{3-Ax}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        We still have the constant of integration.
        Using the condition given in the question $(4,0)$ we can form an equation and solve it on our G.D.C. to find $A$.
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        0&=\frac{4(4A-2)}{3-4A}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}\\[6pt]
        A&=0.5
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Using this value for $A$ we get
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        &y=\dfrac{x(0.5x-2)}{3-0.5x}\hspace{1em}\text{...as required}\tag*{\textbf{{AG}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

3.  1. To find the actual value of $y$ when $x=5.5$ we evaluate $f(5.5)$ on a G.D.C.

        \begin{aligned}

    2. We found the actual value, in the previous part, when $x=5.5$, to be $y=16.5$.

        There is a large difference between this and the estimate of $y=8.17$, we found in part (a).

        To help explain this large difference let's examine a sketch of $y=f(x)$ on our G.D.C.

        ![AA1336-a1](images/AA1336-a1.JPEG)

        The $\textcolor{red}{\text{red}}$ line, drawn at $x=5.5$ shows the gradient at this point.

        Notice that the gradient around $x=5.5$ increases very rapidly.

        Euler's method involves using the gradient at a previous point to estimate the location of the next point. As the gradient increases rapidly our estimate is significantly less than the actual value ($8.17<16.5$).

        Therefore, as the gradient increases
        $\text{very rapidly around }x=5.5$ Euler's method does not give a good approximation for the value of $y$.
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \tag*{\textbf{{R1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
