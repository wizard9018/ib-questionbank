# AA1399

**Maximum mark: 14**

Two athletes, Mateo and Diego, compete in a $250$ metres race along a straight track.

Mateo's displacement, in metres, from the starting line during the race can be modelled by

$$

\begin{aligned}

  s_M(t) = 9.5\,t + 6e^{-t/2} - 6, \quad \text{where } t \geq 0.

\end{aligned}

$$

Time, $t$, is measured in seconds from when the race starts.

1.  1. Find an expression for Mateo's velocity, $v_M(t)$.

    2. Hence show that when Mateo's velocity is $9\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}$, then $t=2\ln6$ seconds.[5 marks]

2.  1. Find an expression for Mateo's acceleration, $a_M(t)$.

  

    2. Hence show that Mateo is always speeding up during the race.[3 marks]

Diego's displacement, in metres, from the starting line during the race can be modelled by

$$

\begin{aligned}

  s_D(t) = 9\,t + 4e^{-t/3} - 4, \quad \text{where } t \geq 0.

\end{aligned}

$$

Mateo crosses the finishing line ahead of Diego.

3. Find the time at which Mateo overtakes Diego.[2 marks]

4. Find the distance Diego is from the finishing line at the moment Mateo crosses the finishing line.[4 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  1. Differentiating $s_M(t)$ term by term and applying the chain rule to the exponential term.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        v_M(t) &= 9.5 + 6 \cdot \left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\right)e^{-t/2} \tag*{\textbf{(M1)}} \\[6pt]

        &= \hspace{0.2em}9.5 - 3e^{-t/2} \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    2. Setting $v_M(t) = 9$ and rearranging to isolate the exponential term.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

            9.5 - 3e^{-t/2} &= 9 \tag*{\textbf{M1}} \\[6pt]

            - 3e^{-t/2} &= -\frac{1}{2}\\[6pt]

            e^{-t/2} &= \frac{1}{6}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Taking the natural logarithm of both sides

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

            \ln(e^{-t/2})&=\ln\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)\tag*{\textbf{M1}}\\[6pt]

            -\frac{t}{2}&=\ln\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)\\[6pt]

            t&=-2\left(\ln\left(6^{-1}\right)\right)\tag*{\textbf{A1}}\\[6pt]

            t &= \hspace{0.2em}2\ln6\,\text{ s}\hspace{1em}\text{... as required} \tag*{\textbf{AG}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

    

2.  1. We can differentiate $v_M(t)$ using the **chain rule (5.6)** to obtain Mateo's acceleration.

        When we let $u=-\frac{1}{2}t$ then $v=-3e^u$.

    

        Differentiating both we have $\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{1}{2}$ and $\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}u}=-3e^u$.

    

        Then using the formula

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

            \frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t}&=\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}u}\times\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}t}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        we have

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

            a_M(t)&=\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t}\\[6pt]

            &= -3 e^{u}\cdot \left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\right) \tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}\\[6pt]

            a_M(t) &= \hspace{0.2em}1.5\,e^{{-t}/_{2}} \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

    2. First let's note the conditions for which an object is speeding up or slowing down.

        - If the velocity and acceleration both have the same sign ($v(t) \text{ and }a(t)>0$ or $v(t) \text{ and } a(t)<0$), an object is **speeding up**

        - If the velocity and acceleration have opposite signs ($v(t)>0 , a(t)<0$ or $v(t)<0 , a(t)>0$), an object is **slowing down**

        Since $e^{-t/2} > 0$ for all real values of $t$, we know that $a_M(t) = 1.5\,e^{-t/2} > 0$ for all $t \geq 0$. 

        Similarly, we have our expression for Mateo's velocity from part $\text{(a)}\,\text{(i)}$, $v_M(t)=9.5 - 3e^{-t/2}$. By examining this function on the G.D.C. we can see that $v_M(t)>0$ for all $t \geq 0$ also.

        $$\text{Both velocity and acceleration are positive throughout the race}$$ 

        $$\\[-2.3em]$$

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \tag*{{\textbf{R1}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Therefore Mateo is continually speeding up. 

        $$\\[-2.3em]$$

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \tag*{{\textbf{AG}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

3. Mateo overtakes Diego when their displacements are equal, so we set up the equation

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    9.5\,t + 6e^{-t/2} - 6 &= 9\,t + 4e^{-t/3} - 4 \tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    On the G.D.C., we can sketch the graphs of $\color{B52209}{y = 9.5x + 6e^{-x/2} - 6}$ (Mateo's displacement) and $\color{039855}{y = 9x + 4e^{-x/3} - 4}$ (Diego's displacement), using $x$ in place of $t$, and find the point of intersection. This is shown on the following axes.

    ![AA1399c](images/AA1399c.png)

    From the graph, $t = 4.521\ldots$, so

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    t &= 4.521\ldots \\[6pt]

    &= \hspace{0.525em}\boxed{4.52\,\text{ s}}\hspace{1em}\text{(3 s.f.)} \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

4. First we should find the time when Mateo crosses the finish line. We can do that by setting Mateo's displacement equal to $250$ metres.

    Using the GDC, we obtain

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        9.5\,t + 6e^{{-t}/_{2}} - 6 &= 250 \tag*{\textbf{(M1)}} \\[6pt]

        t &= 26.95\ldots\,\text{s} \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Then we substitute this time into Diego's displacement function and subtract the value from the $250$ metres. 

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        d &= 250 - \left[\,9(26.95\ldots) + 4e^{{-26.95\ldots}/_{3}} - 4\,\right] \tag*{\textbf{(M1)}} \\[6pt]

        &= 11.47\ldots \\[6pt]

        d&= \hspace{0.2em}11.5\,\text{m} \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$
