# AA1414

**Maximum mark: 9**

The diagram shows a cone with a volume of $\displaystyle \frac{8\pi}{3}\,\text{cm}^3$.

![cone](images/cone.JPEG)

The radius of the circular base is $r\,$cm and the vertical height is $h\,$cm.
The cone is open, so only the curved surface is present.
The curved surface area of the cone is $A\,\text{cm}^2$.

1.    Show that $\displaystyle A=\pi\sqrt{r^4+\frac{64}{r^2}}$. [3 marks]

The minimum value of $A$ occurs when $r=2^k$, where $k \in \mathrm{Q}$.

2.    Find $k$. [6 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  We have been given a cone with a volume of
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{8\pi}{3}\,\text{cm}^3,
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    radius $r\,\text{cm}$, and height $h\,\text{cm}$.

    The __formula for the volume of a cone (3.1)__ is given by
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    V
    =
    \frac13\pi r^2h.
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Using the volume given in the question, we obtain
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{8\pi}{3}
    &=
    \frac13\pi r^2h.
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Making $h$ the subject gives
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    h
    =
    \frac{8}{r^2}.
    \tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{M1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    The cone is open, so only the curved surface contributes to the surface area.

    Using the __formula for the curved surface area of a cone (3.1)__
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    A
    =
    \pi rl,
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    where $l$ is the slant height.
    Using Pythagoras' theorem,
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    l
    &=
    \sqrt{r^2+h^2}
    \tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Substituting for $h$ gives
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        A&=\pi r \sqrt{r^2+\left( \frac{8}{r^2} \right)^2} \\[6pt]
        &=\pi\sqrt{r^2\left(r^2+\frac{64}{r^4}\right)}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}\\[6pt]
        &=\displaystyle \pi\sqrt{r^4+\frac{64}{r^2}}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{AG}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

2. To find the minimum value of $A$ we must solve
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{\mathrm dA}{\mathrm dr}
    =
    0\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(M1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Let us begin by differentiating
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    A
    =
    \pi
    \left(
    r^4+\frac{64}{r^2}
    \right)^{1/2}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    The function is raised to the power $\dfrac12$, therefore we must use the __chain rule (5.6)__.
    Before doing so, let
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    u
    &=
    r^4+\frac{64}{r^2}\\[12pt]
    &=
    r^4+64r^{-2}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Then
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    A
    =
    \pi u^{1/2}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Using the __formula for the chain rule (5.6)__
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{\mathrm dA}{\mathrm dr}
    =
    \frac{\mathrm dA}{\mathrm du}
    \times
    \frac{\mathrm du}{\mathrm dr}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Differentiating $u^{1/2}$ with respect to $u$ using the __power rule (5.3)__ gives
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm du}
    \left(
    \pi u^{1/2}
    \right)
    =
    \frac{\pi}{2}u^{-1/2}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Next,
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{\mathrm du}{\mathrm dr}
    &=
    4r^3+64(-2)r^{-3}\\[6pt]
    &=
    4r^3-\frac{128}{r^3}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Substituting these results into the chain rule formula gives
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{\mathrm dA}{\mathrm dr}
    &=
    \frac{\pi}{2}
    u^{-1/2}
    \left(
    4r^3-\frac{128}{r^3}
    \right)\\[6pt]
    &=
    \frac{\pi}{2}
    \left(
    r^4+\frac{64}{r^2}
    \right)^{-1/2}
    \left(
    4r^3-\frac{128}{r^3}
    \right)
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Hence we need to solve
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{\pi}{2}
    \left(
    r^4+\frac{64}{r^2}
    \right)^{-1/2}
    \left(
    4r^3-\frac{128}{r^3}
    \right)
    =
    0
    \tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(M1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    The first two factors can never be zero, therefore
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    4r^3-\frac{128}{r^3}
    &=
    0
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Solving this gives
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    4r^6
    &=
    128\\[6pt]
    r^6
    &=
    32\\[6pt]
    r^6
    &=2^5\\[6pt]
    r&=2^{\frac{5}{6}}
    \tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Since the question states that there is a minimum value of $A$, and $r>0$ then this stationary point must correspond to the minimum.

    Therefore the minimum value of $A$ occurs when $r=2^{\frac{5}{6}}$.

    Therefore $k=\dfrac{5}{6}$
    $$
    \\[-30pt]
    \begin{aligned}
    \tag*{\textcolor{black}{\textbf{A1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
