# AA1049

**Maximum mark: 13**

A semicircle with centre $(0,0)$ and radius $4$ has a triangle, $\triangle$\,ABC, inscribed as shown in the figure below.

![question](images/question.png)

The equation of the curve is given by $y=\sqrt{a-x^2}$, where $a \in \mathbb{Z}$.

1. Find the coordinates of point B in terms of $x$ only. [2 marks]

2. Hence, find an expression for the area of triangle ABC, in terms of $x$. [2 marks]

3. Show that $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}(\text{Area})}{\mathrm{d}x}=\dfrac{8-2x-x^2}{\sqrt{16-x^2}}$. [3 marks]

4. Hence or otherwise, find the coordinates for B that maximize the area. [6 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  First lets find $a$, we can see from point $A$ that when $x=4$ then $y=0$. Therefore
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        0&=\sqrt{a-(-4)^2}\\
        a&=(-4)^2\\
        a&=16 \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Therefore $y=\sqrt{16-x^2}$

    Hence, point B has coordinates $(x,y)$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \quad \text{B}\left(x,\sqrt{16-x^2} \right) \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

2.  We have
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \text{Area }\triangle \text{ABC} &= \tfrac{1}{2}\text{base} \times \text \tag*{\textbf{M1}}\\[7pt]
    &= \tfrac{1}{2}\big(x-(-4)\big)\left( \sqrt{16-x^2} \right) \\[7pt]
    & \hspace{0.28em}= \tfrac{1}{2}(x+4)\left( \sqrt{16-x^2} \right) \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

3.  We need to use the product rule, first differentiate both functions of
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        (x+4)'&=1 &\left((16-x^2)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\right)'=-2x\times\tfrac{1}{2}(16-x^2)^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\\[6pt]
        &\textcolor{white}{gap} &=-x(16-x^2)^{-\tfrac{1}{2}} \tag*{\textbf{M1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Now using the product rule
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \dfrac{\mathrm{d}(\text{Area})}{\mathrm{d}x} &=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(1\times(16-x^2)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}+-x(16-x^2)^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}(x+4)\right)\tag*{\textbf{A1}}\\[6pt]
        &=\frac{(16-x^2)^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}}{2}\left((16-x^2)-x(x+4)\right)\\[6pt]
        &=\frac{(16-x^2)^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}}{2}\left(16-4x-2x^2\right)\tag*{\textbf{A1}}\\[6pt]
        &\hspace{0.28em}= \dfrac{8-2x-x^2}{\sqrt{16-x^2}}\tag*{\textbf{AG}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

4. The extreme is attained at points where the derivative is zero. Hence,
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \dfrac{8-2x-x^2}{\sqrt{16-x^2}} &= 0 \tag*{\textbf{M1}}\\[7pt]
        -(x^2+2x-8) &= 0 \tag*{\textbf{A1}}\\[7pt]
        -(x+4)(x-2) &= 0 \\[7pt]
        x &= -4,2 \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Making a sketch of $y=-(x+4)(x-2)$ we can see that for the red part of the curve $y$ is positive and the green part $y$ is negative.

    Using the first derivative test, we get
    $$
     \tag*{\textbf{M1}}
    $$

    ![Screenshot 2024-03-08 at 10.43.12 AM](<images/Screenshot 2024-03-08 at 10.43.12 AM.png>)

    The sign of the first derivative changes from positive to negative at $x=2$.
    $$
     \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    $$
    Evaluating $y$ for $x=2$, we find $y=\sqrt{16-2^2} = \sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt{3}$.

    Therefore, the are is maximum at $\text{B}\left(2,2\sqrt{3}\right)$.
