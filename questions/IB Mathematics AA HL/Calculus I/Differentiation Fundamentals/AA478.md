# AA478

**Maximum mark: 22**

Let $f(x) = (x+1)e^{-2x}$, $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

1.  Find $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}x}$. [2 marks]

2.  Prove by induction that
    $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^nf}{\mathrm{d}x^n} = \big[n(-2)^{n-1} + (-2)^n(x+1)\big]e^{-2x}$
    for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$.[7 marks]

3.  Find the coordinates of any local minimum and maximum points on the
    graph of $y = f(x)$. Justify whether any such point is a minimum or
    a maximum. [5 marks]

4.  Find the coordinates of any points of inflexion on the graph of
    $y = f(x)$. Justify whether any such point is a point of inflexion.
    [5 marks]

5.  Hence sketch the graph of $y = f(x)$, indicating clearly the points
    found in parts (c) and (d) and any intercepts with the axes.
    [3 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  Using the product rule, we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \hspace{0.2em} \dfrac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}x} &= \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\big[(x+1)e^{-2x}\big] \\[6pt]
    &= e^{-2x} - 2(x+1)e^{-2x} \\[10pt]
    &= \big[1 - 2(x+1)\big]e^{-2x}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

2.  Let $\mathrm{P}(n)$ be the following statement:
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \hspace{7.9em} \dfrac{\mathrm{d}^nf}{\mathrm{d}x^n} = \big[n(-2)^{n-1} + (-2)^n(x+1)\big]e^{-2x} \hspace{3em} [\text{for $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$}]
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Using mathematical induction, we will prove that $\mathrm{P}(n)$
    holds for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$.

:u[Base case]. For $n = 1$, from part (a), we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}x} = \big[1 - 2(x+1)\big]e^{-2x}
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence P$(1)$ holds.

:u[Inductive hypothesis]. We assume that $\mathrm{P}(k)$ holds
for some $k \geq 1$. In other words, we assume that
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^kf}{\mathrm{d}x^k} = \big[k(-2)^{k-1} + (-2)^k(x+1)\big]e^{-2x} \hspace{0.5em}
\end{aligned}
$$

for some $k \geq 1$.

:u[Inductive step]. To show that $\mathrm{P}(k+1)$ holds, we
need to show that
$$
\begin{aligned}
\hspace{1.6em} \dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{k+1}f}{\mathrm{d}x^{k+1}} = \big[(k+1)(-2)^k + (-2)^{(k+1)}(x+1)\big]e^{-2x}
\end{aligned}
$$

If we differentiate both sides of the equation (IH), we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hspace{5.55em} \dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{k+1}f}{\mathrm{d}x^{k+1}} &= \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\Big[\big[k(-2)^{k-1} + (-2)^k(x+1)\big]e^{-2x}\Big] \\[14pt]
&= \big[(-2)^k\big]e^{-2x} - 2\big[k(-2)^{k-1} + (-2)^k(x+1)\big]e^{-2x} \\[16pt]
&= \big[(-2)^k\big]e^{-2x} + \big[k(-2)^k + (-2)^{k+1}(x+1)\big]e^{-2x} \\[16pt]
&= \big[(k+1)(-2)^k + (-2)^{(k+1)}(x+1)\big]e^{-2x} \\
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence $P(k+1)$ holds as well.

:u[Conclusion]. P$(1)$ holds, and for every $k \geq 1$, if
P$(k)$ holds then P$(k+1)$ also holds.

Hence, by mathematical induction, we conclude that P$(n)$ holds for all
$n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$.

3.  If we solve the equation $f'(x) = 0$ for $x$, we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \big[1 - 2(x+1)\big]e^{-2x} &= 0 \\[6pt]
    1 - 2(x+1) &= 0 \\[6pt]
    - 2x - 1 &= 0 \\[6pt]
    x &= -0.5 \hspace{0.6em}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Hence $-0.5$ is the only
    critical number of $f$.

    Using the ID Test, we have

    ![6db5903b56fcbc8c908bce990a2d28a2f4d84f81.svg](images/6db5903b56fcbc8c908bce990a2d28a2f4d84f81.svg)

    The sign of $f'$ changes from positive to negative at $-0.5$.
    Hence, by the First Derivative Test, $f$ has a local maximum at
    $-0.5$.
    Evaluating $y = f(x)$ for $x = -0.5$, we find
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \hspace{3em} y &= f(-0.5) \\[6pt]
    &= (-0.5+1)e^{-2(-0.5)} \\[7pt]
    &= 0.5e
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Hence the graph of $y = f(x)$ has a local
    maximum at
     $\mathrm{A}(-0.5,0.5e)$

4.  If we solve the equation $f''(x) = 0$ for $x$, we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \big[2(-2) + (-2)^2(x+1)\big]e^{-2x} &= 0 \hspace{3.6em} \\[6pt]
    \big[\hspace{-0.15em}-4 + 4(x+1)\big]e^{-2x} &= 0 \\[6pt]
    -4 + 4(x+1) &= 0 \\[6pt]
    x &= 0
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Hence there is at most one point of inflexion
    on the graph of $y = f(x)$.

    Using the Concavity Test, we have

    ![4ab9272c6eeca47267a3b6db4da801aa3659585d.svg](images/4ab9272c6eeca47267a3b6db4da801aa3659585d.svg)

    The graph of $y = f(x)$ changes concavity from downward to upward at
    $0$.

    Hence the graph of $y = f(x)$ has an inflexion point at $0$.

    Evaluating $y = f(x)$ for $x = 0$, we find
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    y &= f(0) \\[6pt]
    &= (0+1)e^{-2(0)} \\[7pt]
    &= 1
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Hence the graph of $y = f(x)$ has the inflexion
    point at
     $\mathrm{B}(0,1)$

5.  We first note that the domain of the function
    $f(x) = (x+1)e^{-2x}$ is $(-\infty,\infty)$.

    Solving the equation $f(x) = 0$ for $x$, we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    (x+1)e^{-2x} &= 0 \\[6pt]
    x+1 &= 0 \\[6pt]
    x &= -1 \hspace{1.7em}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Hence the point
     $\mathrm{C}(-1,0)$
    is the $x$-intercept of the graph of $y = f(x)$.

    From part (d), we know $y = 1$ when $x = 0$.

    Hence the point
     $\mathrm{B}(0,1)$
    is the $y$-intercept of the graph of $y = f(x)$.

    We have
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \lim_{x\rightarrow -\infty} f(x) &= \lim_{x\rightarrow -\infty} \big[(x+1)e^{-2x}\big] \hspace{0.9em} \\[6pt]
    &= -\infty
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    and

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty} f(x) &= \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty} \big[(x+1)e^{-2x}\big] \hspace{0.9em} \\[6pt]
    &= 0
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Hence the $x$-axis is a horizontal asymptote of
    the graph of $y = f(x)$.
