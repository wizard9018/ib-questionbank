# AA1317

**Maximum mark: 16**

Let $f(x)=4(x-1)^2$.

1.    Write down the range of $f$. [1 mark]

Consider another function $g$ such that $g(x)=\dfrac{f(x)}{e^x}$ for $x\geq 0$.

2.    Find the $y$-intercept of $g$. [1 mark]

3.    Show that $g'(x)=\dfrac{-4(x^2-4x+3)}{e^x}$. [3 marks]

The graph of $g$ has two stationary points.

4.    

    1.    Find the $x$-coordinates of the stationary points of $g$. 

    

    2.    Given that $g''(x)=\dfrac{4(x^2-6x+7)}{e^x}$, determine the nature of the stationary points. [5 marks]

5.    

    1.    Find the values of $x$ for which the graph of $g$ is concave-down. 

    2.    Hence, write down the region for which $g$ is increasing and concave-down. [6 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1. The given function $f$ is a quadratic function. As the $x^2$ term is positive it is concave-up (opens upwards).

    Therefore the vertex is the the minimum point and with the $y$-coordinate of this point we can write down the range.

    

    The vertex form of a quadratic is

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    y=a(x-h)^2+k

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    where the vertex is $(h,k)$.

    Our quadratic is already written in vertex form. We can see this more clearly if it is written like this

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    y=4(x-1)^2+0

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Hence the minimum point is $(1,0)$ which means the range is $f(x)\geq0$.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

2. Substituting $f$ into the new function $g$ we have $g(x)=\dfrac{4(x-1)^2}{e^x}$.

    To find the $y$-intercept we evaluate the function at $x=0$.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    g(0)&=\frac{4(0-1)^2}{e^0}\\[6pt]

    &=4

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Hence the coordinates of the $y$-intercept are $(0,4)$.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

3. The function $g$ is a quotient. Hence we will use the quotient rule to differentiate it.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    \hspace{8em}&\text{If }& &\hspace{-5em}&y&=\frac{\textcolor{red}{u}}{\textcolor{blue}{v}}&\\[12pt]

    \hspace{8em}&\text{then }&&\hspace{-5em}&\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}&= \frac{v\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}-u\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}}{v^2}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    For $g(x)=\dfrac{\textcolor{red}{4(x-1)^2}}{\textcolor{blue}{e^x}}$ we have

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    u&=4(x-1)^2&v&=e^{x}\\[12pt]

    \frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}&=2\times4(x-1)^1&\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}&=e^{x}\\[12pt]

    &=8(x-1)&&

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Now we can substitute these expressions into the quotient rule formula giving

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    g'(x)&= \frac{e^x(8(x-1))-4(x-1)^2(e^x)}{(e^x)^2}\tag*{\textbf{{M1A1}}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    By factorising the numerator we can cancel the common term and then simplify the numerator

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

    g'(x)&= \frac{4\cancel{e^x}(2(x-1)-(x-1)^2)}{(e^x)^{\cancel{2}}}\\[12pt]

    &= \frac{4(2(x-1)-(x-1)^2)}{e^x}\\[12pt]

    &= \frac{4(2x-2-(x^2-2x+1))}{e^x}\\[12pt]

    &= \frac{4(2x-2-x^2+2x-1))}{e^x}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}\\[12pt]

    &= \frac{4(-x^2+4x-3)}{e^x}\\[12pt]

    &= \dfrac{-4(x^2-4x+3)}{e^x}\hspace{3em}\text{...as required}\tag*{\textbf{{AG}}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

4.  1. To find $x$-coordinates of the stationary points we set the derivative equal to zero.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \frac{-4(x^2-4x+3)}{e^x}&=0\tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Solving for $x$

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        -4(x^2-4x+3)&=0\\[6pt]

        x^2-4x+3&=0

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        This quadratic can be factorised

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        (x-3)(x-1)&=0

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Hence by the null factor theorem,  $x=1$ and $x=3$.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

    2. As we are given $g''(x)$ let's conduct a second-derivative test to determine the nature of the stationary points. 

        To do this we evaluate the second derivative using the $x$-values of the stationary points we found in part (d)(i).

        If $g''(x)<0$ then the point is a maximum.

        If $g''(x)>0$ then the point is a minimum.

        Substituting $x=1$ and $x=3$ into the second derivative

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        g''(1)&=\frac{4((1)^2-6(1)+7)}{e^{(1)}}&&g''(3)=\frac{4((3)^2-6(3)+7)}{e^{(3)}}\tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}\\[6pt]

        &=\frac{4(2)}{e}&&\hspace{2.1em}=\frac{4(-2)}{e^3}\\[6pt]

        &>0&&\hspace{2.1em}<0

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Note that we do not need to know the exact values here. It is enough to know whether the final outcome is positive or negative.

        As $g''(1)>0$, there is a $\text{local minimum at }x=1$.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        As $g''(3)<0$ there is a $\text{local maximum at }x=3$.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

5.  1. A function is concave-down if $g''(x)<0$ (and concave-up if $g''(x)>0$).

        Hence we can form the inequality

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        g''(x)&<0\tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}\\[6pt]

        \frac{4(x^2-6x+7)}{e^x}&<0

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Solving for $x$ we get

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        4(x^2-6x+7)&<0\\[6pt]

        x^2-6x+7&<0

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        To solve this inequality we need to find the roots of $x$.

        The roots are the $x$-intercepts which are the points where the quadratic changes from positive to negative and vice versa.

        Hence the graph changes concavity when $g''(x)=0$. Note, these are known as points of inflexion.

        Hence we must solve

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        x^2-6x+7=0

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        The quadratic cannot be easily factorised thus we will use the quadratic formula.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \text{If}\hspace{1em}\,ax^2+bx+c&=0\\[6pt]

        \text{then}\hspace{4.7em}x&=\dfrac{-b\pm \sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        From the equation $a=1$, $b=-6$ and $c=7$ so

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        x&=\dfrac{-(-6)\pm \sqrt{(-6)^2-4(1)(7)}}{2(1)}\tag*{\textbf{{(M1)}}}\\[6pt]

        &=\dfrac{6\pm\sqrt{8}}{2}\\[9pt]

        &=\dfrac{6\pm2\sqrt{2}}{2}\\[9pt]

        &=3\pm\sqrt{2}\tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        We now have the two roots but we still need to decide for what region of $x$ is $x^2-6x+7<0$.

        Note that $y=x^2-6x+7$ is concave-up for all $x$, this is because the coefficient of $x^2$ is positive.

        As the graph is U-shaped the region below the $x$-axis is when $x^2-6x+7<0$. This occurs when $x$ is $\textcolor{red}{\text{between}}$ the two roots $x^2-6x+7<0$.

        ![AA1317a1](images/AA1317a1.JPEG)

        Hence the desired region is $3-\sqrt{2}<x<3+\sqrt{2}$.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \tag*{\textbf{{A1}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

    2. In part (d) we found that the graph of $g$ has a minimum at $x=1$ and a maximum at $x=3$.

        We are told that $g$ has only $2$ stationary points, therefore the function must be $\textcolor{green}{\text{increasing}}$ on the interval $1<x<3$.

        In part (e)(i) we found that the function is $\textcolor{blue}{\text{concave-down}}$ between $3-\sqrt{2}<x<3+\sqrt{2}$.

        ![AA1317a2](images/AA1317a2.JPEG)

        Hence $3-\sqrt{2}<x<3$.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

        \tag*{\textbf{{A2}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$
