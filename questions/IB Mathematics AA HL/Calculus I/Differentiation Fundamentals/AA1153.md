# AA1153

**Maximum mark: 16**

Helen is working with the function
$$
\begin{aligned}
    f(x)&=e^{-x}(x-2)^2
\end{aligned}
$$
where $x>0$.

She wants to learn more about its behaviour.

1.  Show that $f'(x)=-e^{-x}(x^2-6x+8)$. [2 marks]

2.  Help Helen by finding $f''(x)$.[1 mark]

3.  Hence, or otherwise find
    1.  the exact coordinates of any stationary points.

    2.  the nature of these stationary points.[6 marks]

The function is concave down between $a<x<b$.

4.  Find $a$ and $b$ in the form $s \pm \sqrt{t}$ where $s, t \in \mathbb{Z}$. [4 marks]

Helen begins looking at another function $g(x)$.

She knows that $g(2)=5$ and $g'(2)=3$.

5.  Find the exact value of $[f(g(2))]'$. [3 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  We can differentiate the function using the product rule
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \textcolor{white}{hack} \tag*{\textbf{M1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        f'(x)&=(e^{-x})'(x-2)^2+e^{-x}((x-2)^2)'\\[6pt]
        &=-e^{-x}(x-2)^2+2e^{-x}(x-2) \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Factorise out the $e^{-x}$ term and simplify
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        &=-e^{-x}((x-2)^2-2(x-2))\\[6pt]
        &=-e^{-x}(x^2-4x+4-2x+4)\\[6pt]
        &\hspace{0.25em}=-e^{-x}(x^2-6x+8) \hspace{2em} \text{... as required}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
2.  We can use the product rule again
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        f''(x)&=(-e^{-x})'(x^2-6x+8)+(-e^{-x})(x^2-6x+8)'\\[6pt]
        &=e^{-x}(x^2-6x+8)-e^{-x}(2x-6)\tag*{\textbf{A1}}\\[6pt]
        &\hspace{0.25em}=e^{-x}(x^2-8x+14)
    \end{aligned}
    $$
3.  1.  To find the $x-$coordinate of stationary points we must solve $f'(x)=0$.
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            \textcolor{white}{hack} \tag*{\textbf{M1}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            0&=-e^{-x}(x^2-6x+8)\\[6pt]
            0&=-e^{-x}(x-2)(x-4)
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        With the function fully factorised we can solve for $x$
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            -e^{-x}=0&&\hspace{1.8em} &x-2=0&x-4=0&\\[6pt]
            \text{no solution}&& &\hspace{1.8em}x=2&x=4& \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

        We can see that there are $2$ stationary points at $x=2$ and $x=4$.

        Next we can find the $y-$coordinate of these two points.
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            f(2)=e^{-2}(2-2)^2 & &f(4)=e^{-4}(4-2)^2\\[6pt]
            f(2)=0 & &f(4)=4e^{-4}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        The exact coordinates of the two stationary points are
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \hspace{0.525em}(2, 0) \text{ and } (4, 4e^{-4}) \tag*{\textbf{A1A1}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
    2.  To classify the stationary points we can evaluate $f''(x)$ at the stationary points.

        If $ f''(x_{\text{stat point}}) > 0 $, the S.P. is a minimum, and if $ f''(x_{\text{stat point}}) < 0 $, the S.P. is a maximum.
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            \textcolor{white}{hack} \tag*{\textbf{M1}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            f''(2)&=e^{-2}(2^2-8\times 2+14) & &f''(4)=e^{-4}(4^2-8\times 4+14)\\[6pt]
            f''(2)&=2e^{-2} & &f''(4)=-2e^{-4}\\[6pt]
            f''(2)&>0 \hspace{1em} \text{... minimum}& &f''(4)<0 \hspace{1em} \text{... maximum}
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \hspace{0.525em}(2, 0) \text{ is a minimum and } (4, 4e^{-4}) \text{ is a maximum.} \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

4.  When a function changes concavity we know that $f''(x)=0$.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
       f''(x)&=e^{-x}(x^2-8x+14)\\[6pt]
       &=0
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    $e^{-x}\neq 0$ therefore we can use $x^2-8x+14$ to learn about the concavity.

    The original function $f(x)$ is concave down when $f''(x)<0$.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \textcolor{white}{hack} \tag*{\textbf{R1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    We can make a rough sketch of $y=x^2-8x+14$, recognising the region of the graph which is below zero. This is shown as the solid line.

    ![AA1153_a](images/AA1153_a.JPEG)

    Hence, between the roots, $a$ and $b$, will be the region for which $f(x)$ is concave down.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \textcolor{white}{hack} \tag*{\textbf{M1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    To find $a$ and $b$ we can use the quadratic formula.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
       0&=x^2-8x+14\\[6pt]
       x&=\frac{-(-8)\pm \sqrt{(-8)^2-4\times1 \times 14}}{2\times 1}\tag*{\textbf{M1}}\\[6pt]
       x&=\frac{8\pm \sqrt{8}}{2}\\[6pt]
       x&=4\pm \sqrt{2}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    As $b>a$ then we can say that $f(x)$ is concave down in the region
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \hspace{0.525em}4-\sqrt2 < x< 4+\sqrt 2 \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

5.  Using the chain rule we can differentiate $f(g(x))$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \textcolor{white}{hack} \tag*{\textbf{M1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        [f(g(x))]'&=f'(g(x))\times g'(x)
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Therefore

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        [f(g(2))]'&=f'(g(2))\times g'(2)
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    We were given $g'(2)=3$ and $g(2)=5$, hence

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        [f(g(2))]' &=f'(5)\times 3 \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Using part (b) we can find $f'(5)$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    f'(5)&=-e^{-5}(5^2-6\times 5+8)\\[6pt]
    &=-e^{-5}(3)\\[6pt]
    &=-3e^{-5}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Hence we can evaluate $[f(g(x))]'$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    [f(g(x))]'&=f'(5)\times 3 \\[6pt]
    &=-3e^{-5}\times 3 \\[6pt]
    &\hspace*{-4.35em}[f(g(x))]'=-9e^{-5} \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
