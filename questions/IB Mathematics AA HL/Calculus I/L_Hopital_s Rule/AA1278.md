# AA1278

**Maximum mark: 8**

Consider the function $\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{\cos(mx)-\cos(nx)}{x^2}$ where $m,n \in \mathbb{R}$.

The function has a maximum value of $f_{\text{max}}$ and it is known that $\displaystyle f_{\text{max}}= \lim_{x \to 0} f(x)$.

1.  Show that $\displaystyle f_{\text{max}}=\frac{n^2-m^2}{2}$. [6 marks]

It is now known that $m>0$ and $n=4\sqrt{m}$.

2.  Hence, using these conditions find the largest possible value of $f_{\text{max}}$. [2 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  To find $f_{\text{max}}$ we need to evaluate $f(x)$ as $x \rightarrow 0$. 

    

    Hence we can write

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        f_{\text{max}}&=\lim_{x \to 0} \left(\frac{\cos(mx)-\cos(nx)}{x^2}\right)

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    We can begin by attempting to evaluate the limit using direct substitution of $x=0$, recall $\cos{0}=1$.    

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        f_{\text{max}}&= \frac{\cos(0)-\cos(0)}{(0)^2}\tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}\\[12pt]

        &= \frac{1-1}{(0)^2}\\[12pt]

        &=\frac{0}{0}

    \end{aligned}

    $$    

    This is an indeterminate form. Hence we can use L'Hôpital's Rule to attempt to find the limit.

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

         \tag*{\textbf{R1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Using the standard derivative for cosine and the power rule we can differentiate the numerator and denominator to get

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        f_{\text{max}}&=\lim_{x \to 0} \left(\frac{-m\sin(mx)-(-n\sin(nx))}{2x}\right)\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{M1}}}\\[12pt]

        &=\lim_{x \to 0} \left(\frac{-m\sin(mx)+n\sin(nx)}{2x}\right)

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Again we can attempt to evaluate the limit by direct substitution of $x=0$

    $$

     \begin{aligned}

        f_{\text{max}}&=\lim_{x \to 0} \left(\frac{-m\sin(0)+n\sin(0))}{2(0)}\right)\\[12pt]

        &=\frac{0}{0}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Again we have an indeterminate form. Let's try L'Hôpital's Rule one more time.

    $$

     \begin{aligned}

        f_{\text{max}}&=\lim_{x \to 0} \left(\frac{-m^2\cos(mx)+n^2\cos(nx)}{2}\right)\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    As $\cos{0}=1$ we get 

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

         \tag*{\textbf{M1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        &=\frac{-m^2+n^2}{2}\\[12pt]

        &=\dfrac{n^2-m^2}{2} \hspace{2em}\text{...as required}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

2.  We can take the result from part (b) and replace $n$ with $4\sqrt{m}$, this gives $f_{\text{max}}$ in terms of $m$

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        f_{\text{max}}&=\frac{(4\sqrt{m})^2-m^2}{2}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    This is a quadratic function in $m$. 

    

    By completing the square we can write $f_\text{max}$ in vertex form and find the coordinates of the maximum point. 

    

    Note, although we are told to look for a maximum value we can also see that as the coefficient of $m^2$ is negative then the parabola will be concave down hence the vertex will be a maximum point.

    When completing the square it is often easier to work with a positive squared term hence we will factorise by $-\tfrac{1}{2}$ which gives 

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        f_{\text{max}}&=-\frac{1}{2}(m^2-16m)

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Now we can complete the square

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        &=-\frac{1}{2}\left[(m-8)^2-64\right]\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(M1)}}}\\[6pt]

        &=-\frac{1}{2}(m-8)^2+32

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Hence the largest value of $f_\text{max}$ is $32$    

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

         \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$
