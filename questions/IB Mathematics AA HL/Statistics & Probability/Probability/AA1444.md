# AA1444

**Maximum mark: 14**

 

A class contains $30$ students. Each student studies chemistry ($C$), physics ($P$), both subjects, or neither subject. It is given that $18$ students study chemistry, $14$ students study physics and $4$ students study neither subject.

 

The Venn diagram below records the number of students in each region of $U$. The values $p$, $q$, $r$ and $s$ represent numbers of students.

![AA1444a](images/AA1444a.png)

1.  1. Write down the value of $s$.

    2. Find the value of $r$.

    

    3. Write down the value of $p$ and the value of $q$.[5 marks]

A student is chosen at random from the class. 

2.  1. Given that the student does not study physics, find the probability that the student studies chemistry.

    2. Hence show that studying chemistry and studying physics are not independent events.[5 marks]

Two students are chosen at random from the class, one after the other.

3. Find the probability that at least one of the two students studies physics. [4 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1.  1. In the diagram, $s$ is outside of both circles. Hence it represents the number of students who study neither subject.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

            s &= \hspace{0em}4 \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

 

    2. We know that the total of all four regions of the Venn diagram is the class size, $30$. 

        Adding the three given totals we have

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

            n(C)+n(P)+n(C'\cap P')&=18+14+4\\[6pt]

            &=36.\tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        This is greater than the number of students in the class. 

        Notice that $r$ lies inside both circles, so students in this region have been counted in both $n(C)$ and $n(P)$ ($r$ has been counted twice). The extra amount over the class size is therefore the value of $r$.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

            r &= 36-30  \\[6pt]

            &= \hspace{0em}6 \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

 

    3. To find $p$ and $q$ we need to subtract the overlap from each set total.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

            p &= n(C) - r \\[6pt]

            &= 18 - 6 \\[6pt]

            &= \hspace{0em}12 \tag*{\textbf{A1}} \\[6pt]

            q &= n(P) - r \\[6pt]

            &= 14 - 6 \\[6pt]

            &= \hspace{0em}8 \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

 

2.  1. We need to use the **conditional probability formula (4.6)**.

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

            \mathrm{P}(A \mid B) = \dfrac{\mathrm{P}(A \cap B)}{\mathrm{P}(B)}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Considering the context of the question we have 

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

            \mathrm{P}(C \mid P') = \dfrac{\mathrm{P}(C \cap P')}{\mathrm{P}(P')}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        The numerator counts students in the $\text{\textquotedblleft}$only chemistry" region ($p = 12$). 

        The denominator counts students who do not study physics ($30 - n(P) = 16$).

        Therefore

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

            \mathrm{P}(C \mid P') &= \dfrac{12}{16} \tag*{\textbf{(M1)}} \\[6pt]

            &= \hspace{0em}\dfrac{3}{4} \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

 

    2. This is a show that question with the word $\text{\textquoteleft}$hence'. Therefore we need to use the previous answer in our solution. 

        If two events were independent, conditioning on $P'$ would leave the probability of $C$ unchanged, so $\mathrm{P}(C \mid P') = \mathrm{P}(C)$. 

        $$\\[-2.3em]$$

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

            \tag*{{\textbf{R1}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        Hence we can compare the value of $\mathrm{P}(C \mid P')$ with $\mathrm{P}(C)$. 

        $$\\[-2.3em]$$

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

            \tag*{{\textbf{M1}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

        

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

            \mathrm{P}(C) &= \dfrac{18}{30}\\[6pt]

            &= \dfrac{3}{5} \\[6pt]

            \mathrm{P}(C \mid P') &= \dfrac{3}{4} 

        \end{aligned}

        $$

 

        Since $\dfrac{3}{5} \neq \dfrac{3}{4}$, the events $\text{\textquotedblleft}$studies chemistry" and $\text{\textquotedblleft}$studies physics" are $\hspace{0em}\text{not independent}\hspace{0.2em}$.

        $$\\[-2.3em]$$

        $$

        \begin{aligned}

            \tag*{{\textbf{A1}}}

        \end{aligned}

        $$

3. If we want to find $\text{\textquotedblleft}$at least one student studies physics" this is either $\text{\textquotedblleft}$one student studies physics" or $\text{\textquotedblleft}$two students study physics". 

    We could calculate both of these probabilities or, as this is a non-calculator question, we should recognize that we get an equivalent result if we find $1$ minus the probability that $\text{\textquotedblleft}$neither student studies physics".

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        \mathrm{P}(\text{at least one studies }P) &= 1 - \mathrm{P}(\text{neither studies }P) \tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    We calculated $n(P')=16$ in part b(i). 

    The probability the first student selected does not study physics is $\dfrac{16}{30}$.

    The probability the second student selected does not study physics is $\dfrac{15}{29}$.

    (this is an example of drawing without replacement).

    $$\\[-2.3em]$$

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        \tag*{{\textbf{(A1)}}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$

    Then, multiplying these probabilities and subtracting from one

    $$

    \begin{aligned}

        \mathrm{P}(\text{at least one studies }P) &=1- \dfrac{16}{30} \times \dfrac{15}{29} \tag*{\textbf{(A1)}} \\[9pt]

        &=1-\dfrac{8}{29} \\[9pt]

        \mathrm{P}(\text{at least one studies }P) &= \hspace{0em}\dfrac{21}{29} \tag*{\textbf{A1}}

    \end{aligned}

    $$
