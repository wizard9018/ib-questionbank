# AA1057

**Maximum mark: 18**

The duration, $E$, expressed in months, of the $\textit{Everlast}$ brand of car battery, follows a normal distribution with a mean of $42$ months and a standard deviation $\sigma$ months.

It is known that $10\%$ of the $\textit{Everlast}$ batteries will have a larger duration than $60$ months.

1. Find the value of $\sigma$. [4 marks]

2. Find the probability that an $\textit{Everlast}$ battery lasts less than $12$ months.[2 marks]

A battery is considered defective if it lasts less than $12$ months.

A retailer has purchased a batch of $550$ $\textit{Everlast}$ batteries.

3.  1. What is the probability the retailer finds at least four defective batteries?

    2. What is the probability the retailer finds at least $8$ defective batteries, given that at least four defective batteries have been found?

    3. Find the expected number of defective batteries purchased by the retailer. Round your answer to the nearest integer.[7 marks]

The retailer has sold the entire batch of batteries and needs to buy new ones.

They have decided to try another brand of batteries called $\textit{Permalast}$.

Let the number of defective batteries in a batch be represented by the discrete random variable $D$. With $D_e$ being the number of defective $\textit{Everlast}$ batteries in a batch and $D_p$ being the number of defective $\textit{Permalast}$ batteries in a batch.

The probability that a single $\textit{Permalast}$ battery is defective is $k$, where, $0.5<k<1$.

The retailler buys a new batch of $550$ $\textit{Everlast}$ and $550$ $\textit{Permalast}$ batteries.

It is known that $4\text{Var}(D_e)<\text{Var}(D_p)<5\text{Var}(D_e)$.

4. Find the range of possible values for $k$. [5 marks]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1. Using that $E\sim \mathrm{N}(42, \sigma^2)$ and $Z\sim \mathrm{N}(0,1)$, we have
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathrm{P}(X > 60) &= 0.10 \tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}\\[8pt]
        \mathrm{P}\left(Z > \dfrac{60-42}{\sigma}\right) &= 0.1 \\[8pt]
        \mathrm{P}\left(Z < \dfrac{60-42}{\sigma}\right) &= 1-0.1 \\[8pt]
        \dfrac{18}{\sigma} &= \text{invNorm}(0.9, 0, 1) \tag*{\textbf{M1A1}}\\[8pt]
        \sigma &= \frac{18}{1.28155...} \hspace{5.52em}[\hspace{0.1em}\text{by using G.D.C.}\hspace{0.1em}]\\[8pt]
        &=14.04547...\\[6pt]
        &\hspace{-0.525em}\sigma = 14.0  \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

2. Using that $E\sim \mathrm{N}(42, \sigma^2)$, we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathrm{P}(X < 12) &= \text{normCdf}(-\infty, 12, 42, 14.045...) \tag*{\textbf{M1}}\\[8pt]
        &=0.0163429...\hspace{5.52em} [\hspace{0.1em}\text{by using G.D.C.}\hspace{0.1em}]\\[6pt]
        &\hspace{0.29em} = 0.0163 \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

3. 1. Let $X$ be the number of defective batteries among the purchased batteries.

    Then, we have $X \sim \mathrm{B}(550,0.0163)$.
    $$
    \tag*{\textbf{M1}}
    $$

    Hence the probability of finding at least $4$ defective batteries is
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathrm{P}(Y \geq 4) &=\text{binomCdf}(550, 0.0163..., 4, 550) \\[8pt]
        &=0.97933...\\[6pt]
        &\hspace{0.275em}= 0.979 \hspace{3em} [\text{by using G.D.C.}] \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    2.  Using the conditional probability formula $\mathrm{P}(A\,|\,B) = \dfrac{\mathrm{P}(A \cap B)}{\mathrm{P}(B)}$, we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathrm{P}(X \geq 8 \hspace{0.1em}|\hspace{0.1em} X \geq 4) &= \dfrac{\mathrm{P}\left(\{X \geq 8\} \cap \{X \geq 4\}\right)}{\mathrm{P}(X \geq 4)} \hspace{1em} \tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}\\[10pt]
        &= \dfrac{\mathrm{P}(X \geq 8)}{\mathrm{P}(X \geq 4)} \\[10pt]
        &= \dfrac{\text{binomCdf}(550, 0.0163..., 8, 550)}{0.97933...} \tag*{\textbf{A1}}\\[8pt]
        &=0.690970...\\[6pt]
        &\hspace{0.275em}=0.691 \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    3. Using the formula for the mean of a binomial distribution, we obtain
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \text{E(X)} &= 550 \times 0.0163... \tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}\\[9pt]
        &=8.9886...\\[6pt]
                        &\hspace{0.275em}\boxed{= 9} \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
4. We can (re)define the two random variables
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        &D_e\sim \mathrm{B}(550,0.0163...) &D_p\sim \mathrm{B}(550,k)
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Using the formula for the variance of a binomial distribution we can evaluate
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \text{Var}(D_e)&=550\times 0.0163...(1-0.0163...)\\
        &=8.8417... \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Therefore we can rewrite the inequality given in the question
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        4\text{Var}(D_e)<\text{Var}(D_p)&<5\text{Var}(D_e)\\
        4\times 8.8417...<\text{Var}(D_p)&<5 \times 8.8417...\\
        35.366...<\text{Var}(D_p)&<44.208... \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Using the same formula again we can write
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        \text{Var}(D_p)&=550k(1-k) \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Therefore we need to solve the following inequality for $k$
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        35.366...<550k(1-k)&<44.208... \tag*{\textbf{(M1)}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        0.912<k<0.931  \tag*{\textbf{A1}}
