# AA1289

**Maximum mark: 18**

_Lesta Laboratory_ conducts experiments on different metals.

A sample of a metal is taken, if the weight falls in a particular range then the sample is used in an experiment. Otherwise it is rejected.

One type of metal is _Alloy X_, for which the samples have a weight that is normally distributed with a mean of $19.6\,$grams and a standard deviation of $2.1\,$grams.

_Lesta Laboratory_ will use a sample of _Alloy X_ in an experiment if the weight is between $17$ g and $23$ g.
1.  Find the probability a randomly selected sample of _Alloy X_ will be used in an experiment.[2 marks]

_Alloy Y_ is another type of metal used at _Lesta Laboratory_.

The weights, $W_Y$, of samples of _Alloy Y_ are  normally distributed with a mean of $\mu_Y$ and a standard deviation of $\sigma_Y$.

It is known that $3.061\%$ of samples of _Alloy Y_ weigh less than $35\,$g.

2.  Show that $\mu_Y=35+1.872\sigma_Y$[2 marks]

It is also known that $\mathrm{P}(W_Y>46)=0.1714$.

3.  1.  Show that $\mu_Y=46-0.9486\sigma_Y$

    2.  Hence write down the values of $\mu_Y$ and $\sigma_Y$.[3 marks]

A sample of _Alloy Y_ is used in an experiment if the weight of the sample lies within $k$ standard deviations, $\sigma_Y$, of its mean, $\mu_Y$. The probability of this occurring is $54.67\%$.

4.  1.  Find $k$.

    2.  If _Lesta Laboratory_ takes $8$ independent  samples of _Alloy Y_ what is the probability more than $5$ are used in an experiment?[6 marks]

_Lesta Laboratory_ is testing some samples of metals for an experiment. They select $2$ samples of _Alloy X_ and $2$ samples of _Alloy Y_.

5.  What is the probability there will be more samples of _Alloy X_ than _Alloy Y_ that can be used in the experiment?[5 marks]

2.  The sketch below represents the region P$(Y<35)=0.03061$

    ![AA1289-a2](images/AA1289-a2.JPEG)

    When $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are unknown we will need to standardise the distribution. For this we will use the formula
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        z&=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    Recall that the C.R.V. $Z$ follows the standard normal distribution such that $Z \sim \mathrm{N}(0,1^2)$.

    In the diagram below, the axis representing the $Z-$distribution is drawn below the distribution for $Y$. The $Z-$value is aligned with $Y=35$, which is the boundary of the region with a probability of $0.03601$.

    ![AA1289-a3](images/AA1289-a3.JPEG)

    To find the $Z-$value associated with P$(Z<0.03061)$ we must use the inverse normal function.
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    z &= \text{invNorm}(0.03061,0,1) \\[6pt]
    &=-1.871...\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(A1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Substituting $z$ and $35$ into the formula
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        -1.8719...&=\frac{35-\mu_Y}{\sigma_Y}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{M1}}}\\[6pt]
        -1.872\,\sigma_Y&=35-\mu_Y\\[6pt]
    &\hspace{-1.5em}\mu_Y=35+1.872\sigma_Y\hspace{2em}\text{...as required.}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{AG}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    2.  In this part of the question we can define a new discrete random variable $S$ as the number of samples used in an experiment.

        Where $S=\{0,1...,8\}$.

        Addtionally
        - We are given a fixed number of trials $n=8$.

        - he possible outcomes of each trial are the sample is $\textit{used}$ or $\textit{not used}$. i.e. each trial is a Bernoulli trial.

        - The probability of success, $p=0.5467$, of an individual trial is independent of any other trial - note we can assume this.

        Hence the conditions have been fulfilled such that $S$ follows a binomial distribution.

        Therefore we can say that
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
            S& \sim B(n,p)\\[6pt]
            S &\sim B(8,0.5467)
        \end{aligned}
        $$
        Where $n$ is the number of trials and $p$ is the probability of success of a single trial.

        When we are asked for $\textbf{more than}$ $5$ selected we can use the G.D.C. to calculate
        $$
        \begin{aligned}
        \mathrm{P}(Y>5) &=\text{binomCdf}(8,0.5467,6,8) \tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(M1)}}}\\[12pt]
        &=0.2145...\\[8pt]
        &=0.215\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}
        \end{aligned}
        $$

5.  The probability of a usable _Alloy X_ sample is $0.8394...$ from part (a).

    The probability of a usable _Alloy Y_ sample is $0.5467$ from part (d).

    We can use more of _Alloy X_ than _Alloy Y_ in the following ways
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        2\text{}\textit{ Alloy X}\text{ and }0\textit{ Alloy Y}\\[6pt]
        2\textit{ Alloy X}\text{ and }1\textit{ Alloy Y}\\[6pt]
        1\textit{ Alloy X}\text{ and }0\textit{ Alloy Y}\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(M1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Let's calculate the probability of each of these outcomes.

    There is only $1$ way to choose $2$ samples of $X$ (or $Y$) or $0$ samples of $X$ (or $Y$).

    Hence for $2\textit{ Alloy X}\text{ and }0\textit{ Alloy Y}$ we have
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        (0.8394...)^2\times(1-0.5467)^2&=0.1447...\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(A1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    However there are $2$ ways to choose $1$ sample of $Y$. We can either choose the first and reject the second or reject the first and choose the second. Therefore we need to multiply the probability by $2$.

    Hence for $2\textit{ Alloy X}\text{ and }1\textit{ Alloy Y}$ we have
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        (0.8394)^2\times2(1-0.5467)(0.5467)&=0.3492...\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(A1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    and similarly for $1\textit{ Alloy X}\text{ and }0\textit{ Alloy Y}$ we have
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        2(0.8394...)(1-0.8394)\times(1-0.5467)^2&=0.05540...\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{(A1)}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Adding them all together we get
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
        P(\text{More samples of }\textit{Alloy X}\text{ than } \textit{Alloy Y})&=0.1447...+0.3492...+0.05540...\\[6pt]
        &=0.5494...\\[6pt]
        &=0.549\tag*{\textbf{\textcolor{black}{A1}}}
    \end{aligned}
    $$
