## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

1. Use of Wien's Law

    ${\lambda}_{max} T= 2.9 \times 10^{-3}$

    OR

    $T = \dfrac{2.9 \times 10^{-3}}{{\lambda}_{max}}$ $\checkmark$

    Substitute and solve,

    $T=\dfrac{2.9 \times 10^{-3}}{6.90 \times 10^{-7}}$

    $T = 4200\, \ce{K}$ $\checkmark$

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:\
    Stars are described as blackbody emitters that produce a continuous spectrum with a peak intensity at a specific wavelength, ${\lambda}_{max}$.  Wien's Law states that this wavelength is inversely proportional to the temperature, $T$, of the star.

    - ${\lambda}_{max} = \dfrac{b}{T}$

    Rearranged,

    - $T=\dfrac{b}{{\lambda}_{max}}$

    The proportionality constant, $b$, is $2.9 \times 10^{-3}\, \ce{m}\, \ce{K}$.

    This gives,

    - $T=\dfrac{2.9 \times 10^{-3}}{{\lambda}_{max}}$

    Substituting and solving (noting that $1\, \ce{nm} = 10^{-9}\, \ce{m}$),

    - $T=\dfrac{2.9 \times 10^{-3}}{6.90\times10^{-7}}$

    - $T= 4200\, \ce{K}$

2. 1. The star/Star A is moving away from the observer $\checkmark$

        The $\underline{\textrm{Doppler Effect}}$ results in the wavelength being longer/$\ll$Doppler$\gg$ shifted towards red end of spectrum $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:\
        Relative motion between an observer and a source of waves results in the Doppler Effect where waves are observed to have a different frequency than those emitted.  When a source of waves is moving away from the observer, the distance between wavecrests increases - the wavelength increases and frequency decreases.  This difference is described as a Doppler “shift”. Note that the wave speed remains the same.

        The emission spectrum from a star is said to be “red-shifted” when the star is moving away from an observer.  This is due to red wavelengths being at the longer end of the visible spectrum.  If a star were moving towards an observer the spectrum would be described as “blue-shifted” since the observed wavelengths would be shorter than those emitted.

    2. Use of (accept equal sign),

        $\dfrac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda} \approx \dfrac{v}{c}$ $\checkmark$

        $v \approx c \dfrac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda}$

        Substitution and solve,

        $v \approx (3.00\times10^8)\Bigl(\dfrac{663-656}{656}\Bigr)$ $\checkmark$

        $v\approx 3.2\times10^6\, \ce{m}\,\ce{s}^{-1}$ $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$:\
        When a source is moving relative to an observer and emitting a wave of frequency, $f$, the observed frequency, $f'$, is given by,

        - $f'=f+\Delta f$

        Similarly, the observed wavelength, $\lambda '$, is given by,

        - $\lambda '=\lambda+\Delta \lambda$

        Both the frequency and wavelength are both shifted by small amounts in comparison to their original value, we can use the data booklet formula

        - $\dfrac{\Delta f}{f}=\dfrac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda}$

        When the speed of the source is very small compared to the wave speed ($v\ll c$), as is the case with a moving star emitting electromagnetic waves, it can be further said that,

        - $\dfrac{\Delta f}{f}=\dfrac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda}\approx \dfrac{v}{c}$

        With reference to wavelength and rearranging,

        - $v \approx c \dfrac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda}$

        Substitution and solve,

        - $v\approx (3.00\times10^8)\Bigl(\dfrac{663-656}{656}\Bigr)$

        - $v\approx3.2\times10^6\,\ce{m}\,\ce{s}^{-1}$
