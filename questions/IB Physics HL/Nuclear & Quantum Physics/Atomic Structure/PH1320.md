# PH1320

**Maximum mark: 8**

In the Bohr atomic model, an electron is in orbit in the ground energy level around a hydrogen nucleus.

![Screenshot 2026-01-05 at 10.31.54 AM](<images/Screenshot 2026-01-05 at 10.31.54 AM.png>)

1. 1. Explain the force that keeps the electron in orbit. [2 marks]

    2. Show that the total energy of an orbiting electron can be expressed as:

        $E_{total}=-k\dfrac{e^2}{2r}$

        where $e$ is the elementary charge and $r$ is the radius of the orbit. [3 marks]

According to Bohr's condition for quantization of angular momentum, angular momentum of electron is quantized and it is an integer multiple of $\dfrac{h}{2\pi}$. The result of this is that the electron could only exist in certain orbital radii.

2. In the $n=2$ state of the hydrogen atom according to the Bohr model, the radius of the electron orbit is $2.12\times10^{-10}$ m. Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of the electron which is orbiting in $n=2$. [3 marks]

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$: \
        In the Bohr model, the reason the electron stays in its orbit is due to the electric force of attraction between the positive nucleus and the negative electron. This force prevents the electron from spiraling into the nucleus and maintains its stable orbit (maintain centripetal acceleration).  Thus, the electric force of attraction between the nucleus and electron is the primary force that keeps the electron in orbit.

    2. ::indent
        - $E_{total}=E_{kinetic}+E_{potential}$

        where

        - $F_{centripetal}=F_{Coulomb}$; $\dfrac{mv^2}{r}=k\dfrac{e^2}{r^2}$

        and

        - $E_{kinetic}=\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2=\dfrac{1}{2}k\dfrac{e^2}{r}$ $\checkmark$

        - $E_{potential}=-k\dfrac{e^2}{r}$ $\ll$ electrical potential energy $\gg$ $\checkmark$

        - $E_{total}=\dfrac{1}{2}k\dfrac{e^2}{r}+(-k\dfrac{e^2}{r})$
        $E_{total}=-k\dfrac{e^2}{2r}$ $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$: \
        The total energy of an electron orbiting a nucleus in the Bohr model is the sum of its kinetic energy and potential energy.

        $1.$ Potential Energy:

        The potential energy of an electron at a distance $r$ from the nucleus is given by:

        - $E_{potential}=-k\dfrac{e^2}{r}$

        where $k$ is Coulomb's constant, $e$ is the elementary charge, and $r$ is the radius of the orbit.

        The negative sign indicates that the force is attractive (since potential energy decreases as the electron moves closer to the nucleus).

        $2.$ Kinetic Energy:

        The electron moves in a circular orbit, so the centripetal force required to keep it in orbit is provided by the Coulomb force:

        - $F_{centripetal}=F_{Coulomb}$

        - $F_{centripetal}=\dfrac{mv^2}{r}$ and $F_{Coulomb}=k\dfrac{e^2}{r^2}$

        Equating the two expressions for the centripetal force gives us:

        - $mv^2=k\dfrac{e^2}{r}$

        Solving for the kinetic energy gives $E_{kinetic}=\dfrac{1}{2}k\dfrac{e^2}{r}$

        $3.$ Total Energy:

        The total energy of the electron is the sum of its kinetic and potential energies:

        - $E_{total}=E_{kinetic}+E_{potential}$

        - $E_{total}=\dfrac{1}{2}k\dfrac{e^2}{r}+(-k\dfrac{e^2}{r})$

        Simplifying the expression:

        - $E_{total}=-k\dfrac{e^2}{2r}$

2. Bohr condition:

    - $mvr=n\dfrac{h}{2\pi}$

    de Broglie wavelength: $\lambda=\dfrac{h}{p} \hspace{2em}$ giving $\hspace{2em} mv=\dfrac{h}{\lambda}$ $\checkmark$

    - $\dfrac{h}{\lambda}r=n\dfrac{h}{2\pi}$

    - $\lambda=r\dfrac{2\pi}{n}$ $\checkmark$

    - $\ll\lambda=(2.12\times10^{-10})\dfrac{2\pi}{2}\gg$

    - $\lambda=$ :answer[$6.66\times10^{-10}$ $\ll \text{m}\gg$] $\checkmark$

    $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$: \
    According to Bohr's model, the angular momentum ($L$) of an electron is quantized and given by:

    - $L=mvr=n\dfrac{h}{2\pi}$ $\quad$ (1)

    The de Broglie wavelength ($\lambda$) of a particle is given by:

    - $\lambda=\dfrac{h}{p}$ $\quad$ (2)

    where $p$ is linear momentum ($p=mv$)

    Rearrange (1) as $mv=n\dfrac{h}{2\pi r}$, and (2) as $p=\dfrac{h}{\lambda}$ and equating both:

    - $n\dfrac{h}{2\pi r}=\dfrac{h}{\lambda}$ $\quad$ (3)

    Cancel $h$ from both side and re-arrange (3) to calculate $\lambda$;

    - $\lambda=r\dfrac{2\pi}{n}$

    For $n=2$, $r$ is given as $2.12\times10^{-10}$ m:

    - $\lambda=2.12\times10^{-10}\Bigl(\dfrac{2\pi}{2}\Bigr)$

    - $\lambda = 6.66\times10^{-10}$ m
