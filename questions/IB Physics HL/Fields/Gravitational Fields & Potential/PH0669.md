# PH0669

**Maximum mark: 16**

Geostationary satellites are satellites that complete one circular orbit in the same amount of time it takes for the Earth to rotate once on its axis. A geostationary satellite is placed in an orbit at $3.6\times{10}^7$ m above the Earth\'s surface, at the equator.  The radius of the Earth is $6.4\times{10}^3$ km.

![PH0669](images/PH0669.png)

1.  The satellite undergoes uniform circular motion.

    1. Define gravitational field strength. [2 marks]

    2. Show that the linear speed of the satellite is approximately $3.1\times{10}^3\, \ce{m}\,\ce{s}^{-1}$. [3 marks]

    3. Calculate the gravitational field strength at the point in the orbit of the satellite. [3 marks]

2. Another geostationary satellite of mass $m$ is orbiting around Earth. The radius of the circular path it follows is $r$.

    1. Deduce that the total energy of the satellite is $E=-\dfrac{GMm}{2r}$. [3 marks]

    2. Due to several factors, the radius of the orbit of the satellite can decrease over time. Calculate the energy required to return a satellite with a mass of $2.2\times{10}^3\ \ce{kg}$ from an orbit with a radius of $4.1\times{10}^7\ \ce{m}$ to its geostationary orbit with a radius of $4.2\times{10}^7\ \ce{m}$. The mass of the Earth is $6.0\times{10}^{24}\, \ce{kg}$ [2 marks]

    3. Identify with ticks [$\checkmark$] the changes in kinetic, potential and total energy of the satellite when it is is moved to a higher orbit as described in b(ii). [2 marks]

    2. $d=2\pi r=\left(2\right)\pi\left(3.6\times{10}^7+6.4\times{10}^6\right)$ $\checkmark$

        The radius $r$ of the orbit from the centre of the earth (after converting $6.4\times 10^3\, \ce{km}$ to $6.4 \times 10^6\, \ce{m}$):

        $\hspace{1em}$ $r=3.6\times{10}^7\ \ce{m} +6.4\times{10}^6\ \ce{m}$

        The distance $d$ travelled by the satellite in one revolution:

        $\hspace{1em}$ $d=2\pi r=\left(2\right)\pi\left(3.6\times{10}^7\ +6.4\times{10}^6\right)\ \ce{m}$

        The time $t$ of one revolution:

        $\hspace{1em}$ $t=\left(24\right)\left(60\right)\left(60\right)=8.6\times{10}^4\, \ce{s}$

        The linear speed $v$ of the satellite:

        $\hspace{1em}$ $v=\dfrac{d}{t}=\dfrac{\left(2\right)\pi\left(3.6\times{10}^7\ +6.4\times{10}^6\right)\ }{8.6\times{10}^4}$

        $\hspace{1em}$ $=3.10\times{10}^3\, \ce{m}\,\ce{s}^{-1}$

    3. Any evidence linking gravitational field strength and the centripetal acceleration $\checkmark$

        $\hspace{1em}$ $\dfrac{v^2}{r}=\dfrac{{(3.1\times{10}^3)}^2}{(3.6\times{10}^7\ +6.4\times{10}^6)}$ $\checkmark$

        $\hspace{1em}$ :answer[$2.3\times{10}^{-1}\, \ce{N}\,\ce{kg^{-1}}\ or \ \ce{m}\,\ce{s}^{-1}$] $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$: \
The force $F$ acting on the satellite at that point should be the centripetal force that causes the satellite to orbit the Earth:

        $\hspace{1em}$ $F=mg=\dfrac{mv^2}{r}$

        Where $m$ is the mass of the satellite, $v$ is the linear speed of the satellite, and $r$ is the separation between the centre of the earth and the satellite.

        $\hspace{1em}$ $g=\dfrac{v^2}{r}=\dfrac{{(2.6\times{10}^3)}^2}{(3.6\times{10}^7\ +6.4\times{10}^6)}=2.3\times{10}^{-1}\, \ce{m}\,\ce{s}^{-2}$

2.
    1. $\hspace{1em}$ $\dfrac{GmM}{r}=mv^2$ $\checkmark$

        $\hspace{1em}$ $E=\dfrac{mv^2}{2}+\left(-\dfrac{GMm}{r}\right)$ $\checkmark$

        $\hspace{1em}$ $E=\dfrac{GmM}{2r}+\left(-\dfrac{GMm}{r}\right)=-\dfrac{GMm}{2r}$ $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$: \
        The gravitational force acting on the satellite is the centripetal force:

        $\hspace{1em}$ $\dfrac{GmM}{r^2}=\dfrac{mv^2}{r}$

        It can be simplified as:

        $\hspace{1em}$ $\dfrac{GmM}{r}=mv^2$

        Or using

        $\hspace{1em}$ $v=\sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}}$

        $\hspace{1em}$ $E_K=\ \frac{1}{2}mv^2=\dfrac{GMm}{2r}$

        The total energy of the satellite is the sum of kinetic energy $E_K$ and potential energy $E_P$ that it possesses:

        $\hspace{1em}$ $E=E_K+E_P$

        $\hspace{1em}$ $E=\dfrac{mv^2}{2}+\left(-\dfrac{GMm}{r}\right)$

        Expression in the first relationship can be replaced with $mv^2$:

        $\hspace{1em}$ $E=\dfrac{GmM}{2r}+\left(-\dfrac{GMm}{r}\right)=-\dfrac{GMm}{2r}$

    2. $\dfrac{\left(6.67\times{10}^{-11}\right)\left(6.0\times{10}^{24}\right)\left(2.2\times{10}^3\right)}{2}\left(\dfrac{1}{4.1\times{10\ }^7}-\dfrac{1}{4.2\times{10\ }^7}\right) \ \ce{J} $ $\checkmark$

        :answer[$2.6\times{10}^8\ \ \ce{J}$] $\checkmark$

        *Deduct 1 mark for negative answers*

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$: \
        The difference in the total energy of the satellite $\Delta E$, between its initial orbit at a radius $r_i$ of  $4.1\times{10\ }^7\ \ce{m}$ and its final orbit at a radius $r_f$ of $4.2\times{10\ }^7\ \ce{m}$   is:

        $\hspace{1em}$ $\Delta E = - \dfrac{GMm}{2r_i} - \left(-\dfrac{GMm}{2r_f}\right)$

        $\hspace{1em}$ $\Delta E = \dfrac{GMm}{2} \left(\dfrac{1}{r_f} - \dfrac{1}{r_i}\right)$

        $\hspace{1em}$ $\Delta E = \dfrac{(6.67 \times 10^{-11})(6.0 \times 10^{24})(2.2 \times 10^3)}{2} \left(\dfrac{1}{4.1 \times 10^7} - \dfrac{1}{4.2 \times 10^7}\right)$

        $\hspace{1em}$ $\Delta E = 2.6 \times 10^8 \ \ce{J}$

    3.  *One mark for both increases indicated.\
        One mark for decrease in KE indicated*
        | | Increases | Decreases |Remains Constant|
        | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: |
        | Kinetic Energy |  | $\checkmark$  | |

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$: \
        As calculated in b(ii), the total energy increases as it is moved to a higher orbit.

        The kinetic energy in orbit is
$E_K=\dfrac{GmM}{2r}$

        As $r$ increases, this value will decrease.

        Alternatively, the orbital speed equation is

        $\hspace{1em}$ $v=\sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}}$

        As $r$ increases, orbital speed decreases, and therefore through

         $\hspace{1em}$ $E_K=\ \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$

        The kinetic energy will decrease.

        Gravitational potential energy is given by the equation

         $\hspace{1em}$ $E_P=\dfrac{-GmM}{r}$

        As $r$ increases, the value for $E_P$ will become less negative, which is a net increase in gravitational potential energy.

        Similarly, total energy is given by

         $\hspace{1em}$ $E_{Total}=\dfrac{-GmM}{2r}$

        As $r$ increases, the value for $E_{Total}$ will become less negative, which is a net increase in gravitational potential energy.
