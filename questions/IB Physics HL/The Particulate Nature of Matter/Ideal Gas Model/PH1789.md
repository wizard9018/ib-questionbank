        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$: \
        For an ideal gas, internal energy is equal to kinetic energy. The potential energy of an ideal gas is assumed to be zero because one of the assumptions of the ideal gas model is that there are no intermolecular forces between the particles.

    2. This equation only applies to **monatomic** ideal gases. $\checkmark$

        $\underline{\textrm{Further Explanation}}$: \
        For an ideal gas, internal energy is equal to kinetic energy as potential energy is zero. However, the kinetic energy equation, $E_k=\dfrac{3}{2}nRT$ only applies to monatomic ideal gases.

        $\underline{\textrm{Extra information above and beyond the syllabus}}$

        The equation $E_k=\dfrac{3}{2}nRT$ is derived from the ideal gas model for monatomic gases, where the internal energy is purely due to the kinetic energy of the gas particles. This equation assumes that the gas consists of particles that have only translational motion and no rotational or vibrational energy modes, which is true for monatomic gases like noble gases (e.g., helium or neon).

        However, air is a mixture of diatomic gases, primarily nitrogen ($\textrm{N}_2$) and oxygen ($\textrm{O}_2$). Diatomic molecules have additional energy contributions beyond just translational motion. These molecules also have rotational and, at higher temperatures, vibrational energy modes. This increases the total internal energy of the gas.

        For diatomic gases, the correct expression for the internal energy would account for these extra degrees of freedom. In general, the internal energy for a diatomic ideal gas can be expressed as:

        - $E_k=\dfrac{5}{2}nRT$

        This includes translational, rotational, and, at sufficiently high temperatures, vibrational contributions to the energy.
