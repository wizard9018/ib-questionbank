## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:

$\hspace{1em}$ $\varepsilon\ =\ I(r\ +\ R)\ =\ Ir\ +\ IR\ =\ V\ +\ Ir$

Where $V$ is the potential difference between the terminals (what the voltmeter is measuring in this question) and $I$ is the current in the circuit.

$\varepsilon$ is the emf of the cell and $𝑟$ is the internal resistance of the cell – both of which are constant.

Rearranging $\varepsilon\ =\ V\ +\ Ir$ allows us to write:

$\hspace{1em}$ $V\ =\ -Ir\ +\ \varepsilon$

(Because $\varepsilon$ and $r$ are constant) we can compare this to $y\ =\ mx\ +\ c$

$\hspace{1em}$ $V\ =\ -Ir\ +\ \varepsilon$

$\hspace{1em}$ $y\ =\ mx\ +\ c$

Graph relating $V$ and $I$ is linear with a negative gradient (of magnitude $r$ and y-intercept of $\varepsilon$).

A more intuitive alternative:

$\hspace{1em}$ $\varepsilon\ =\ I(r\ +\ R)$

As resistance of variable resistor $R$ increases, $R\ +\ r$ increases so $I$ decreases.

$\hspace{1em}$  Potential difference across internal resistance $=\ Ir$

As $I$ deceases, the potential difference across the internal resistance decreases (linearly as Potential difference across internal resistance $α\ I$

$\hspace{1em}$  $V\ =\ \varepsilon\ fr -$ Potential difference across internal resistance

As potential difference across internal resistance decreases $V$ increases linearly

So as $I$ decreases $V$ increases linearly.
