Compare and contrast the role of territorial disputes in the outbreak of **two** wars, each chosen from a different region.

Guidance:
- The primary focus of the responses should be the role of territorial disputes in the start of two wars. Other factors should also be addressed to refer to the “role” element of the question.
- The command term calls for both similarities and differences in the role of territorial disputes in the start of wars. A running comparison structure, rather than a structure organized by wars allows for better analysis, but both can earn marks.
- There are many wars of the 20th century in which territorial disputes were a key cause. Some possible examples include:
    - **The Falklands/Malvinas War (1982):** Arose from a long-standing territorial dispute between Argentina and the United Kingdom over sovereignty of the Falkland Islands (Islas Malvinas). Argentina claimed the islands as part of its national territory, while Britain had taken back control in 1833. Other issues included both British and Argentine domestic politics, national pride/identity, miscommunication/willingness to escalate by the UK. After years of unfruitful diplomatic negotiations, Argentina's military government invaded in 1982 to assert its claim, prompting a British military response to retake the islands.
    - **The Russo-Japanese War (1904-1905):** Driven by rival imperial ambitions in Northeast Asia, particularly over control of Manchuria and Korea. Russia sought to expand its influence in the region after gaining leases in Manchuria, while Japan viewed these areas as vital to its own security and economic interests. Other issues included imperial competition over control of economic resources and national prestige. Failure to reach a diplomatic compromise led Japan to launch a surprise attack on Russian forces in 1904.
    - **The Chaco War (1932-1935):** A conflict between Bolivia and Paraguay centered on disputed territory in the Chaco Boreal region. Both nations claimed the area, which was sparsely populated but believed to hold oil reserves and offered access to the Paraguay River. Other issues included economic pressures due to the Great Depression, national rivalry/prestige, and the role of foreign influence (foreign oil interests). The dispute escalated into full-scale war as each sought to secure control of the region.
    - **The Indo-Pakistani wars (1947-48, 1965, 1971, 1999):** All linked to territorial disputes, especially over Kashmir, whose accession to India at partition in 1947 was contested by Pakistan. Other issues included religious/ideological divides, national identity and legitimacy, Cold War issues/external influences, and internal domestic instability.

**Indicative content for First World War (1914-1918) and the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937-1945):**

$\underline{\textrm{Role of territorial disputes}}$:
- First World War
    - The results of the Franco-Prussian War and France's loss of the provinces of Alsace and Lorraine generated a feeling of resentment and the desire to recover the territories lost in the Treaty of Frankfurt (1871).
    - The incorporation of Bosnia into the Austro-Hungarian Empire (1908) generated resentment in Serbia, which wanted this region for its access to the sea and because its population was mostly ethnically Serb.
- Second Sino-Japanese War
    - Expansionist policies of Japan.
    - The creation of the puppet state of Manchukuo by Japan  (1932) remained unrecognized by Chinese authorities.

$\underline{\textrm{Role of other causes}}$:
- First World War
    - Ideology: Nationalism was the motivation for the assassination of Archduke Franz-Ferdinand. Militarism encouraged the Arms Race among the Great Powers. Imperialism increased rivalries between England and Germany and France and Germany.
    - Failure of diplomacy/Alliance system: The system of alliances turned a war between Austro-Hungary and Serbia into a European conflict (the July Crisis).
    - Economic rivalry: Competition for colonies and markets, particularly between the UK, France and Germany, heightened tensions and mutual suspicion.
- Second Sino-Japanese War
    - Expansionism/resource needs: Japan's resource scarcity drove its goal to secure access to raw materials. Trade embargoes, such as the 1941 oil embargo, also increased pressure.
    - Ideology: Japanese militarism and Shinto led to the belief in the superiority of the nation and justified international aggression. The ideological division in China between the Kuomintang and the Chinese Communist Party contributed to Japanese aggression against what was perceived as a weak adversary due to internal conflict.
    - Failure of diplomacy: The League of Nations' inability to halt Japanese aggression in Manchuria in 1931 encouraged further expansion.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Territorial disputes have often led to armed conflict throughout history. Two wars in which this is evident are WWI (1914-1918) and the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937-1945). It is also true that territorial disputes alone are not enough to explain the outbreak of both wars. Other causes, such as ideology and the failure of diplomacy, were fundamental for the start of these wars ($\checkmark\, \text{organisation}$). This essay will compare and contrast the role of territorial disputes as a cause of the First World War and the Second Sino-Japanese War ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$).

In both wars, territorial disputes were the initial material cause of the conflicts ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$) ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$). In the case of WWI, France's defeat during the Franco-Prussian War and the subsequent loss of the provinces of Alsace and Lorraine motivated its desire for revenge against Germany, both due to the humiliation suffered and to recover its lost territories ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Similarly, in the case of the Second Sino-Japanese War, Japan's desire to maintain its control over Manchuria through the creation of the puppet state of Manchukuo made it want to protect this land ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). The Chinese government refused to recognize this occupation and rejected Japan's expansionist ambitions to dominate East Asia. In both cases, the territories in question were rich in raw materials, giving them economic importance ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). A difference resides in the fact that while France wanted to recover territory that had been taken from it, Japan wanted to protect land that it had taken from China ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In both cases, territorial conflicts were a consequence of the growing nationalism that characterized the second part of the 19th century and the first part of the 20th ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). In World War I, nationalism was a driving ideological force ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). In France, the desire to recover Alsace-Lorraine became a matter of national honor. In the Balkans, Serbian nationalism sought to unite all South Slavs, challenging Austro-Hungarian authority over Bosnia ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Nationalism became closely linked to militarism as a way to expand or recover the European powers` territories. Both militarism and nationalism increased tension in Europe, making it susceptible to war in the face of any minor conflict ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). This was the case with the assassination of Franz Ferdinand by a Serbian terrorist nationalist group that sought to incorporate Bosnia into Serbia ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Japan also shared this militaristic ideology. Coupled with the state-sponsored religion, Shintoism and its belief in the superiority of the Japanese nation and the divine nature of the Emperor who could do no wrong ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$), these beliefs encouraged Japanese aggression. They led to both the invasion of Manchuria and the invasion of mainland China in 1937.

The importance of ideology differs in the outbreak of both wars. All European powers that participated in the war shared the same ideologies of militarism and nationalism. In the case of the Second Sino-Japanese War, China was mired in the middle of a civil war between the contrasting ideologies of the Chinese Communist Party and the Kuomintang ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$) ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). This civil dispute encouraged the Japanese government as it sought to capitalize on China's weakness, exacerbated by the ideological conflict it was experiencing.

Despite the importance of territorial disputes and the ideologies that drove the outbreak of war, they do not suffice to explain the wars ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). The Alsace and Lorraine issue had existed since 1871, while WWI didn't start until 43 years later. The same is true for the Second Japanese War that began in 1937, while Japan had maintained control over Manchuria since 1931 ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$).  In both conflicts, however, we can recognize the failure of diplomacy as a key factor  ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$). In World War I, the rigid system of alliances turned a regional conflict between Austria-Hungary and Serbia into a continent-wide war. Austria-Hungary's participation in the Triple Alliance with Germany and the existence of the Russo-French Alliance since 1894 set the stage for escalating conflict ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). The lack of diplomatic flexibility these alliances created turned territorial tension into total war  ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). To avoid a repetition of this diplomatic failure, the League of Nations was established in 1920. This organization should have stopped Japanese aggression in Manchuria. However, the failure to take decisive action and the fact that the Assembly of the League only ordered Japan to leave almost two years after the invasion ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$) encouraged Japan to keep expanding at the expense of its neighbors. In both wars, diplomacy failed to de-escalate the situation ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In conclusion, territorial disputes were important as causes of both WWI and the Second Sino-Japanese War as they constituted the initial material reason for conflict. However, nationalism and militarism were in part responsible for the territorial disputes as well as an increase in tension ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). This tension could have been resolved through diplomacy, but in both cases, this failed to materialize positively. Therefore, the role of territorial disputes in the outbreak of both wars studied is very similar ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
