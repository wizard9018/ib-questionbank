# HI1047

To what extent did foreign intervention determine the outcome of **two** 20th-century wars?

**Indicative content for Russia and Cuba:**

Foreign intervention
- In Russia
    - Foreign intervention was supportive of the Whites and it provided both men and supplies
    - Foreign intervention ended up undermining the Whites as they were accused of being “puppets” of the imperialist countries of the West as well as Japan, an enemy of Russia since the Russo-Japanese War.
- In Cuba
    - The US provided weapons to the Batista regime until 1958, which were subsequently stolen by the M-26-7 in raids and used by the guerrillas
    - The US supplied Cuba with planes, ships, tanks, and other technology, such as napalm, which was used against the rebels. In 1958, it stopped supporting the regime and introduced an arms embargo against Batista's regime.
    - According to Tad Szulc, the United States began funding the 26th of July Movement around October 1957 and ended around mid-1958, though this is somewhat contested.

Mobilization of human and economic resources
- In Russia
    - Conscription introduced in 1918 by the Bolsheviks
    - War Communism was used to supply the Red Army. This was effective in supplying the troops but generated a famine that led to rebellions.
    - Political officers in each Red Army unit were tasked to provide “political education” and make sure of the loyalty of soldiers and officers

    - In Mexico, Castro had secretly trained a fighting force of 82 volunteers who would sail with him on his mission to liberate Cuba (known as the Granma Expedition), which was funded by a variety of anti-Batista sources.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Foreign intervention in civil wars is a common phenomenon as countries support opposing sides in the conflict either for ideological purposes and/or to keep a degree of influence over the country at war that had been enjoyed so far. This was the case in both the Russian Civil War (1917-1922) and the Cuban Revolution (1953-1959) ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). While foreign intervention in both cases provided temporary advantages to counterrevolutionary forces, it often proved counterproductive ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). The mobilization of human and economic resources, as well as the use of technology, were far more critical to determine both the victory of the 26th of July Movement (M-26-7) against Batista in Cuba and of the Bolsheviks (Reds) in their struggle against the Whites in Russia ($\checkmark\, \text{organization}$). This essay will seek to determine the relative importance of each of these factors ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$).

Even though foreign intervention was aimed at helping win the war, its impact on the outcome of the war was the opposite of what was intended by the countries that assisted both the Whites and Batista ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). In Russia, the Whites received foreign support from the Allied powers, who provided weapons and even troops.  England sent £100 million to buy supplies and the Czechoslovak Legion, for example, consisted of upwards of 50,000 men who at times were able to control the entire Trans-Siberian railway and several major cities in Siberia. At the peak of its intervention, there were roughly 70,000 Japanese Imperial troops in the Russian Far East  ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). However, this assistance undermined the White cause, as Bolshevik propaganda painted them as “puppets” of foreign imperialists, particularly Japan, which had already humiliated Russia in the Russo-Japanese War of 1904-1905 ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Thus, the intervention backfired, consolidating Bolshevik legitimacy among Russians ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). In Cuba, the United States had supported Batista's control over the island since 1933. In the eyes of Cubans, Batista's dictatorship was tied to US economic domination of their country ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). When the revolutionary war started in 1953, it was natural for the US to supply him with weapons, planes, ships, and even napalm, which was used against Castro's guerrillas ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Like the Russian Civil War, this foreign support ended up undermining the legitimacy of Batista ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Part of the appeal of the M-26-7 was precisely its call for the reduction of US economic dominance and the enjoyment of natural resources by the Cuban people. Moreover, by 1958, Washington imposed an arms embargo, effectively cutting Batista off from further resources. With this embargo, Batista had little hope for success, especially if the U.S. was covertly aiding Castro's 26th of July Movement for a brief period between 1957 and 1958, as reported by American journalist Tad Szulc ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). In both cases, foreign intervention weakened the side they were supporting rather than strengthening it, as reliance on external powers gave both the Reds and Castro the opportunity to gain broader support ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

A factor that was much more important for the outcome of both revolutions was the mobilization of human and economic resources ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). In Russia, the Bolsheviks drew on the massive population of the former empire by introducing conscription in 1918. This allowed the Red Army to grow from a few thousand men to half a million by August 1918 and 5 million in 1920 ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). The Whites, who did not control the state apparatus that had remained from Tsarist times, could not muster such a force and relied on volunteers and drafts on the sparsely populated areas they controlled  ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). At the height of its power, the number of soldiers barely surpassed 1 million. By comparison, the M-26-7's fighting force was always smaller than its rival's. Up until 1958, Castro's forces remained quite small in numbers, sometimes fewer than 200 men, while the Cuban army and police force had a manpower of around 37,000 ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). It would seem that this disadvantage in numbers would spell out defeat. However, while much of the fighting in the Russian Civil War involved conventional battles where numbers were important, Cuba's Revolutionary War was fought using guerrilla tactics. In this case, knowledge of the terrain as well as support of the local population would determine the outcome of the war ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$).  Besides, the mobilization of human resources implies not only the amount of active combatants but also the mobilization of the population in general in support of the war. In the case of Russia, this was done through the policy of War Communism which, even though it ended up having disastrous consequences for the Russian people and had to be abandoned in 1921, was able to keep the Red Army supplied ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). In Cuba, support for the rebels was organized by secret groups in the cities that provided resources, printed anti-regime propaganda, and prepared the way for popular uprisings in support of Castro's cause ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Sabotage and the dissemination of propaganda were key parts of the M-26-7's strategy to destabilize the Batista regime. Urban organizations like the Student Revolutionary Directoriate were key for this ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). In both the Russian Civil War and Cuban Revolution, support of the people, whether coerced through conscription and War Communism by the Reds or voluntarily organized like the support for the M-26-7, was key in determining the outcome ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

Finally, technology had a limited impact on the result of both wars ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). In both cases, the countries were relatively poor and predominantly rural so technology was not widely available ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). In the case of Russia, it can be argued that the Reds' control of most of the railroad system for a great part of the war allowed them considerable advantage, especially because White armies were often far away from each other, and couldn't coordinate effectively due to the lack of communication between them. The tachanka, a horse-drawn cart with a mounted machine gun, and some use of artillery also contributed to the Red Army's effectiveness, despite shortages of modern equipment and ammunition ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). In Cuba, by contrast, guerrilla warfare replaced conventional strategies. Castro's forces compensated for their lack of heavy weapons by using innovative technology, particularly through Radio Rebelde, which broadcast propaganda across the island and bolstered support in rural and urban areas ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Many of the weapons the US had provided to Batista were eventually stolen and recycled by the rebels, turning Batista's own resources against him. Thus, while Russian success was linked to conventional military infrastructure like railroads, Cuban success derived from adapting guerrilla tactics to a technologically uneven battlefield ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In conclusion, in both the Russian Civil War and the Cuban Revolutionary War foreign intervention did little to determine the outcome of the conflicts ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Its small impact was also the opposite of what was intended by the intervening powers and the forces they supported in the conflict. The mobilization of human and economic resources was much more important both for the victory of the Reds, who far outweighed the Whites both in men and resources, and for the victory of the 26th of July Movement, which was able to harness the support of the population as well as to make the most of their limited resources through guerrilla tactics ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$). Technology also had a limited role in the wars, being more important in the case of Russia through control of the railways by the Reds, who were able to capitalize on one of the weaknesses of the Whites ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$) ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
