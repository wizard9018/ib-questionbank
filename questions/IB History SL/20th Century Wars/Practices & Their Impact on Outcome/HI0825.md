# HI0825

Analyse **three** examples of international involvement in the Soviet-Afghan War.
[6 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:
\
Any **three** points with supporting explanation:
- United States: Through Operation Cyclone, the CIA covertly funded and armed the Mujahideen as part of its Cold War containment strategy, aiming to weaken Soviet influence. Support included advanced Stinger surface-to-air missiles, which significantly hindered Soviet air superiority by targeting helicopters and low-flying aircraft. This involvement escalated the conflict and indirectly contributed to the rise of Islamist militant groups, including the Taliban and al-Qaeda in later decades.
- Pakistan: The Inter-Services Intelligence (ISI) served as the intermediary for U.S. and Saudi funds and weapons, distributing them to various Mujahideen factions. Pakistan hosted millions of Afghan refugees and training camps near the Durand Line, helping to build a deep cross-border insurgency. Its role enhanced its strategic importance to the West, especially during the Reagan era, though it also fueled extremism within its borders.
- Saudi Arabia: Co-financed the jihad with the U.S., often matching American funding dollar-for-dollar. Promoted the spread of Wahhabi ideology among Mujahideen fighters, viewing the war as a religious struggle against atheist communism. Several Saudi volunteers, including Osama bin Laden, participated, strengthening transnational jihadist networks.
- China: Opposed Soviet expansionism and sought to counter Moscow’s influence in Asia; thus, it supplied weapons, including small arms and mines, to the Mujahideen via Pakistan. This marked a rare instance of tacit alignment with the U.S. during the Cold War, despite China's communist system. China’s support was also motivated by fears of Soviet influence in neighbouring Central Asia and Xinjiang.
- United Nations: Passed non-binding resolutions condemning the Soviet invasion, but no concrete actions (e.g., sanctions or peacekeeping) were taken due to Cold War divisions in the Security Council. The UN provided humanitarian support to Afghan refugees but lacked the authority or consensus to influence the course of the war.
- NATO countries: Generally aligned with the U.S. stance, issuing diplomatic condemnations of Soviet aggression. Some NATO members, such as the UK, also provided indirect logistical and intelligence support, though without committing troops. The war deepened the East-West divide, reinforcing anti-Soviet policies in the West during the final decade of the Cold War.
- Soviet bloc: Warsaw Pact allies (e.g., East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Bulgaria) publicly supported the USSR, framing the war as a defence of socialism against U.S.-backed “terrorists.” However, most did not send troops due to political caution and lack of popular support. The war contributed to growing dissent and disillusionment in Eastern Europe regarding the Soviet leadership, especially as the war dragged on without a clear victory.
\
[6 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Firstly, the United States became heavily involved in the Soviet-Afghan War as part of its broader Cold War agenda to contain Soviet expansion ($\checkmark$).Through a covert operation known as Operation Cyclone, the CIA provided billions of dollars in weapons, training, and logistical support to the Afghan Mujahideen. A key component of this support was the supply of advanced Stinger missiles, which enabled resistance fighters to shoot down Soviet helicopters and disrupt air dominance ($\checkmark$).

Secondly, Pakistan played a central role in facilitating the Mujahideen resistance. The country's intelligence agency, the ISI, acted as a conduit for American and Saudi aid, distributing funds and weapons to various Afghan factions ($\checkmark$). Pakistan also hosted extensive networks of refugee camps and guerrilla training centres along its border, which not only supported the war effort but also entrenched militant ideologies within Pakistan's territory. This involvement elevated Pakistan's strategic position during the Cold War, though it also laid the foundation for future instability in the region ($\checkmark$).

Finally, China supported the Afghan resistance as part of its geopolitical effort to counterbalance Soviet power in Central and South Asia ($\checkmark$). Despite being a communist state itself, China feared Soviet encroachment in neighbouring areas such as Xinjiang and sought to undermine Moscow's influence. It supplied the Mujahideen with light weapons, landmines, and ammunition, often through Pakistani channels. This indirect collaboration with the U.S. highlighted the complexity of Cold War alliances and reflected China's broader strategy to assert regional autonomy and challenge Soviet dominance ($\checkmark$).
