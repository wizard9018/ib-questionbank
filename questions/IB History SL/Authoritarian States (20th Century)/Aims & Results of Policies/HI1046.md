# HI1046

Evaluate the effectiveness of domestic economic policies in solving the problems faced by **two** authoritarian states, each from a different region.
[15 marks]

Guidance for responses
- Candidates should examine the domestic economic policies of at least two authoritarian states from different regions (e.g., Stalin, Mao, Hitler, Mussolini, Castro, etc.).
- Responses must identify the problems these regimes faced (e.g., economic backwardness, unemployment, dependence on foreign powers, inflation, agrarian underdevelopment) and analyse how far economic policies solved them.
- Strong responses will distinguish between aims, short-term successes, long-term results, and unintended consequences.
- Evaluation should consider both effectiveness in solving immediate problems and whether policies supported regime survival.
- A substantiated conclusion should directly answer “to what extent” economic policies were effective.

**Indicative Content:**

$\underline{\textrm{Key Problems Faced}}$
- Stalin (USSR): Backward, agrarian economy; fear of foreign invasion; need for rapid industrialisation.
- Mao (China): Desire to modernise and catch up with the USSR; rural underdevelopment; eliminate landlordism.
- Hitler (Germany): Mass unemployment from Great Depression; resentment of Versailles reparations; desire for autarky.
- Mussolini (Italy): Postwar economic instability, inflation, unemployment; goal of “corporatist state”; need for prestige.
- Castro (Cuba): Dependence on U.S. sugar exports; economic inequality; underdevelopment.

$\underline{\textrm{Policies and Their Effectiveness}}$

Stalin
- Five-Year Plans: Rapid industrialisation, major growth in heavy industry (steel, coal, oil). By late 1930s, USSR became a major industrial power.
- Collectivisation: Aimed to modernise agriculture and feed cities, but caused famine (1932-33) and millions of deaths; nevertheless, it secured state control over agriculture.
 -Overall: Effective in building industry and military strength, less so in improving living standards or agricultural efficiency.

Mao
- First Five-Year Plan (1953-57): Soviet-style industrialisation with some success in steel, coal, and electricity.
- Great Leap Forward (1958-62): Attempted to combine industrialisation with communes; disastrous famine caused 30-40 million deaths.
- Overall: Mixed; initial industrial gains undermined by catastrophic agricultural failure.

Hitler
- Public works (autobahns), rearmament, and conscription cut unemployment dramatically.
- Four-Year Plan (1936): Autarky partially achieved; successes in synthetic fuel/rubber but Germany remained dependent on imports.
- Overall: Effective in solving unemployment and preparing for war, but long-term sustainability is questionable.

Mussolini
- “Battle for Grain” boosted wheat production, reducing imports but harming other crops.
- Public works and corporatism stabilised the economy but failed to create real growth.
- “Battle for the Lira” (1926) overvalued currency, harming exports.
- Overall: Limited success; prestige more than economic transformation.

Castro
- Agrarian Reform (1959): Land redistribution aimed at reducing inequality, initially popular but reduced efficiency.
- Nationalisation of industries and reliance on Soviet aid stabilised the economy but increased dependence.
- Sugar harvest targets (1970 “Ten Million Ton Harvest”) failed.
- Overall: Some gains in literacy, healthcare, and social equality, but long-term economic weakness persisted.

$\underline{\textrm{Possible Arguments for Agreement (economic policies were effective)}}$
- Hitler: solved unemployment and restored national pride.
- Stalin: turned the USSR into an industrial power capable of defeating Nazi Germany.
- Mao (early years): some success with First Five-Year Plan.
- Castro: improved social equality and services despite weak growth.

$\underline{\textrm{Possible Arguments for Disagreement (economic policies were ineffective)}}$
- Stalin: collectivisation was disastrous for agriculture.
- Mao: The Great Leap Forward was a catastrophic failure.
- Mussolini: policies prioritised image over substance.
- Castro: dependence on the USSR left the economy vulnerable.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Authoritarian leaders often presented economic transformation as essential for consolidating power and proving their regimes' legitimacy ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). In Italy, Benito Mussolini aimed to stabilise the fragile post-First World War economy and build prestige for his Fascist regime. In China, Mao Zedong sought to modernise a backward agrarian society and construct a socialist economy ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Both leaders implemented ambitious policies framed in ideological or nationalist terms, but while Mussolini achieved little beyond propaganda victories, Mao combined limited industrial progress with disastrous agricultural failures ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

Agricultural policy was central to both regimes, but exposed their weaknesses ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Mussolini's Battle for Grain (1925) sought to make Italy self-sufficient by raising wheat production. Wheat yields rose, and imports declined, allowing Mussolini to boast of success ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Yet the policy distorted farming: land was shifted away from traditional crops like olives and citrus, damaging exports and worsening Italy's trade balance ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Mao initially implemented land reforms that redistributed land from landlords to peasants, undermining old elites and winning rural support ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$).However, this was followed by collectivisation, culminating in the establishment of People's Communes during the Great Leap Forward (1958-62). These aimed to boost efficiency but instead disrupted farming practices, reduced incentives, and contributed to a catastrophic famine that killed tens of millions ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). While Mussolini exaggerated modest gains for propaganda, Mao's agricultural policy was far more transformative and ultimately disastrous ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

Industrialisation was another key area where both regimes sought legitimacy ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Mussolini promised to modernise Italy through corporatism and public works, but results were limited. The “corporate state” largely functioned as a mechanism for state control rather than genuine collaboration between employers and workers, and industrial growth remained modest compared with other European states ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Public works projects such as draining the Pontine Marshes were more symbolic than economically transformative ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Mao, by contrast, pursued Soviet-style industrialisation with his First Five-Year Plan (1953-57), which achieved significant gains in coal, steel, and electricity output ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). This represented genuine progress in modernising China's economy. Yet his subsequent Great Leap Forward aimed to accelerate development through backyard steel furnaces and mass mobilisation. Steel production figures were inflated, quality was poor, and agricultural collapse undermined the entire policy ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). In industrialisation, Mussolini's efforts were shallow and prestige-driven, while Mao's were initially successful but collapsed under ideological excess ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

Propaganda and ideology deeply shaped both leaders' economic approaches ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$).  Mussolini framed his policies as “battles”: the Battle for Grain, the Battle for Land, the Battle for the Lira, which projected him as a decisive leader transforming Italy's fortunes ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Yet these campaigns were more about image than results. The Battle for the Lira (1926), which revalued the currency, created short-term prestige but damaged exports and slowed growth ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Mao's economic policies were also steeped in ideology: the Great Leap Forward reflected his belief in mass mobilisation and revolutionary willpower overcoming material constraints ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$).Propaganda, such as giant rallies, posters, and the cult of Mao as “Great Helmsman,” pushed unrealistic quotas and silenced dissenting voices ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). In both cases, propaganda served to mask economic failures, but while Mussolini's campaigns inflated minor achievements, Mao's propaganda helped drive catastrophic mismanagement ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In conclusion, the economic policies of Mussolini and Mao reveal both the ambitions and failures of authoritarian economic management ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$). Mussolini's “battles” were superficial and prestige-driven, leaving Italy economically weak and unprepared for war, contributing directly to the regime's collapse in 1943. Mao achieved limited industrial progress in the First Five-Year Plan, but the catastrophic famine of the Great Leap Forward exposed the dangers of ideology-driven economics ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). Neither leader solved their countries' fundamental problems, but authoritarian control allowed them to claim success and, in Mao's case, maintain political power despite devastating failure ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
