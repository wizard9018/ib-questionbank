# HI1244

To what extent did the social policies of 20th-century authoritarian states have a significant impact? Discuss with reference to **two** states, each chosen from a different region.

Possible suitable examples include:

**USSR - Lenin (1917-1924), Power Struggle Era (1924-1928), and Stalin (1918-1953)**

Women and Family
- Under Lenin, the Communist Party attempted to liberate women from domestic and patriarchal structures. The 1917 Decree on Civil Marriage, Children and Registry Books made divorce easier to obtain, while abortion was legalised in 1920. The Zhenotdel worked to promote literacy, workplace participation, and social equality for women. However, these reforms remained limited in practice, especially in rural areas.
- During the Power Struggle era, early Bolshevik gender reforms remained in place. State provision of childcare, canteens, and social support expanded slowly, and this period represented the last phase of genuine Soviet gender liberalism before later Stalinist reversals. However, funding remained inconsistent and many women continued to shoulder traditional domestic responsibilities.
- Under Stalin, social policy shifted sharply. The 1936 Family Law made divorce more difficult and abortion illegal (except for medical necessity). Traditional family roles were promoted, and pro-natalist measures, including maternal awards such as the “Mother Heroine”, encouraged large families. As a result, earlier gains in female emancipation were substantially rolled back.

Education
- Under Lenin, spreading literacy was a central priority. The 1919 Decree on the Liquidation of Illiteracy initiated mass literacy campaigns, and free schooling expanded dramatically. Adult programmes (likbez) educated millions, laying the foundation for a more educated workforce.
- During the Power Struggle era, literacy campaigns continued, and unified labour schools expanded across the country. Polytechnic education was encouraged, and the state began to standardise curricula. These developments prepared the ground for later centralised educational structures.
- Under Stalin, education became more formalised and disciplined. Ten years of compulsory schooling were introduced; curricula emphasised science, engineering, and Russian history. However, fees were imposed on upper secondary and university education from 1940, limiting access for poorer students and contributing to the formation of a privileged elite.

Religion
- Under Lenin, the Communist Party promoted state atheism and sought to undermine the Russian Orthodox Church. The 1918 Decree on the Separation of Church and State stripped the Church of privileges, and church property was confiscated during the Civil War. Religious persecution intensified during famine relief campaigns, although suppression was uneven across regions.
- During the Power Struggle era, anti-religious activity increased. The League of Militant Atheists, founded in 1925, expanded atheist propaganda and supported the continued closure of churches. Urban religious institutions were more easily dismantled, but rural areas often resisted these measures.
- Under Stalin, the campaign against religion deepened. The destruction of rural churches frequently provoked unrest, while urban churches were closed with little resistance. Only during the Second World War were restrictions relaxed slightly, allowing some churches to reopen for morale. Overall, religious life was severely restricted.

Language / Culture / Nationality Policy
- Under Lenin, the government introduced korenizatsiya (“indigenisation”) to encourage minority languages and cultures. Minority-language schools, theatres, and publications expanded, and local elites were promoted to reduce ethnic resistance.
- During the Power Struggle era, korenizatsiya reached its peak. Minority-language education continued, and cultural institutions flourished. Although tensions emerged between centralising pressures and local autonomy, no mass deportations occurred, and cultural diversity remained relatively protected.
- Under Stalin, these policies were reversed. Russian culture and language were promoted as dominant, and Russian became mandatory in non-Russian schools. Entire minority groups, such as Chechens, Crimean Tatars, and Volga Germans, were deported from their homes during the war. Despite these pressures, minority identities persisted, especially in rural areas.

**China - Mao (1949-1976):**
- Women and family: To counter the Chinese male-dominated society, the Communist Party of China (CCP) advocated for female equality. In 1950, Mao introduced the New Marriage Law, which banned arranged marriages and entitled women to divorce. However, in the Muslim western provinces, female members were still subject to the orders of husbands, fathers and brothers.
- Religion: The CCP sought to replace religious worship with loyalty to the Communist Party and the state. Buddhism, Confucianism, and major foreign religions were forbidden from being openly practised. However, in 1976, the Muslim proportion still formed a large minority of the Xinjiang population.
- Health: The CCP promoted modern medicine and disease prevention in rural areas. The Rural Co-operative Medical Scheme was established, which increased the number of “barefoot doctors” and built township health centers and county hospitals. Mao wanted a basic literacy standard for peasants and workers.
- Education: Basic education was provided to all peasant families up to the primary level, with a heavy emphasis on engineering and technical training to support industrialisation. However, Mao did not allow higher education institutions to flourish.

**Germany - Hitler (1933-1945):**
- Women and family: The Nazis were determined to boost the birth rate, so they wanted women to embrace motherhood. Fewer women were allocated university places, and fewer professions offered positions for women. However, in 1942, the pressure of war obliged the Nazis to demand that women go into the factories. The Nazis wanted to get rid of the mentally and physically disabled. In 1939, they initiated the programme of $\lq$mercy killing'. Children with severe disabilities were murdered by starvation or lethal injections. However, public rumours and Catholic opposition led Hitler to halt the programme in 1941.
- Religion: Hitler wanted to replace Christianity with a faith reflecting Nazi values. He united all the Protestant Churches together into one official Reich Church under a pro-Nazi Reich Bishop. However, many Protestant pastors formed an opposition group, the Confessional Church.
- Culture: Hitler promoted the concept of *Volksgemeinschaft*, a new collective national identity. The concept of $\lq$Blood and Soil' portrayed the peasantry as the nation's most racially pure element and as the symbol of traditional German values. However, it failed to seduce the working class from their established identity.

**Tanzania - Nyerere (1961-1985):**
- Health: Nyerere aimed to make health a basic right for all Tanzanians. He invested public money in health programmes that trained doctors, built health centres, and vaccinated children against various diseases.
- Education: Nyerere's education policy aimed to increase the literacy rate. He significantly expanded school provision for children, established teacher training colleges, and founded the Institute of Curriculum Development. However, the problem of overly large class sizes in schools persisted.
- Women and family: Nyerere strongly advocated for women's full equality. He took a series of legal initiatives that required women to receive equal pay, outlawed discrimination against women in employment, and granted women equal rights to stand for parliament. However, traditional male dominance was still ingrained in Tanzanian society.
- Culture/community: Nyerere promoted *Ujamaa*, a co-operative, communalist approach to the development of Tanzania. The programme of “villagization,” which grouped peasants into collective farms, led to a significant drop in agricultural production. However, the programme provided free, compulsory education in every village.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
Communist social policies in the Soviet Union under Lenin and Stalin and in China under Mao had mixed and uneven impacts ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). While Lenin's reforms significantly transformed society, particularly in advancing women's rights and literacy, Stalin's policies reversed or limited many of these gains ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Mao's reforms in China similarly produced partial change, as progressive laws often failed to eliminate traditional hierarchies or create full equality in practice ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Overall, while Communist social policies under these authoritarian regimes aimed at profound transformation, their actual impact was often mixed, producing both significant change and notable continuity ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In the Soviet Union under Lenin, social policies had a significant impact. One element of these policies was Lenin's attempt to liberate women from their traditional roles of homemaking and child-rearing ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). The Decree on Divorce, issued in 1917, made divorce easier, allowing marriages to be dissolved at the request of either or both spouses ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). In addition, the Decree on Women's Healthcare, adopted in 1920, legalised abortion, permitting them to be done freely and without charge in a state hospital ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). These measures were important in allowing female emancipation from family and motherhood ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). However, Stalin began to stress the value of the family and changed the earlier Bolshevik social policy ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). The 1936 Divorce Decree made divorce more difficult by requiring both spouses to appear to register a divorce and increasing divorce costs ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). The 1936 Decree on the Prohibition of Abortions made abortion illegal ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$) unless the pregnancy threatened the woman's life or health ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). The strictures of freedom under Stalin and the emphasis on family allowed little room for female liberation despite previous progress under Lenin ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

Lenin wanted to improve educational outcomes by spreading literacy among a largely illiterate population in the Soviet Union ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). In 1919, he signed the decree “On elimination of illiteracy among the population of Soviet Russia” ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). This decree launched a mass literacy campaign and expanded access to schooling, while millions of illiterate adults were enrolled in special literacy schools across the country ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Formal education was made a priority in the Soviet Union, and these foundations contributed to a major rise in literacy, from 23 per cent in 1914 to 75 per cent in 1937 ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Under Stalin, schooling became more systematised, with the state gradually moving toward a ten-year compulsory programme ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). However, in 1940, fees were charged for the last three years (ages fifteen to eighteen) of non-compulsory secondary schooling and university education ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). The payment of fees challenged the notion of an egalitarian education system since an elite of privileged people developed for those able to pay for extra education ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). University graduates had access to the key areas of Soviet administration, so this limitation also prevented many poorer citizens from accessing positions of power and influence within the new Soviet system ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

Mao also attempted to change women's roles in Chinese society through policymaking. To counter women's subordinate role in Chinese society, the Chinese Communists advocated for female equality ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). In 1950, the New Marriage Law was introduced, ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$) which forbade concubinage, discontinued arranged marriages and entitled women to divorce their partners ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). The role of women improved as many women used their new freedom to divorce and remarry ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). However, female subordination was the norm in the Muslim western provinces ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$), where female members were subject to the orders of husbands, fathers and brothers ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). The new marriage laws were unable to end traditional notions of female inferiority ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). In addition, Mao made a priority of promoting women within the Communist Party ranks ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). However, by the 1959 National People's Congress, women still made up only around 23 per cent of delegates, and it remained rare for women to be appointed to senior government or Party positions ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Despite the Communist Party's public advocacy for women's rights, few women were promoted within the Communist Party and change to traditional roles was slow and limited ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

Mao's policy toward religion had a substantial impact on Chinese society, as the Chinese Communist Party sought to replace religious worship with loyalty to the Communist Party and the state ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). During a major anti-religious campaign that began in 1950 ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$), Buddhism, Confucianism, Christianity and Islam were forbidden to be openly practiced. Priests, monks and nuns were prohibited from wearing their distinctive dress, churches and mosques were closed and their property seized, and foreign clergy were expelled from China ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). These measures effectively outlawed formal expressions of belief and aimed to weaken organisations that might challenge Party authority ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$). However, religious life did not disappear entirely. In 1950, patriotic churches, led by Chinese Protestants, emerged in China ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Such churches were only tolerated if clergy openly supported the regime and accepted state control over appointments and worship practices ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). The fact that some churches were permitted to remain open, provided they posed no threat to state security, shows that the CCP's success in transforming religious behaviour was uneven and depended on negotiated cooperation rather than total eradication ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

On the one hand, social policies in the Soviet Union under Lenin/Stalin and China under Mao did little to change the lives of women ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$). In the Soviet Union, the restrictions on the right to divorce and abortion allowed little room for female independence, and in China, obedience of wives to their husbands continued in all rural areas, while few women were promoted within the ranks of the Communist Party ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). However, this is not to say that the social policies in the two countries were not important ; rather, they reveal the tension between revolutionary ideals and the limited, often uneven, social change that authoritarian regimes were willing, or able, to achieve ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
