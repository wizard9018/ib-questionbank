# HI0746

$\text{\textquotedblleft}$Weak political systems were the most significant factor in the emergence of authoritarian states.” Discuss with reference to **two** states. 

[15 marks]

Candidates must demonstrate a clear understanding of the question's requirements. In this case, candidates should offer a considered and balanced review of the statement that weak political systems were the most significant factor in the emergence of authoritarian states.

A high-scoring response should:

 - Clearly address the role of weak political systems and consider the ways in which they affected the conditions in which authoritarian states emerged.

 - Identify other factors important in the emergence of the authoritarian states chosen.

 - Offer a considered and balanced review of the hypothesis that economic factors were the most significant determinant of the conditions in which authoritarian states emerged.

 - Use a well-structured argument with relevant historical examples and differing perspectives integrated into the answer.

Possible suitable examples include:

 - **Germany** - Adolf Hitler (1933-1945): The Weimar Republic was a fragile democracy established after WWI, plagued by hyperinflation, political extremism, proportional representation that led to unstable coalition governments, and emergency powers under Article 48. Hitler exploited public disillusionment with democratic inefficiency, especially during the Great Depression, and used the Reichstag Fire and the Enabling Act to dismantle democratic structures. Other factors included Nationalist resentment over the Treaty of Versailles, economic crisises and Nazi propaganda..

 - **China** - Mao Zedong (1949-1976): After the fall of the Qing dynasty, China's Republican government under the KMT struggled with warlords, corruption, and failure to implement effective national governance, especially in rural areas. Mao and the CCP capitalized on the KMT's inability to unify China or address peasant concerns, offering land reform, anti-Japanese resistance, and ideological alternatives.  Other factors included Japanese invasion and CCP grassroots mobilization helped the Communists gain legitimacy and support.

 - **Italy** - Benito Mussolini (1922-1943): Post-WWI Italy had a liberal parliamentary monarchy that failed to deliver economic recovery, territorial gains (the “mutilated victory”), or political stability, resulting in frequent government changes. Mussolini exploited public anger and fear of socialism by forming paramilitary Blackshirts and securing support from conservative elites and the king, who appointed him Prime Minister. Other factors included Nationalist sentiment, elite support, and Mussolini's charisma and propaganda skills.

 - **Russia/USSR** - Lenin (1917-1924): The Provisional Government was weak, politically divided, and failed to address key issues such as ending Russia's involvement in World War I, land reforms, and economic hardship, which led to widespread dissatisfaction. Lenin and the Bolshevik Party capitalized on the Provisional Government's failures by promising "peace, land, and bread." Lenin led the October Revolution (1917), which successfully overthrew the Provisional Government and established Bolshevik rule. Other factors included Lenin's skillful use of propaganda, control over the Soviet press, and his leadership in the October Revolution enabled him to rally support, particularly from soldiers and workers. 

 - **Spain** - Francisco Franco (193-1975): The Second Spanish Republic (1931–1939) was deeply divided between left and right, leading to social unrest, political assassinations, and economic instability. Franco led a military rebellion that capitalized on the Republic's weaknesses, eventually winning the Civil War and establishing a military dictatorship. Other factors included support from Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy, as well as disunity amongst the republicans.

 - **Argentina** - Juan Peron (1946-1955, 1973-1974): Argentina's democratic institutions were undermined by political instability, military interference, and economic inequality during the 1930s and 1940s. Peron used his military background and popular support among workers to gain power, presenting himself as a stabilizing, nationalist force. Other factors include his populist appeal and ability to use mass media and labour unions.

 - **Cuba** - Fidel Castro (1959-2008): Batista's Cuba was a U.S. backed authoritarian regime plagued by corruption, repression, and inequality, with growing unpopularity. Castro used guerrilla warfare, populist rhetoric, and anti-imperialist sentiment to rally support and overthrow Batista. Other factors included widespread public dissatisfaction, U.S. support for Batista, and Castro's charismatic leadership.

 - **Egypt** - Gamal Abdel Nasser (1954-1970): Egypt under King Farouk was a nominal constitutional monarchy, but it was plagued by corruption, inefficiency, British influence, elite dominance, electoral manipulation, and a lack of popular trust in its institutions. Nasser and the Free Officers Movement capitalized on dissatisfaction with Farouk's rule, particularly after Egypt's defeat in the 1948 Arab-Israeli War, using the monarchy's failure to address key issues to justify the 1952 coup. Other factors include anti-imperialist sentiment, rising Arab nationalism, and Nasser's charisma and control over the media and military.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:

\

In both Germany under Adolf Hitler and China under Mao Zedong, weak political systems played a central role in the emergence of the authoritarian regimes. ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$)

 In Germany, the Weimar Republic's structural instability, constitutional flaws, and inability to manage economic crises enabled Hitler's rise. ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$) In China, the Kuomintang's (KMT) failure to establish effective national governance and address rural issues created space for the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) to gain support and seize power. ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$)  This essay will first examine how the weaknesses of the Weimar system facilitated the Nazi seizure of power. It will then assess the KMT's ineffective political leadership and how it contributed to the CCP's rise. Finally, it will acknowledge other contributing factors while reinforcing that political weakness was the most significant cause in both cases. ($\checkmark\, \text{organization}$) 

In Germany, the weakness of the Weimar Republic was a fundamental factor in Hitler's rise to power. ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$) Established in 1919 after Germany's defeat in World War I, the Weimar system was created in crisis and remained fragile throughout its existence. ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$) It relied on proportional representation, which led to unstable coalition governments and frequent changes in leadership, over a dozen different governments between 1919 and 1933, each struggling to maintain political cohesion. ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$) This instability made it difficult to pass decisive legislation or confront growing threats such as extremism. ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$) Moreover, Article 48 of the Weimar Constitution, which allowed the president to rule by decree in times of emergency, was increasingly abused. ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$)  By the early 1930s, Germany was essentially governed by presidential decree, paving the way for authoritarianism. ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$) 

Economic crises further exposed the fragility of Weimar democracy and increased the public's desire for strong leadership. ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$)  The Great Depression hit Germany especially hard, with unemployment peaking at over six million in 1932. ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$)  The Weimar government appeared powerless to stop the economic collapse, deepening popular disillusionment. ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$)  Hitler and the Nazi Party capitalized on this discontent by promising national rejuvenation, economic recovery, and a return to traditional values. ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$) Their rise was not solely due to their message, but also due to the political vacuum following the fall of Muller's Great Coalition in 1930, which was followed by a period where Chancellors increasingly relied on Article 48, rather than consulting parliament. ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$) Hitler's appointment as Chancellor in 1933, followed by the Enabling Act, which gave him dictatorial powers, was the culmination of the Weimar system's failures. ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$) Without these political weaknesses, the Nazis would likely have faced much stronger resistance in their path to power. ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$) 

The rise of Mao Zedong and the CCP was also facilitated by a weak political system under the KMT. ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$) After the fall of the Qing dynasty in 1911, China entered a prolonged period of instability marked by warlordism, civil war, and foreign invasion. ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$)  The Republic of China under the KMT, particularly after Chiang Kai-shek took control in the late 1920s, was never able to unify the country or establish effective governance. ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$) While the Nationalist government made some progress in modernizing urban areas, it remained corrupt, authoritarian, and disconnected from the rural majority. ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$)  This inability to create a stable and inclusive political system left large parts of the population alienated and open to alternative forms of leadership. ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$) 

The KMT's inability to address land reform, rural poverty, and Japanese aggression eroded its legitimacy and contributed to the CCP's rise. ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$)  During the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937-1945), the KMT focused on defending cities and preserving military strength, often at the expense of the rural population. ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$)  In contrast, the CCP used guerrilla tactics to resist the Japanese in occupied territories and implemented land reforms to win over the peasantry. ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$) The failure of the KMT to mobilize mass support and govern effectively during this critical period revealed the weakness of its political structure. ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$) This enabled the CCP to portray itself as the true defender of China and laid the foundation for its eventual victory in the Chinese Civil War (1946-49). ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$) The collapse of the KMT regime was not solely a result of Communist strength, but also a reflection of its own systemic weaknesses. ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$) 

However, while weak political systems were central in both Germany and China, other factors also played important roles. ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$) In Germany, nationalist resentment over the Treaty of Versailles, widespread anti-communist sentiment, and the Nazi Party's skillful propaganda machinery contributed to Hitler's appeal. ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$)  Similarly, in China, the CCP's ideological flexibility, grassroots mobilization, and military strategy were critical to their success. ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$)  Nevertheless, these factors became effective only because the existing political systems failed to provide stability, legitimacy, and effective government. ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$) 

In conclusion, weak political systems were the most significant factor in the emergence of authoritarian states in both Germany and China. ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$)  The inability of the Weimar Republic and the KMT regime to manage political, economic, and social crises created opportunities for authoritarian leaders to seize power. (✓ analysis) While other factors contributed to their success, they were secondary to the collapse or failure of existing political structures. ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$)
