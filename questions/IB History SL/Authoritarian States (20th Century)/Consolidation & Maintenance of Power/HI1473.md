# HI1473

Compare and contrast the nature and treatment of opposition in **two** authoritarian states.
[15 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:
\
**General Guidance:**
\
Strong answers should clearly address the question by comparing and contrasting both the nature of opposition and the ways in which opposition was treated in two authoritarian states. Answers that rely on lengthy description of opposition are unlikely to score well. The strongest answers will maintain a running comparison of the two states and support arguments with specific, relevant evidence. Conclusions will vary depending upon the states chosen, but all should arrive at a balanced, well-supported conclusion that evaluates the extent to which the nature and treatment of opposition were similar or different between the two authoritarian states.

**Possible suitable examples include:**
- **Nazi Germany under Hitler (1933-1945)**
    - Opposition in Nazi Germany came from a range of political, religious, social and military groups, including Communists, Social Democrats, trade unionists, religious leaders, youth organisations and some members of the armed forces. While these groups opposed the regime for different reasons, they were united in their rejection of aspects of Nazi rule.
    - Hitler responded by eliminating political opposition through legal measures, censorship, propaganda, surveillance and the use of the Gestapo and concentration camps. As the regime became more radical, particularly during the Second World War, repression intensified and many opponents faced imprisonment or execution.
- **Mao's Communist China (1949-1976)**
    - Opposition in Mao's China came from a range of political, social and ideological groups, including Nationalist supporters, landlords, intellectuals, religious groups, party critics and individuals accused of being $\text{\textquotedblleft}$counter-revolutionaries." Opposition was often linked to resistance to Communist policies or criticism of Mao's leadership, although perceived opposition could also emerge from within the Communist Party itself.
    - Mao responded through political campaigns, including land reform, the Anti-Rightist Movement and the Cultural Revolution, using public denunciations, imprisonment, forced labour, purges and widespread political persecution to eliminate real and perceived opponents. These campaigns often encouraged ordinary citizens to participate in identifying and exposing enemies of the revolution.
- **Mussolini's Fascist Italy (1922-1943)**
    - Opposition in Fascist Italy came from a variety of political and social groups, including socialists, communists, liberal politicians, trade unionists, Catholic critics and anti-fascist intellectuals. Much of the early opposition challenged Mussolini's destruction of parliamentary democracy and the growing influence of Fascism, although organised resistance declined as the regime consolidated power.
    - Mussolini responded through a combination of intimidation, legal measures and political control. Fascist squads (Blackshirts) used violence against opponents, while censorship, the banning of political parties, the OVRA secret police, imprisonment, exile and propaganda were used to suppress dissent and strengthen the Fascist state.
- **Castro's Cuba (1959-2008)**
    - Opposition in Castro's Cuba came from a range of political, social and economic groups, including supporters of the Batista regime, political opponents, wealthy elites affected by revolutionary reforms, religious critics, dissidents and pro-democracy activists. Many opponents challenged the establishment of a one-party socialist state, while others opposed restrictions on political freedoms, economic reforms or the increasing influence of the Communist Party.
    - Castro responded by consolidating one-party rule and restricting political opposition through censorship, state control of the media, surveillance by security organisations, imprisonment, exile and limitations on political organisation. These measures were intended to prevent organised resistance and reinforce the authority of the revolutionary government.
\
[15 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:
\
All authoritarian states suppress opposition, but the nature of that opposition and the methods used to eliminate it reflect each regime's ideology and circumstances ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). In Nazi Germany under Adolf Hitler, opposition largely meant identifiable political, religious and racial enemies, whereas in Mao Zedong's China it was defined ideologically, extending to anyone deemed insufficiently revolutionary ($\checkmark\, \text{examples}$). Consequently, Hitler built a centralised system of persecution, while Mao relied on mass political campaigns to identify enemies ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$).

The nature of opposition differed in each state ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). In Nazi Germany, early opposition came from the Communist Party (KPD), the Social Democratic Party (SPD) and trade unions, which opposed the destruction of democracy after 1933. Religious figures, including members of the Confessing Church and Bishop Clemens von Galen, criticised aspects of Nazi policy, and student groups such as the White Rose opposed the regime during the war. Some conservative military officers also turned against Hitler, culminating in the failed July Bomb Plot of 1944 ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Though differing in motivation, these groups were systematically dismantled by the regime, preventing a united resistance movement ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In contrast, opposition in Mao's China was often determined by ideological loyalty rather than organised political activity ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Former Kuomintang supporters, landlords and wealthy peasants were identified as $\text{\textquotedblleft}$class enemies" following the Communist victory in 1949, while many businessmen and industrialists were targeted during the Three-Anti Campaign (1951) and Five-Anti Campaign (1952). Intellectuals who criticised government policies during the Hundred Flowers Campaign became victims of the Anti-Rightist Campaign, while the Cultural Revolution expanded the definition of opposition to include teachers, writers, party officials and anyone accused of promoting "bourgeois" ideas ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$).Unlike Nazi Germany, where opposition generally came from identifiable political or religious groups, Mao's campaigns meant that almost anyone could be labelled a counter-revolutionary, creating widespread fear throughout society ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$) ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

Both regimes relied on repression to eliminate opposition, but the methods they employed reflected their different political systems ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). In Nazi Germany, Hitler rapidly dismantled democracy following the Reichstag Fire in February 1933. The Reichstag Fire Decree suspended civil liberties, while the Enabling Act allowed Hitler to legislate without parliamentary approval. By July 1933, political parties were banned, trade unions dissolved and Germany a one-party state. The Gestapo and SS, under Heinrich Himmler, monitored opponents through surveillance, informants and arbitrary arrest. Early concentration camps such as Dachau (March 1933) initially housed political prisoners, notably communists and trade unionists. Censorship and propaganda directed by Joseph Goebbels promoted ideological conformity and discouraged dissent ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$).

Mao also used state repression but frequently combined it with mass political mobilisation ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Land Reform campaigns targeted landlords through public $\text{\textquotedblleft}$struggle sessions", while the Anti-Rightist Campaign punished critical intellectuals. During the Cultural Revolution, Mao encouraged the Red Guards to attack perceived enemies of the revolution, leading to public humiliation, imprisonment and forced labour for millions. Unlike Hitler's reliance on institutions such as the Gestapo, Mao deliberately encouraged ordinary citizens to denounce neighbours, colleagues and even family, making repression a mass social movement as well as a state policy ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$).

Despite these differences, both regimes shared important similarities. Hitler and Mao portrayed opponents as enemies of the nation or the revolution and justified repression as essential for protecting the state ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Both relied on propaganda, censorship and surveillance to discourage dissent and eliminate alternative political organisations ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). In each case, repression was not simply defensive but formed part of a broader effort to reshape society according to the regime's ideological goals and strengthen the authority of the leader ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

The scale and purpose of repression, however, differed significantly ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). In Nazi Germany, repression was closely linked to the creation of a Volksgemeinschaft based on Nazi racial ideology. Political opponents were imprisoned or executed, but persecution increasingly extended to groups considered racially or biologically inferior, particularly Jews, Roma and people with disabilities. During the Second World War, repression intensified as the regime sought to eliminate both political and racial enemies ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Thus, opposition was suppressed not only to secure Hitler's political authority but also to facilitate the racial transformation of German society ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In contrast, Mao's repression aimed to maintain continuous revolutionary change rather than racial purification ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). Mao believed that class struggle continued even after the Communist victory in 1949 and that enemies could emerge within both society and the Communist Party. Consequently, campaigns such as the Anti-Rightist Movement and the Cultural Revolution repeatedly identified new $\text{\textquotedblleft}$counter-revolutionaries" and $\text{\textquotedblleft}$capitalist roaders", resulting in millions being persecuted, imprisoned or sent to labour camps. Unlike Hitler, who had largely destroyed organised political opposition by the mid-1930s, Mao repeatedly revived campaigns against perceived enemies to reinforce his personal authority and revolutionary ideology ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Consequently, repression in Mao's China was cyclical and ideological, whereas in Nazi Germany it became increasingly focused on enforcing political obedience and implementing racial policy ($\checkmark\, \text{perspectives}$) ($\checkmark\, \text{analysis}$).

In conclusion, the nature of opposition differed: Hitler faced identifiable political and racial enemies, while Mao defined opposition through ideological loyalty ($\checkmark\, \text{focus}$). So did its treatment: Hitler relied on centralised institutions such as the Gestapo and concentration camps, Mao on repeated mass campaigns ($\checkmark\, \text{knowledge}$). Both consolidated power through repression, but their contrasting ideological objectives shaped whom each regime persecuted and how ($\checkmark\, \text{conclusion}$).
