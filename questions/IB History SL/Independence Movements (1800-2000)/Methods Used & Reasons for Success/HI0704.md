# HI0704

Explain **two** reasons for growing religious divisions within the Nationalist movement.

[4 marks]

---

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:

\

Any **two** of the following with supporting explanation:

 - The nationalists' protests following the Partition of Bengal in 1905 unnerved Muslims in Bengal due to the appeals to Hindu nationalism made by some protestors. Representatives of the Muslim community met the Viceroy in 1906 and the Simla Deputation and stressed the view that the Muslims as a distinct community needed separate representation.

 - In December 1906, the Muslim League was formed with only Muslim membership, as distinct from the Congress, which included people of all religions, including Muslims. The aim of the Muslim League was to secure constitutional protection for Muslims with separate electorates and reserved seats. This was dramatically opposed to the Congress's stand of all-Indian representation in legislatures. 

 - The Morley-Minto Reforms of 1909 provided for Indian representation in central and provincial legislative councils and introduced separate electorates for Muslims and reserved seats. This introduced the concept of religious divisions in the polity of Indian nationalism and consolidated the idea of religious divisions. 

 - The 1927 rejection by the Congress of the offer made by the Muslim League to end its support for separate electorates in exchange for an agreement that Muslims could fill one-third of the seats on the Central Legislative Council furthered religious divisions. This stand by the Congress went against their earlier agreement with the Muslim League in the Lucknow Pact of 1916, where the Congress had agreed that Muslims would have a fixed proportion of seats in the future Indian parliament and extra seats in areas where Muslims were in a minority. This caused anger and distrust from the Muslim League. 

 - In 1930, the first proposal for a Muslim $\text{\textquotedblleft}$homeland" that would be part of United India was made by Urdu poet and philosopher Muhammad Iqbal at a session of the Muslim League. In 1933, Chaudhri Rahmat Ali, an Indian student in Cambridge, put forward a plan for a federation of ten Muslim states called Pakistan. These actions by the Muslim League furthered the Muslim League's program to establish a separate identity unlike other parties like the Congress or the Communist Party that included all religions, including Hindus and Muslims. 

 - The Muslim League fared badly in the 1937 provincial elections, with the Congress winning 70$\%$of the popular vote. The Muslim League won barely 5% of the Muslim vote.  The Muslim League leader Jinnah proposed that the League could form part of coalition governments in provinces with a large Muslim minority. Congress refused Jinnah's offer. While the Congress did appoint its own Muslim members to provincial governments, the rejection of Jinnah's proposal had angered the League. Jinnah now aware of the lack of support from most Muslims worked to increase Muslim support for the League using the slogan, $\text{\textquotedblleft}$Islam is in danger".

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:

\

The Partition of Bengal in 1905 witnessed the initial start of growing religious divisions that impacted the Nationalist movement and led to the eventual partition of India in 1947 ($\checkmark$). The appeals to Hindu nationalism by a section of nationalists protesting the partition unnerved Muslims in Bengal. Representatives of the Muslim community met the Viceroy in 1906, and this Simla Deputation put forward the view in the meeting that the Muslims as a distinct community needed separate representation. In December, the Muslim League was formed in Dhaka with an aim to secure constitutional protection for Muslims with separate electorates and reserved seats ($\checkmark$). 

Religious divisions within the Nationalist movement were further exacerbated when, in 1927, the Congress, as a result of pressure by Hindu nationalists, rejected the Muslim League's offer to end its support for separate electorates in exchange for an agreement that Muslims could fill one-third of the seats on the Central Legislative Council ($\checkmark$). This increased tension between the two groups as it went against the earlier Lucknow Pact of 1916 between the two parties, in which the Congress had agreed that Muslims would have a fixed proportion of seats in the future Indian parliament and extra seats in areas where Muslims were in a minority. This widened the gulf between the two parties and ensured the retention of separate electorates ($\checkmark$).
