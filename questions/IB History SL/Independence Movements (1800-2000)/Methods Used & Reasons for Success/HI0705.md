# HI0705

$\text{\textquotedblleft}$Violent methods were essential to achieving independence between 1800-2000.” Discuss with reference to **two** states, each chosen from a different region.

A high-scoring response should: 

 - Clearly address the **methods** used to achieve independence in each state. 

 - Analyse the **impact** and **effectiveness** of **violent methods** in the achievement of independence in relation to the **impact** and **effectiveness** of **other methods** that led to independence in each state.

Possible suitable examples include: 

 - **Ben Bella and Algeria**:

     - The struggle for Algerian independence from French colonial rule was violent and traumatic. Algeria was considered an integral part of France and the settler population ($\text{\textquotedblleft}$pied-noirs") enjoyed full citizenship rights, while the indigenous Muslim majority was heavily discriminated against.Political reforms and non-violent nationalist efforts had limited success before 1954.FLN (National Liberation Front) launched the Algerian War of Independence in 1954.Violence became a tool to both confront French authority and unify Algerians under the FLN's and Ben Bella's leadership.While violence was central to the final achievement of independence,diplomatic efforts, including negotiations leading to the Evian Accords, were ultimately needed to finalize independence. 

 - **José Martí and Cuba**: 

     - The struggle for Cuban independence from Spanish colonial rule in the late 19th century involved a complex mix of armed resistance, political activism, and foreign intervention. Violent methods were central to the Cuban independence movement, and Martí, though primarily a thinker and organizer, saw armed struggle as a necessary path to liberation. The Cuban War of Independence (1895-1898) that  Martí helped plan relied on widespread and sustained violence to challenge Spanish control. However, final independence came only after U.S. intervention, suggesting that while violence was essential to initiating and sustaining the fight, it was not sufficient alone to secure complete sovereignty. In Martí's case, violence was a necessary means, but not the only factor shaping Cuba's path to independence.

 - **Ho Chi Minh and Vietnam**: 

     - The Vietnamese struggle for independence in the 20th century was prolonged and violent.Ho Chi Minh recognized that colonial powers would not concede power through peaceful negotiation and committed to a long-term armed struggle. From the defeat of the French in 1954 to the eventual withdrawal of the Americans in 1975, violent resistance, through both conventional and guerrilla warfare, was indispensable. While diplomacy as in the Geneva Conference, ideology that helped unify different groups, and political strategy played key roles, it was ultimately the military success of Vietnamese forces that secured full independence and national unification.

 - **Kolokotronis and Greece**: 

     - The Greek War of Independence (1821-1829) marked the end of four centuries of Ottoman rule, with the creation of an independent Greek state. Violent methods were essential in initiating and sustaining the fight for independence. The military leadership of Theodoros Kolokotronis and the victories he achieved on the battlefield were critical in undermining Ottoman control and asserting Greek nationalist claims. While diplomatic and international support from Britain, France, and Russia helped finalize independence with the Treaty of Constantinople (1832), it was the armed struggle that made this support possible. Therefore, violence was not just present, but indispensable to Greece's independence.

 - **Nehru, Gandhi and India**: 

     - India's independence movement under the leadership of Mohandas Gandhi and Jawaharlal Nehru was based on a movement rooted in non-violence; the constant threat of violence did play a critical supporting role and helped pressure the British, especially in the final years. The threat of unrest, mutiny, and revolutionary violence contributed to Britain's decision to leave,together with the rise of anti-colonialism and the economic exhaustion of Britain, following the Second World War. While violence was not the main method, it was a significant factor in making non-violent resistance more effective and credible. 

 - **Collins, de Valera and Ireland**: 

     - Ireland's struggle for independence from British rule in the early 20th century involved both political negotiation and violent resistance. Éamon de Valera and Michael Collins played different roles in this struggle. While de Valera provided important political leadership and helped articulate the Republican vision, it was Collins' violent guerrilla campaign that broke British control and forced negotiation. The Easter Rising sparked nationalist sentiment, but it was sustained violence during the War of Independence that made continued British rule unfeasible. While politics legitimized the movement, violence was the critical instrument that brought independence.

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:

\

India and Ireland, both achieved independence from British colonial rule through a combination of political negotiation, mass mobilisation, and armed resistance ($\checkmark$ examples). While India's independence is widely associated with the non-violent philosophy of Gandhi and the political leadership of  Nehru, Ireland's struggle was marked by armed resistance, especially under the direction of Collins and the Irish Republican Army (IRA). In Ireland, violent methods were crucial in achieving independence, while in India, independence was achieved primarily through non-violent mass resistance, though the threat of violence played an accelerating role ($\checkmark$ focus). 

The use of violence played a role in both independence movements. Ireland's independence struggle highlights the critical role of violent methods in undermining colonial rule, while in India, the latter years of the Indian independence movement also saw pockets of violent resistance that influenced the British decision to grant India independence ($\checkmark$ focus). Violence erupted in Ireland following the British introduction of the Home Rule Bill in 1912, which the Irish Nationalists felt fell far short of complete Irish independence. Armed insurrection became a central strategy for Irish nationalists with the Easter Rising of 1916 and the proclamation of the Irish Republic ($\checkmark$ examples). The British response with the use of force and execution of the leaders consolidated public demand for full independence and laid the foundation for future armed campaigns. Clashes between the IRA and the British forces continued during the Irish War of Independence (1919-1921), with the use of violent methods by Collins and the Irish Republican Army (IRA). This included targeted assassinations and ambushes ($\checkmark$ knowledge). The British deployment of the Black and Tans and the Auxiliary Division to suppress the violence and unrest was marked by brutality and further alienated the Irish population and increased support for the Republicans ($\checkmark$ analysis). 

In India, violent resistance also impacted the British decision to grant India independence ($\checkmark$ focus). Revolutionary groups carried out political assassinations and bombings. Subhas Chandra Bose and the Indian National Army (INA) launched campaigns that, despite being militarily unsuccessful, put pressure on the British with the post-war INA trials (1945-46) ($\checkmark$ examples). The rising tide of nationalism and the perceived injustice of the trials created a powerful wave of resentment within the Royal Indian Navy, culminating in the mutiny of 1946  and signalled a breakdown in the loyalty of Indian armed forces ($\checkmark$ knowledge). While these efforts did not lead directly to independence, they inspired sections of the youth and maintained pressure on the British government. In addition, the threat of violence from the growing conflict between Hindus and Muslims linked to the demand for a separate Muslim homeland, accelerated British withdrawal, as colonial authorities feared that prolonged occupation would lead to a civil war that they would need to deal with ($\checkmark$ analysis). 

In Ireland, violence was not only present but essential in achieving independence, as political concessions from Britain were only made under pressure from armed insurrection and domestic instability ($\checkmark$ focus). The Indian Independence movement, however, was based mainly on non-violent resistance under the leadership of Gandhi, as the Indian National Congress spearheaded mass civil disobedience movements that directly challenged British rule ($\checkmark$ focus). Gandhi's Satyagraha (truth-force) philosophy emphasized moral persuasion and non-violent protest. Campaigns such as the Non-Cooperation Movement (1920-22), the Salt March (1930), and the Quit India Movement (1942) mobilized millions of Indians across social, religious, and caste lines ($\checkmark$ knowledge). These movements severely disrupted British administration and helped delegitimize colonial rule domestically and internationally. While the movements demonstrated the potential of unified civil resistance, they also revealed the fragility of colonial authority when denied mass cooperation ($\checkmark$ analysis).

Non-violent political strategy was hence the dominant and most effective element in the Indian independence movement, as the decades-long nationalist movement had already laid the groundwork for self-rule. The sustained, moral force of mass non-violence, combined with Britain's weakened post-WWII position, proved decisive. In Ireland, the failure of peaceful negotiation and the success of guerrilla warfare under Collins suggest that violence was essential to forcing British withdrawal. Political concessions in Ireland only followed military pressure and sustained unrest. Therefore, in the Irish case, violent methods were essential, while in the Indian case, they were influential but not indispensable ($\checkmark$ analysis). Independence was ultimately achieved in both nations, but by different routes shaped by local conditions and strategic choices ($\checkmark$ conclusion).
