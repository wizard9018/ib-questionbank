# HI0999

With reference to its origin, purpose, and content, analyze the value and limitations of Source A for a historian studying the anti-apartheid resistance.

[4 marks]

$\\$

| | | |

| :-- | :-- | :-- |

|**Source A** $\\$ $\hspace{5em}$ | $\hspace{3em}$ | Extract from the book $\textit{\textquotedblleft}$*The Final Prize"* by Norman Levy and published in 2013. Levy opposed Bantu Education and promoted alternative learning for black students during apartheid. He was a member of the Congress of the People, and also contributed to founding the Congress of Democrats, a white anti-apartheid organization, also integrated by Rusty Bernstein. 

:box[

(…) the idea of a Congress of the People (COP) at which the delegates would adopt a Freedom Charter which would assert the demand for civil rights for all South Africans, seemed to be the appropriate next step. (…) The leadership believed that this was an objective (...) about which $\text{\textquotedblleft}$all levels of the congress membership were passionate”. That this assessment was accurate was to some extent manifested in (…) the language of the campaign. This was essentially the genius of Rusty Bernstein who (…) reached out to the masses with a quality of prose that matched the expectations of the occasion. Accordingly, the Freedom Charter helped to $\text{\textquotedblleft}$mobilize the people up and down the land and awaken an echo \[for freedom\] in their hearts”. (…)

\

\

This excitement was captured in $\text{\textquotedblleft}$The Call to the Congress of the People”, a passionate document in the format of a leaflet, drafted (…) by Rusty Bernstein. (…) Rusty was obviously touched by the emotions that the idea of the COP had aroused. He was not by nature overtly passionate (…). $\text{\textquotedblleft}$The Call” (…) was an exception, an indication of the extent to which Bernstein was personally inspired by the egalitarian idealism of the action. Its stirring message reached out $\text{\textquotedblleft}$to brothers without land and children without schooling”; to the farmers in the reserves; to the miners $\text{\textquotedblleft}$in the dark shafts and cold compounds, far from their families”; and to the farm workers and workers in the factories. It called on them to speak of long hours, housing and pass laws, of taxes and of cattle-culling and of famine, ending with the repetitive refrain:

$\text{\textquotedblleft}$Let us speak together, all of us together - African and European, Indian and Coloured. Voter and voteless. Privileged and rightless. The happy and the homeless. Let us speak of freedom.”

]

[Source: Levy\, N. (2015, June 18). *The Final Prize by Norman Levy. South African History Online.* Retrieved August 2, 2025, from https://sahistory.org.za/article/freedomcharter-norman-levy. CC.]

## 评分标准 / Mark scheme

$\underline{\textrm{Explanation}}$:

\

A combination of the ideas below that meets the following guidelines:

 - Both values and limitations must be addressed. If only one is addressed, award a maximum of **two** marks.

 - Origin, purpose and content must all be referenced at some point in the response (either as a value, limitation or both) to receive the full **four** marks.

For **value**, any of the following: 

 - Norman Levy was a member of the Congress of the People, and also contributed to founding the Congress of Democrats, a white anti-apartheid organization. 

 - He writes to highlight Rusty Bernstein's role in drafting the Freedom Charter, an essential document in the anti-apartheid resistance movement. 

 - Levy gives testimony of the events leading up to and during the Congress of the People, as part of the resistance to apartheid. 

For **limitations**, any of the following:

 - Source A represents only Norman Levy's perspective in the movement. 

 - The fact that Levy and Bernstein are both part of the same white anti-apartheid organization may influence his attempt to convince the reader regarding Bernstein's leading role.

 - Source A only highlights Bernstein's role, but it does not mention other anti-apartheid movement leaders who were also important.

\

[4 marks]

$\underline{\textrm{Sample Answer}}$:

\

Norman Levy, a member of the Congress of the People and the Congress of Democrats, writes as a witness to the events he describes. His account constitutes a firsthand source, giving historians direct access to the facts related to these crucial events in the anti-apartheid resistance. ($\checkmark\, \text{ VO}$) The purpose of Levy's account is to highlight Rusty Bernstein's contribution to the resistance to apartheid. This allows historians to delve into the roles and motivations of a key figure in the movement. ($\checkmark\, \text{ VP}$) The source's content describes the events leading up to and during the Congress. For historians, this source provides valuable details and a narrative that reflects the atmosphere, decisions, and significance of these critical events, which marked a turning point in the resistance movement against apartheid. ($\checkmark\, \text{ VC}$)

On the other hand, as for the source's origin, since this is an account from the perspective of Norman Levy, who participated in the anti-apartheid movement, it may be subject to personal bias, selective memory, or a particular retrospective interpretation that somehow modifies the facts. ($\checkmark\, \text{ LO}$) Levy's focus on highlighting Rusty Bernstein's role may lead him to overlook other contributions or different leaders' perspectives in the resistance movement. ($\checkmark\, \text{ LP}$) Furthermore, the source content, taken from a chapter in Levy's book, presents only part of the story and may omit important details or broader context surrounding Congress and the whole anti-apartheid resistance. ($\checkmark\, \text{ LC}$)
